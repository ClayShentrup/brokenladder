## 1. cold open: nine witnesses

O1 Henry County, Georgia. Late twenty twenty-five. The county's ethics board is holding a hearing into the transportation director, and it has subpoenaed nine county employees as witnesses.

O2 Midway through, the board's administrator is called out of the room. She comes back and reads a message into the record. The request came from the chair of the county commission, on the county attorney's advice: the ethics board has no authority to subpoena county employees.

O3 And the nine witnesses stand up and walk out.

O4 In most places, that's where the story ends. The board gets the message, and the investigation quietly dies.

O5 This board didn't fold. It voted to open an investigation into the commission chair herself.

O6 To understand why it could do that, and what happened next, you need to know one strange thing about how that board was chosen. Most of its members weren't elected by voters, or appointed by politicians. They were picked by a jury.

[title card: the case against voting]

## 2. two jobs

A1 Any system for choosing leaders has two jobs.

A2 The first is alignment. The people making the choice should want what the public wants. If the deciders have their own agenda, it doesn't matter how smart they are.

A3 The second is competence. The deciders have to actually know what they're choosing between. Good intentions plus bad information still gets you a bad result.

A4 A king fails the first test. A committee of experts fails it too, in a quieter way, because experts are a group with interests of their own.

A5 Mass elections were supposed to pass both. Everyone gets a say, so the result is aligned. And voters can learn about the candidates, so the result is informed.

A6 Let's check both of those claims.

---

## 3. the competence problem

R1 Start with a fact nobody disputes. In a big election, your vote almost certainly won't change the result.

R2 In a presidential election, the chance that your one ballot decides the outcome is, on average, about one in sixty million. Local races have better odds, but your vote still almost never decides anything.

R3 So think about what that does to homework. Reading up on every candidate for county commission takes hours. And the payoff, measured in the chance that your research changes anything, is close to zero.

R4 Economists have a name for this. Rational ignorance. When the cost of learning is real, and the benefit is almost nothing, staying uninformed is the sensible choice.

R5 No insult to voters is intended here. This is arithmetic. Most people know a candidate's party, a slogan, and whatever an ad told them. For a down-ballot race, many don't know that much.

R6 And into that vacuum flows money. Before a candidate can persuade you of anything, you have to have heard their name. Name recognition is the first thing campaign money buys, and it's sold by the thirty-second spot.

R7 Now add what an information war looks like. Echo chambers. Foreign influence campaigns. U.S. intelligence officials have named Russia, China and Iran as actively targeting American elections, and generative AI makes a convincing fake cheaper every year.

R8 A mass election asks millions of people, each with almost no reason to study, to sort truth from fiction in that environment. Then it adds up their guesses.

---

## 4. the alignment problem

T1 Now the other job. Is the electorate even a fair sample of the public?

T2 It isn't. The people who vote are, on average, older, whiter and wealthier than the people who don't. The Census Bureau measures this gap every election, and it's large.

T3 Elections are opt-in. And whenever a survey lets people choose whether to take part, the results skew toward whoever is most motivated to show up.

T4 A pollster who did that would be fired. We call it democracy.

T5 So mass elections are weakest on exactly the two jobs they were supposed to do. Voters have little reason to get informed. And the ones who show up aren't a fair sample.

---

## 5. a different machine

J1 Here's the alternative. It's called election by jury.

J2 Instead of asking everyone to vote, you draw a random sample of citizens. Same way you'd draw a trial jury. Service is required, and it's paid.

J3 The jurors spend a week or two doing what voters never have time for. They hear every candidate make their case. They watch the candidates question each other. They submit their own questions. They talk it over.

J4 Then they vote, by secret ballot. And their choice is binding. Whoever the jury picks takes office.

J5 Look at what that does to both jobs.

J6 Alignment first. A random sample doesn't skew toward the motivated, the old, or the well-off, because nobody opts in. Statisticians call this a representative sample. Every serious poll is trying to approximate one. A jury can actually be one.

J7 Now competence. A juror's vote isn't one in a million. It's one in a few dozen. Suddenly the homework is worth doing. And the jury isn't doing it on their lunch break. They have protected time, set aside for nothing else.

J8 And money mostly stops mattering. Every candidate gets the same time in front of the same jurors. You can't buy name recognition from people who are going to hear from you directly either way.

## 6. watching does most of the work

W1 You might think the magic here is in the deliberation, jurors reasoning together around a table. That helps. But there's evidence that simply watching a fair contest does most of the work.

W2 The debate series Intelligence Squared polls its audience before and after each debate. The audience doesn't discuss anything. They just listen to both sides.

W3 In one twenty-oh-nine debate on religion, the audience started at six hundred seventy-eight for the motion and eleven hundred two against. After ninety minutes of argument, it was two hundred sixty-eight for and eighteen hundred seventy-six against.

W4 That's one debate, so don't lean on it too hard. But big swings after hearing both sides are common across the series.

W5 Now notice the format. Each side is as biased as it likes. Nobody pretends to be neutral. Neutrality comes from the contest itself, the same way it does in a courtroom. Nobody expects the prosecutor to be fair. We expect the trial to be.

W6 Call it omni-biased. Every candidate pushes their own case and pokes holes in everyone else's, and the jurors get to watch.

---

## 7. you can recognize a better surgeon

K1 Here's the objection everyone has. Random people? You'd let a random sample of your neighbors pick the mayor?

K2 But think about what the jury is being asked to do. Nobody's asking them to run the city. They're judging who'd run it best.

K3 Those are different skills. You can't perform surgery, but you can usually tell which of two surgeons explains your options clearly, answers straight, and stays consistent under questioning. Research on how laypeople judge experts finds exactly this. People read competence, honesty and good faith from how someone handles hard questions, even when they couldn't answer those questions themselves.

K4 And a jury pools that judgment across dozens of people with different lives and different blind spots.

K5 What about the obvious fix, then? Only let qualified people choose?

K6 Every filter is a lever. Whoever writes the test decides who passes. America tried this. Literacy tests were sold as a way to make sure voters were competent, and in practice they let local officials decide who got to vote at all.

K7 Randomness is the one selection method nobody can tilt. That's the whole point of it.

---

## 8. how many jurors?

N1 How big does the jury need to be? Smaller than you'd think.

N2 Say a town is split between two candidates, and you draw twenty-three jurors. Sixteen of them prefer candidate A. How sure can you be that A would win if the whole town voted?

N3 About ninety-seven percent sure. And that number barely moves whether the town has a thousand people or a hundred million.

N4 That's the counterintuitive part. Past a point, the size of the population barely matters. What matters is the size of the sample, and whether it's truly random.

N5 With more than two candidates, the voting method starts to matter a lot. Choose-one voting splits the vote between similar candidates. A jury can use score voting instead: rate every candidate, say from zero to five, highest average wins. Simulations suggest a few dozen jurors scoring candidates capture most of the satisfaction you'd get from a perfect election.

N6 And there's a reason to go bigger than statistics alone requires. Bribery.

N7 Say every attempt to bribe or threaten a juror has a one-in-twenty chance of being reported. Try it on fourteen jurors, and the odds you get caught pass fifty percent. Thirty jurors, nearly eighty percent.

N8 And with a secret ballot, a briber can't even check whether the bribe worked.

N9 One simple rule of thumb: the cube root of the electorate. A town of eight thousand gets twenty jurors. A city of a hundred twenty-five thousand gets fifty. A state of eight million gets two hundred. A country of two hundred forty million, about six hundred twenty.

## 9. this isn't new

H1 If this sounds radical, it's one of the oldest ideas in democracy.

H2 In classical Athens, most public offices were filled by lot. Aristotle wrote that choosing officials by lot was considered democratic, and choosing them by election, oligarchic.

H3 Notice what Aristotle was worried about. Not the act of choosing. A popularity contest, decided by a crowd with no time to look closely, favors the rich, the famous and the well-connected.

H4 Venice chose its head of state, the Doge, with a procedure that alternated between drawing lots and electing. Thirty names drawn from the Great Council, cut to nine by lot, who elected forty, cut to twelve by lot, and so on through ten rounds. The method, adopted in twelve sixty-eight, lasted until the republic fell in seventeen ninety-seven.

H5 The point of all that lottery was simple. If you don't know who'll be choosing, you can't bribe them in advance, and you can't build a faction around them.

H6 In nineteen-oh-three, H. G. Wells asked whether polling was really essential to democracy, and proposed the jury method instead. He described mass politics as government by "hustings' bawling, newspaper clamour, and ward organization."

## 10. it's already happening

G1 And it isn't just history. In Georgia, it's routine.

G2 Every county in Georgia seats grand juries, sixteen to twenty-three citizens drawn at random. Besides deciding indictments, Georgia grand juries choose people for public office.

G2b In all one hundred fifty-nine counties, the grand jury picks the board of equalization, the people who decide your property tax appeal.

G2c In dozens of counties, it nominates the voter registrars. In Lowndes County, it has appointed the entire board of elections since nineteen seventy-nine.

G3 It picks six of the seven directors of the Cherokee County water and sewer authority, and four of seven on the county's airport authority. In Towns County, it appoints the whole water authority.

G3b And in Crisp County, the grand jury picks a majority of the power commission, which runs the only county-owned electric utility in Georgia, including its own hydroelectric dam. It has run that dam for nearly a hundred years.

G3c It used to go further. For more than a century, Georgia counties could have their school boards appointed by the grand jury. By the late nineteen-eighties, twenty-eight of the state's school districts still did. A constitutional amendment in nineteen ninety-two ended the option.

G3c2 Georgia almost brought it back. In twenty seventeen, the state Senate voted forty to twelve for an amendment letting districts choose grand-jury-appointed school boards again. A House committee killed it, seventeen to one. The part lawmakers objected to was the grand jury.

G3d One caveat, because critics will raise it. For much of that history, county jury commissioners hand-picked who went on the jury list. In nineteen seventy, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled against the way one Georgia county ran this system. Taliaferro County was majority Black. Its school board was all white, picked by a mostly white grand jury, drawn from a list made by white jury commissioners.

G3e That was a jury that wasn't random. Since twenty twelve, Georgia has drawn its juries from a statewide list of registered voters and licensed drivers. The lesson of Taliaferro County is the case for real randomness.

G4 A random jury choosing officials, as a normal part of county government, for generations.

G5 And grand juries serve longer than you'd guess. In some Georgia counties, a grand juror serves for two or three months, meeting once or twice a week. So the objection that people won't put up with jury service doesn't hold up. They already do.

G6 That brings us back to Henry County.

## 11. the Henry County test

X1 In twenty twenty-one, State Senator Emanuel Jones sponsored a bill creating the Henry County Board of Ethics. Seven members. Five of them appointed by the grand jury, one for each commission district.

X2 That design choice is the whole story. Most ethics boards are appointed by the people they're supposed to watch. A grand jury appointee owes their seat to no one.

X3 Then came the walkout. Nine subpoenaed witnesses leave after the commission chair's office says the board can't compel them.

X4 The board votes to investigate the commission chair, Carlotta Harrell. Her public response: "They can investigate all they want to, but I will not be answering anything that they put out."

X5 Next, the resources dry up. At a January twenty twenty-six meeting, the board laid it out on the record. No budget for a three-hundred-ten-dollar-a-week job posting, so they used a free trial. Barred from the county's broadcast-equipped meeting room. Denied the cameras to stream their meetings.

X6 The board's chair, Felicia Harrell, no relation, put it this way: "They want the Board of Ethics to be a figurehead."

X7 Critics called the board a political hit squad. At that same meeting, it had a nepotism complaint against the county manager, a clean shot if it wanted one. It looked at the evidence. Her husband had been hired thirteen years before she became manager. The board dismissed the case, unanimously.

X8 That's what independence looks like. Follow the evidence wherever it goes, even when it clears someone you could have used.

## 12. the rewrite

V1 Then the state legislature stepped in.

V2 A bill called S.B. three fifty-nine had passed the Georgia Senate unanimously in twenty twenty-five, as an update to the county's ethics code. In early twenty twenty-six, the House replaced it with a substitute.

V3 The substitute shrinks the board to five members. Each one is appointed by a county elected official: the clerk of court, the probate judge, the sheriff, the tax commissioner, and the solicitor-general.

V4 The words "grand jury" don't appear anywhere in it. The randomly chosen appointments aren't repealed by name. They're just gone, overwritten. Nothing in the bill's title tells you.

V5 And the substitute adds a new sentence: the board "shall not accept or hear complaints" against county employees. The transportation director who started all this is a county employee.

V6 It passed the House a hundred sixty-one to three, and the Senate forty-four to six.

V7 One more detail. The new law requires a majority vote of the commission to fire the county manager. While the bill sat on the governor's desk, the commission chair fired the county manager on her own. She told the Atlanta Journal-Constitution that under the new legislation, that decision would require a majority vote.

V8 Governor Kemp signed S.B. three fifty-nine on May twelfth, twenty twenty-six. The grand-jury board is replaced on January first, twenty twenty-seven.

V9 You can read this as a defeat. Look at it the other way. Five board members, chosen by randomly selected citizens, held their ground against the most powerful official in the county, and the only way to stop them was an act of the state legislature.

V10 Independent oversight that nobody bothers to dismantle probably wasn't doing much. This one was worth a law.

## 13. what we'd change

Z1 So here's the proposal. Take the method Georgia already uses for ethics boards and water authorities, and use it for the offices we currently fill with mass elections.

Z2 Draw a jury. Pay them. Give them a week or two. Every candidate gets equal time, can question their rivals, and answers the jury's questions. The jurors score every candidate by secret ballot. The winner takes office.

Z3 No fundraising for name recognition, because the jury hears from everyone directly. No turnout skew, because nobody opts in. No rational ignorance, because each juror's vote really counts, and they have the time to use it well.

Z4 And it holds up better than a mass election against the age of cheap persuasion. It's a lot harder to flood a sequestered room of fifty people with deepfakes than to flood a feed with them.

Z5 None of this means voters are stupid. It means a mass election asks them to do an expert's job, in their spare time, for no reward, in the middle of an information war.

Z6 A jury asks a fair sample of them to do it properly.

---

## 14. close

C1 We trust twelve random citizens to decide whether someone goes to prison for life. We trust a grand jury of up to twenty-three to decide whether a sitting official gets indicted.

C2 In Henry County, five people chosen by grand juries took on the most powerful official in the county, and held out until the state rewrote the law.

C3 Ordinary people can be trusted to choose their leaders.

C4 We just keep asking them to do it without the time, the information, or a reason to pay attention.
