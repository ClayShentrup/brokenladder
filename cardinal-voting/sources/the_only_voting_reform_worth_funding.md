# The Only Voting Reform Worth Funding

Sixteen years ago I co-wrote an essay arguing that the way we count votes, not American temperament or geography or the conspiracies of the powerful, is what keeps us locked in a two-party system. I still believe almost every word of it. What I’ve changed my mind about is the conclusion. For most of the twenty years I’ve spent on this, I was certain the goal was to find the best voting method and convince the world to adopt it. That framing wastes most of the effort poured into this field, including much of my own.

Almost everyone here is trying to answer “which method is best?”. That’s a real question, and I’ll give you my answer. But it’s the wrong question to organize a movement around, because what we actually care about is human welfare, and welfare isn’t a function of which method is best. It’s a function of which method gets adopted, times how much better off people are when it does.

**impact = (benefit per voter) × (scale)**

Benefit per voter is quality: how much better outcomes get. Scale is reach: how many people live under it. The field pours its genius into the first term and treats the second as beneath the theorists, work for organizers and ballot consultants. This is backwards. Scale holds nearly all the variance, and it decides whether any theory touches a human life. A method two percent better that never passes helps no one. A less elegant method that spreads to a hundred jurisdictions is among the larger humanitarian wins available this century. The arithmetic isn’t close, and it doesn’t care how beautiful your method is.

Scale isn’t a reform’s raw reach. It’s the reach your effort is responsible for, which depends on whether your contribution is pivotal, in exactly the sense a single voter is pivotal. Consider what one vote is worth. Almost always nothing; the election comes out the same without it, and it matters only in the knife’s-edge case where it breaks a tie. The expected value of casting it is the stakes times the probability yours is decisive. Funding a reform works the same way. Most effort is inert: the reform either had the momentum to pass without you or never had a chance, and either way you changed nothing. Your effort earns its value only where it’s the marginal input that tips the reform from not-happening to happening, where you’re the pivotal voter, not in an election, but in the adoption of the method itself.

So the question is never “is this reform good”. It’s “is this the reform where my weight is decisive”, which has the two conditions that make a vote decisive. It has to be neglected enough that it isn’t already winning without you, the way your vote is worthless in a landslide. And tractable enough that it can win with you, the way your vote is worthless in a race that’s already lost. Call the product pivotality. It’s high only where both hold, a reform starved of attention and capable of winning, and it collapses if either fails, because a crowded fight doesn’t need you and an unwinnable one can’t use you.

On both terms, quality and pivotality, the answer is approval voting: the plurality ballot with one rule removed, where instead of marking a single name you mark every candidate you approve of, and the most-approved wins. Mathematically it’s just score voting flattened to its simplest case, a score ballot where the only allowed ratings are zero and one, so everything score voting does, approval does too, in lower resolution. The case for it is stronger than most of approval’s own advocates make it, and stranger, because it requires conceding that approval isn’t the most beautiful method, then showing why that doesn’t matter.

### The incumbent is the worst option, and everyone half-knows it

Start with ranked choice voting, the reform with the money, the momentum, and the brand. In its standard single-winner form it’s called instant runoff voting, or IRV: you rank the candidates, the one with the fewest first-place votes is eliminated, those ballots transfer to each voter’s next choice, and it repeats until someone holds a majority. It’s also the worst of the serious options.

Its signature failure is the center squeeze: it eliminates the candidate a majority prefers, in an early round, before the final tally sees him. Not a hypothetical. It happened in Burlington, Vermont, in 2009, a race I’ve cited for sixteen years because it’s so clean. Montroll beat every opponent head to head, the textbook definition of the candidate a majority wanted, the [Condorcet winner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_winner_criterion) (condor-SAY). IRV eliminated him early anyway and handed the office to someone the electorate would have rejected in a straight matchup. Burlington repealed IRV the next year.

The mechanism is worth naming, because IRV’s defenders tout it as a virtue. IRV counts only one of your preferences at a time, your highest choice still standing. While your favorite survives, your support for a compromise candidate is invisible and does nothing to keep him from being eliminated. This is later-no-harm: ranking a second choice can never hurt your first. Adherents sell it as a feature, and fault approval and score for failing it, since under those methods, approving your second choice can help him beat your first. But it’s one fact seen from two sides. “Your later preferences can’t hurt your favorite” is the same thing as “your later preferences can’t save the broadly acceptable candidate from being squeezed out”. The property IRV brags about is the property that strangles the middle, and the one cardinal methods are faulted for is exactly what lets them protect it.

What makes this worth dwelling on is the contortion it forces on the reform’s defenders. The standing objection to Condorcet methods, written into the DNA of the ranked-choice movement from the start by FairVote, the group that has driven the campaign in America, is that they might elect a candidate who is “no one’s first choice”, a compromise that tops few ballots. Set aside that on their own majoritarian terms, the head-to-head winner is the one who should win. If we’re trading hypotheticals about perverse outcomes, IRV’s aren’t hypothetical. It eliminates a candidate who beats both rivals head to head and installs one of those rivals instead, in elections we can name. A method that throws out the majority’s preferred candidate has no standing to complain that a rival elects a compromise too gracefully.

And here’s something that actually happened on paper. We think the odds of the Condorcet winner having few first-place votes are tiny, but if you want absurdly improbable worst cases, IRV supplies one: it can elect a candidate while a huge majority prefers someone else who also got nearly twice as many first-place votes. [A four-candidate example](https://rangevoting.net/CoreSuppPocket) works it out in full.

But this almost never happens. Across the thousands of ranked elections held in the United States, IRV has elected the Condorcet winner in all but two: Burlington and the 2022 Alaska special, where Begich was squeezed out between Palin and Peltola. Stop there and IRV looks unimpeachable, and the case I’m about to make looks irrelevant.

That near-perfect record is real. It’s just a good number drawn from easy conditions, and it won’t survive contact with the races where the method is actually tested.

Center squeeze needs three or more competitive candidates with one in a broadly acceptable middle, drawing wide second-choice support but few first-place votes. That configuration is rare in American IRV elections, because most of them are nonpartisan municipal races, which often have no clean ideological middle. They turn on incumbency, competence, personality, local issues, and collapse to a front-runner. No center candidate, no center to squeeze, and IRV picks the obvious winner exactly as plurality would. Most of those thousands of elections were never a test.

Which is why the two failures are the two you’d predict. Burlington and Alaska were the rare cases with real three-way competition and a genuine middle, one a partisan municipal race, the other a partisan federal one. The moment the squeeze geometry appeared, IRV failed, both times, as the mechanism says it must. The failures aren’t scattered randomly. They’re concentrated exactly where the theory flags danger, which is what a correct theory looks like.

This is why the near-spotless record can’t be read as reassurance about the future. IRV has barely reached the big leagues, partisan races at the county and state level, where the electorate is genuinely divided and a real centrist can draw support from both sides. Just two states use it that way, Maine and Alaska, and Alaska’s first competitive three-way under it produced exactly the squeeze the mechanism predicts. That’s not a reassuring rate; that’s one of a tiny number of real tests already failed. The paucity of failures so far is not evidence IRV is safe. It’s evidence IRV hasn’t yet been put where it would fail.

Two distinct failures are at work here, and it’s worth keeping them apart. The center squeeze is mechanical: sequential elimination drops the broadly acceptable candidate before the final round because he holds too few first-place votes, and this happens even if every voter is perfectly honest. It needs no strategy at all, which is why it surfaces the instant a genuine three-way appears. The second failure is strategic and separate. Because IRV still punishes a vote for someone the polls call unviable, the same favorite betrayal that plagues plurality, voters keep backing whoever money and party affiliation mark as electable. So IRV reproduces plurality’s viability dynamics and stays pinned to two dominant candidates. The familiar complaint that it never breaks the two-party hold is really a symptom of that second failure: the method is still running on strategic exaggeration, with all the distortions that brings, and the persistent duopoly is just the most visible one.

The IRV movement was never built on a claim that IRV produces good outcomes. It runs on perceived pragmatism, there’s precedent in Australia, Canada, Ireland, some American cities, so it feels viable, and on a longer game. IRV is the single-winner form of single transferable vote, and STV is the real destination for much of the movement, proportional representation reached by getting voters used to ranking. I wrote this in 2010 and it’s still the most honest account of why the energy is where it is. It’s a coherent political strategy. It just isn’t an argument that the method counts votes well, and where the method has been stressed, the evidence is that it doesn’t.

So set the incumbent aside. The interesting opponent is the sophisticated one.

### What approval voting is, and where it’s been tried

I described approval already, the plurality ballot with the one-name restriction lifted, but it’s worth seeing it isn’t hypothetical. Fargo became the first US city to adopt it, in 2018, by a 64% landslide. St. Louis followed in 2020, 68% in favor, using approval voting in a nonpartisan primary that sends its top two finishers to a runoff. Both cities ran real elections under it and kept running them.

And then the backlash, which tells you more than the adoptions do. In 2024, Missouri amended its constitution to ban ranked choice and approval voting outright, though it carved out an exception that lets St. Louis keep its system. In 2025, North Dakota went further and banned both statewide with no exception, ending Fargo’s experiment. The North Dakota sponsor was unusually candid about why: he objected that approval voting elects “vanilla”, middle-of-the-road candidates rather than the more “principled”, more ideologically committed ones he preferred. That is the case against approval voting stated honestly, by an opponent. It elects broadly acceptable people, and the factions that thrive on the current system would rather it didn’t.

### The quality term, where the rankings indict the ranked methods

The serious case against approval comes from the Condorcet camp, now offering ranked pairwise methods as IRV’s intelligent successor. Their claim is that their methods produce better outcomes than approval, which is the crude tool you settle for. It’s the first thing I believed when I started on this in 2006, for a week or two, until Warren Smith showed me his Bayesian regret simulations, the same measure as voter satisfaction efficiency under a different name. The numbers settled it for me then, and they settle it now.

To compare methods on outcomes rather than intuitions, you simulate: generate an electorate with known preferences, run each method, and measure how close the winner comes to the candidate voters actually preferred. The standard metric is voter satisfaction efficiency, where 100% is the best possible outcome and 0% is no better than picking at random. I ran it over a spatial model of a polarized two-camp electorate, the terrain most favorable to the Condorcet camp, since voters and candidates sit on a genuine ideological axis where pairwise comparisons mean the most. The methods: the best ranked ones, Ranked Pairs and Schulze, against all three cardinal methods, score, approval, and STAR (score, then an automatic runoff between the top two). The voters misjudge viability the way real voters do, and strategize at the rates real voters strategize.

Read left to right. In the realistic column about ninety percent of voters strategize, one camp slightly more than the other (ninety-five to eighty-five), the mild asymmetry you’d expect between a more and less coordinated side. That ninety percent isn’t invented: it’s roughly the rate at which plurality voters already abandon a favorite for a viable compromise, and what Australia’s century of compulsory ranked elections shows, where most voters just follow the party’s how-to-vote card. The two right columns progressively cut the strategy among the ranked methods while holding the cardinal methods at that level, handing the ranked camp the honest, docile electorate they imagine. I built those columns to be unfair in their favor.

One assumption sits under all three columns, and I want it visible: I model voters as reading viability correctly about three-quarters of the time. That’s generous to the ranked methods. The worse voters perceive who can win, the more often a ranked ballot buries a true winner mistaken for a no-hoper, so more accurate perception flatters Condorcet. Three-quarters is a defensible midpoint and the conservative choice against my own thesis. Push perception toward the perfection the standard simulations quietly assume and the ranked methods improve, but only by assuming away the misperception real elections are full of.

It doesn’t save them. Under realistic conditions score tops the table, and approval and STAR sit even with the best Condorcet methods rather than below them. Rig it toward Condorcet, hand them a ninety-percent-honest electorate, and score still wins every column while approval stays level with Ranked Pairs. The ranked methods climb only when you assume their voters won’t strategize, which is exactly what real voters do. That climb isn’t a defense. It’s a confession: the Condorcet advantage is an artifact of pretending voters are more honest than they are.

Notice approval lands only a hair behind score, which is the result that should reassure anyone worried the cruder ballot throws away too much. Approval is score with every rating rounded to zero or one. A single rounded ballot loses information, but across a large electorate those roundings scatter in both directions and mostly cancel in the sum, so the approval totals come out close to where the fine-grained score totals would have landed. The theory predicts approval should trail score by a little, and the numbers show exactly that little. The expressiveness you give up on each ballot is mostly the expressiveness that was going to wash out anyway.

And notice IRV at the bottom, 57.3% under realistic conditions, far below everything, rising only when handed near-total honesty. This is the competitive electorate from the last section, the one with a real middle, exactly where IRV is supposed to break and exactly where Burlington and Alaska show it breaking. The simulation and those two ballot sets describe the same failure in two languages. (That number is low enough to be worth double-checking, so I validated the IRV tabulation against hand-computed center-squeeze elections before trusting it.)

So the ranked methods buy nothing in outcomes. They optimize pairwise majoritarianism, which feels right because “majority rule” is a sacred phrase, and pay for it in complexity voters can’t parse and failure modes approval doesn’t have.

The Condorcet camp prizes the Condorcet winner, the candidate who beats each rival head to head. Under a reasonable model of strategic voting, approval elects the Condorcet winner anyway. Its worst case, under full tactical voting, is the very thing they want. Approval hands them their prize and skips the ranked ballot. Their own method, meanwhile, carries a liability they rarely volunteer: burial, voters insincerely ranking a strong rival last to knock him out, which is the dominant strategy in exactly the close races that decide things, and can install a winner the electorate didn’t want. So the cardinal methods reach the Condorcet camp’s own goal more reliably than Condorcet methods do, without the failure mode Condorcet introduces.

### Why the strategy-resistance theorems are inert

The case for Condorcet rests on strategy-resistance theorems: proofs that under stated conditions no faction can profitably manipulate the result. STAR has its own version, and the simulations show its runoff really does dampen strategic incentives. The theorems are true. They’re also inert, for the single most important thing I’ve learned in twenty years:

Real voters don’t compute any of it.

Nobody runs a manipulation analysis in the booth. Voters don’t know how the tabulation works and never will. I’ve spent two decades asking people who voted in ranked elections to explain how their votes were counted, and almost none can. I once walked a sharp software engineer, the kind who’d solve any logic puzzle, through a [four-candidate ranked example](https://web.archive.org/web/20190219005105mp_/https://sites.google.com/a/electology.org/www/approval-score-sf#TOC-Intuitive-understanding) and asked who won. He picked the candidate with the best average ranking, reasoning that a weighted average was obviously how it worked. It wasn’t. The winner was someone else. He wasn’t being lazy; the points-average model was his honest picture of the ballot, and he’s smarter than most of the electorate. A 2011 usability study found the same across education levels: asked how ranked votes are counted, voters invent a points system, then trail into “I don’t know”.

What real voters do is intuit, and the intuition is universal, not a habit trained by plurality, which is what I’d once have told you. A voter raised entirely on ranked ballots would do the same, because the intuition is about leverage, not history: push the candidates I care about apart. Favorite to the top, the threat I most want to stop to the bottom, and trust that widening the gap exerts more force. It does, roughly. The voter isn’t wrong that it works. He just has no idea why, and doesn’t need one.

That’s why the theorems are a mirage. They defend against a sophisticated strategist who doesn’t exist, and ignore the blunt exaggeration real electorates run on. Australia has held compulsory ranked elections for a century, and eighty-five to ninety percent of voters just follow the party’s how-to-vote card, ranking as instructed rather than as any equilibrium would prescribe. The strategy real humans use is the crude kind the theorems don’t address, not the clever kind they foreclose. A proof about a voter who has never existed isn’t a feature of a voting method. It’s a decoration.

### **The holistic summary**

This shows up most clearly when you chart voter satisfaction efficiency across the full spectrum of strategic behavior. Here is VSE for several methods as a function of how much of the electorate is gaming its ballots.

Each election draws its own structure: one to three issue dimensions, polarization ranging from unimodal to sharply bimodal, camp splits from 35/65 to 65/35, and three to seven candidates placed either near a camp or anywhere in opinion space. Voters have individual issue salience weights, tastes for candidate valence, idiosyncratic affinities, and differing stakes in the outcome. The two political camps are not equally strategic: one faction’s propensity runs 10% above the nominal level and the other’s 10% below, under every method. Polling is 75% accurate, and each method’s voters read the signal that governs their own race: first-preference share under plurality and IRV, head-to-head margins under pairwise rules, approval share under cardinal ones. Strategic voters use whatever is optimal against the method they face, which means targeted burial against Ranked Pairs and compromise against elimination rules. The shaded band shows what the same head-to-head polls do to Ranked Pairs depending on whether voters use them merely to spot frontrunners (dotted) or to locate the flippable pairwise contest and bury accordingly (solid).

Score voting starts at 97.42 with a sincere electorate and bottoms out at 95.97 with everyone strategizing. Ranked Pairs starts at 95.41 and falls to 86.90. Score voting at its worst is half a point above Condorcet at its best, which means there is no level of strategic behavior at which switching to Condorcet would have helped.

Condorcet’s decline isn’t caused by voters knowing more. It’s caused by voters doing more with what they already know. Head-to-head polls get published regardless of which method is in use, and they tell a ranked-ballot voter two different things. Used casually they identify who is viable, and Ranked Pairs stays in the mid-nineties. Used deliberately they identify which pairwise contest is close enough to flip and which candidate to sink in order to flip it. That’s the shaded band, and it’s eight points wide at full strategy. Same data, same voters, different sophistication.

A cardinal ballot has no equivalent move. A score voter’s only decision is where to put his threshold, so better polling places the threshold better and that is the end of it. There is no burial available, because there is nothing to bury a candidate beneath. That’s a structural difference rather than a numerical one, and unlike any specific number on the chart it doesn’t depend on my having modeled strategy correctly.

The approval comparison is the one worth putting in front of anyone weighing complexity against benefit. Approval voting with seventy percent of its voters behaving strategically scores 95.73. Ranked Pairs with a perfectly sincere electorate scores 95.41. Approval crosses it at forty percent strategy and stays ahead. It wins using the simplest ballot anyone has proposed, against a method that asks voters for a full ordering and asks officials to tabulate every pairwise contest and resolve cycles by a rule almost no voter will ever understand.

Granted, the tri-pair ballot format proposed by Better Choices, in which voters pick their preferred option from each of the three pairs in a top-three selection rather than ordering them 1–2–3, plausibly reduces strategic ranked exaggeration, perhaps substantially. But it does so by making burial harder to express, which concedes that burial is the problem.

### Favorite betrayal, the one criterion that describes a real person

The property that actually matters is the one that matches how people vote when no one’s modeling them. It has an unlovely name, the favorite betrayal criterion, and asks: can you ever be forced to rank a compromise above your true favorite to get a good result?

Under the ranked methods, yes. Under IRV or any Condorcet method, there are real situations where your optimal vote requires burying the candidate you most prefer. My aunt preferred Warren and voted Biden to stop Trump, because it was plain Warren was likelier to lose if she advanced, the dynamic that bites under a multi-round ranked method. The universal “push them apart” intuition, on a ranked ballot, simply is favorite betrayal: the threat sinks to the bottom and drags your favorite down with the compromise you’re hoisting up. The honest voter and the strategic voter are punished for the same instinct.

Cardinal methods don’t have this failure, and the reason is embarrassingly simple. Under score or approval you max your favorite and min your threat, and there’s no ordering to corrupt. Your favorite stays on top by construction. The intuition that wrecks a ranked ballot is harmless on a cardinal one. This isn’t a technical nicety. It’s the difference between a ballot that punishes sincerity and one that doesn’t, and it’s the difference voters feel even when they can’t name it.

STAR doesn’t formally satisfy favorite betrayal; its runoff reintroduces the failure on paper. In practice STAR captures the benefit anyway, because voters treat it like score voting and never reach the strategic depth where the runoff bites. That inverts how we judge methods. A property a method satisfies on the page but voters never exploit is worthless. A property it fails on the page but honors where it matters, in the behavior of actual people, is the one you want. The criterion that counts isn’t on the theorem sheet. It’s the one that matches what voters do.

Which is also the verdict on STAR as a thing to put on a ballot. It works. I like score voting and remain bullish on it long-term. But STAR is a Rube Goldberg machine: score the candidates, then run an automatic runoff between the top two, with a pairwise matrix on hand to explain the result when someone asks. It earns its robustness through machinery, and the machinery buys, in practice, what approval gets free by satisfying favorite betrayal outright. Every tier past two is another gear, another thing the voter can’t parse, another place to fail at the ballot box, for a benefit the simpler method already delivers.

### The pivotality term, where everything but approval is a train wreck

Now the second term, the one the field treats as beneath it, and the one that decides whether any of this matters.

2024 was a massacre for ballot complexity. Ranked choice lost at the ballot in Oregon, Colorado, Idaho, Nevada, and Arizona, was banned in Missouri, and survived repeal in Alaska by a hair. Voters took one look at the complicated ballot and recoiled. It doesn’t matter whether the machinery behind that ballot is instant runoff or the most elegant Condorcet method ever built, because voters don’t react to the tabulation. They react to the thing in front of them, and that’s a ranking grid they don’t trust and never would.

Approval is a different kind of object, and this is the point that reorganizes the whole project. Approval is the only reform that’s a subtraction rather than an addition.

Every other method, ranked, Condorcet, STAR, is a new apparatus, a new ballot and count and set of rules the reformer has to add and then justify in every jurisdiction, forever. Approval adds nothing. It’s the plurality ballot with one rule removed: the rule that voids your ballot if you mark more than one name. The instruction fits in a sentence. Mark every candidate you’d be glad to see win.

This inverts the burden of proof, permanently, in a way no other reform can touch. Today approval is unfamiliar, so it still faces the ordinary “why change?”. But spread it where ordinary voters can hold the two ballots side by side, and the question flips of its own weight. Now the method that owes an explanation is plurality. Why are you throwing my ballot out because I approved of two candidates? There’s no answer, and never was, only habit, an arbitrary restriction that survives because no one has made voters look at it. No ranked method can trigger this flip, because a ranking grid is visibly a new device that argues for itself every time, in every place, forever. Approval alone runs as the repeal of an arbitrary rule, and repealing an arbitrary rule is the easiest thing to sell in politics.

### What twenty years teaches you

Put the terms together. On quality, approval is at worst within a rounding error of the best ranked method, matches them under realistic conditions, and hands the Condorcet camp their own prize as its worst case. On pivotality, approval is the rare reform high on both things that make a contribution decisive. It’s neglected, because the money and talent pour into ranked choice, so a dollar on approval isn’t one the field would have spent anyway. And it’s tractable, the subtraction property, the burden inversion, the one-sentence ballot, while the ranked methods just showed their ceiling by losing five states in one November. High on both is exactly where your weight tips the result, and almost nothing else in this field sits there.

I’ll close on the thing that took me longest to learn, because it isn’t about voting methods at all. It’s about how a person spends a life in a field like this.

The ranked-methods world has more raw intellectual horsepower than anything else in election reform. I’ve spent two decades among these people and I admire them, and I’ve watched many of them talk themselves in circles, refining an elegant quantity voters will never perceive, defending against a strategist who doesn’t exist, while the one variable that decides whether any of it reaches a human being, whether it can win, sits untouched because it’s someone else’s department. It’s the Taleb critique of the academy in miniature: a gorgeous model, confidently wrong about the world it describes. And the people who build the most beautiful machine pay no price when it loses at the ballot. The voters pay it, stuck another decade with plurality, while the reformers admire their proofs.

I’m not interested in being right about which method is most beautiful. I was once, and could afford it then. What I want now is for the thing to actually happen, somewhere, for real people, in a form that spreads, because the cost of getting this wrong isn’t an abstraction. It’s paid out election after election in outcomes that misrepresent what people wanted. Approval improves those outcomes at least as much as anything on offer, and it’s the only reform with a real chance to win big and keep winning. If you’re trying to maximize impact rather than win an argument, the math isn’t close. It never was. It just took me a while to stop optimizing the wrong term.
