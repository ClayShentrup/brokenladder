# Seattle Just Moved Its Real Election to August

#### What ranked-choice voting in the primary does to the city, traced through a simulation of all seven districts.

### The short version

The decisive election moves to August. Everything else follows from that one fact. Seattle’s first ranked-choice primary lands in August 2027, an odd-numbered year, in the lowest-turnout environment American elections produce: about 30% of registered voters, with people 65 and older casting roughly 45% of the ballots and the 18-to-24 cohort casting about 4%. That electorate now does the winnowing. It narrows every field down to the two names November is allowed to choose between, and November, larger and younger and more renter, inherits a menu it had no hand in writing.

How the “bottoms up” version of Ranked Choice Voting works

The second fact takes a model to see, and it is the one that should have changed Seattle’s vote in 2022. Run the system the way elections actually run, with most voters behaving strategically, and ranked-choice in the primary collapses into the plurality system it was sold as replacing, while reliably discarding the candidate the broad electorate would most prefer. The pitch was more voice. The delivery is a summer electorate choosing between two factional finalists, in a count that no longer does anything plurality wouldn’t.

_In every district, the August primary electorate’s ideological median sits to the moderate side of November’s. The shift isn’t assumed; it falls out of who turns out in summer, sorted by age and homeownership. Simulation, 2,000 elections per district._

### What it feels like from inside

For a 27-year-old renter in the Central District, the experience is the recurring sensation of being handed a ballot to ratify a decision already made. She marks November between two candidates, and the one she would have wanted was gone by mid-August, knocked out in an election she may not have registered as the one that counted. Her side has the numbers in the city and never quite has the council, and she cannot locate why. The cause is structural and invisible. She will be handed a dozen wrong explanations, by pundits and by her own organizers: the left is too divided, the agenda overreached, the city moved right, the candidate ran a bad race. The real one is that the filter changed, not the city.

For a 68-year-old homeowner in Wedgwood, the same machinery feels like sense winning. The candidates who survive are reasonable, electable, not extreme. He experiences his structural advantage as moderation, which is the most comfortable way to hold power, because it never feels like power. He votes in August out of forty years of habit, and the council keeps looking like him.

That asymmetry of perception is the engine of everything downstream. The people the August skew favors will never see a skew; they see good judgment prevailing. The people it disfavors feel the weight constantly and cannot name its source. A grievance that cannot find its object curdles, and curdled grievance is the raw material of cynicism and of crank theories about who really runs things.

### The moderate ratchet, stated precisely

This part has to be exact, because a hostile reader reaches for the obvious counterexample first. Seattle elected a progressive mayor in Katie Wilson in 2025, and progressive at-large members in Alexis Mercedes Rinck and Dionne Foster. The city is not drifting rightward, and the simulation does not claim it is. Those were plurality elections under the old rules, in high-salience citywide races where turnout runs highest and the August skew bites least. What follows is a directional tilt that rides on top of whatever mood the city is in, and it bites hardest where salience is lowest: down-ballot district primaries in odd years.

The tilt has two parts. The first is the electorate. The August primary that does the winnowing skews older, wealthier, and more homeowner than November, which on Seattle’s local axis means it skews toward the moderate, Harrell-Chamber pole. Whoever the summer electorate advances out of each camp is the more moderate option that camp can field, because the people choosing are the people most comfortable with moderation.

The second part is the squeeze, and it is where the voting method does its damage. The candidate the broad November electorate would most prefer, the consensus figure who is many voters’ acceptable second choice but few voters’ passionate first, gets eliminated in the August primary and never appears on the fall ballot. Modeled with realistic voters, ranked-choice does this in a bit over a quarter of races citywide, and in up to a third of races in the swing districts.

_With realistic voters (85% strategic), ranked-choice eliminates the candidate November’s electorate would most prefer in 16 to 32 percent of races across every district, lowest in deep-left District 3, where the consensus candidate is often the progressive frontrunner anyway. Approval keeps that candidate on the ballot almost everywhere._

Here is the mechanism, and it is worth being precise because ranked-choice has a genuine advantage that turns out to be the same thing as its central flaw. Ranked-choice’s real edge over plurality is vote-split mitigation. When a minor candidate splits a faction, ranked-choice eliminates them and transfers their votes to the similar candidate still standing, so the faction gets the result it would have gotten anyway, which plurality leaves broken. That is a real improvement, and the simulation gives ranked-choice full credit for it. The trouble is that consolidating each faction onto its strongest candidate is the identical operation to advancing both faction champions and eliminating the consensus candidate sitting between them. The thing the method does well is the thing that squeezes the center. You can watch the cost directly under sincere voting, where ranked-choice squeezes the consensus candidate out 36% of the time against plurality’s 25%: plurality’s vote-splitting, the flaw ranked-choice fixes, is the very thing that occasionally spares the centrist ranked-choice cleanly removes.

_One D6 race with strategic voters. Ranked-choice advances a far-left progressive and a moderate, and the moderate wins November even though the district leans left. The center-left candidate the November electorate would most prefer (green ring) is eliminated in the first round. Approval would have advanced that consensus candidate alongside the moderate, giving November a real contest._

Then comes the part that should have ended the 2022 argument on its own. When voters behave strategically, ranked-choice loses most of what sets it apart from plurality. A strategic voter compromises onto a frontrunner, and once they rank a frontrunner first, the ranked ballot’s first preference is just the plurality vote; the frontrunners hold the first-round lead, they are never eliminated, and the elimination machinery returns the plurality pair. Across the simulated races, strategic ranked-choice squeezes the consensus candidate out 28 percent of the time and strategic plurality 30, with welfare losses a hair apart. The ranked ballot keeps a few points of distinctiveness, no more, in exactly the contested, strategic conditions where it was supposed to matter most.

Stack the two parts and the result is a quiet ratchet toward the Harrell-Chamber-homeowner pole of the Democratic Party. It runs strongest in the swing districts and citywide, and nearly disappears in deep-left District 3, where the consensus candidate is usually the progressive anyway. The city keeps voting its heart in the races that draw a crowd and governing from a council assembled in the races that don’t, and the gap between those two things hardens into a standing feature of Seattle politics.

### Why housing is the issue this reshapes most

Bottom-up ranked-choice is a homeowner amplifier, and the homeowner is the housing incumbent. The August electorate skews toward the people who already own the scarce asset, who read upzoning as a threat to neighborhood character and resale value, and who return a summer ballot with the reliability of a sprinkler timer. Hand that electorate the winnowing power and you have wired a land-use veto into the primary.

Pro-housing candidates still win some seats. The deeper effect is that the decisive electorate for the council is the one with the most to lose from abundance, so the body bends toward scarcity relative to the city’s renter majority. The pro-housing coalition then does what blocked interests do. It routes around: to Olympia for preemption, the way the state already forced middle housing over local objection, and to the ballot box, where citywide measures draw a broader and younger electorate. Seattle ends up taking its density from the legislature and its tenant protections from initiatives, while the body nominally in charge of land use answers to the voters least inclined to change it.

Every other contested issue inherits the same filter. Public-safety framing that polls with older homeowners beats police-accountability framing in the deciding round, so the 2020 reckoning arrives at the 2030 council pre-muted. Big-business taxes face a council assembled by the bloc closest to the Chamber, which is part of why progressive revenue keeps arriving by initiative. On homelessness, enforcement out-competes housing-first in August. On transit and street space, the car-oriented summer electorate is the one a candidate cannot afford to cross, which is brutal for bike lanes and road diets. The pattern holds across every question framed as incumbent comfort against broad or future interest: the deciding electorate is the one most wedded to the status quo.

### How the campaigns actually change

Money and field operations migrate to summer. Ballots drop in mid-July, the contest is over for most races by the second week of August, and the campaign a normal person notices in October is staged over a decision already taken. Consultants retarget around the voter who shows up to every election including odd-year primaries: older, homeowning, already flagged in the file. Door programs concentrate on single-family blocks. Renters and students fall out of the targeting by arithmetic, and the neglect deepens the turnout gap that made them skippable, compounding each cycle.

Messaging tacks to the August median even for the left, because the summer tournament goes to the candidate that electorate can stomach. Endorsement bodies turn into kingmakers, because the ranked ballot rewards a coordinated slate, “rank these three in this order,” and the groups that can deliver a disciplined ranking gain leverage that choose-one never gave them. The Stranger, MLK Labor, the Chamber’s CASE PAC, the DSA: their slate cards become the working ballot for the voters who follow them, and gatekeeping consolidates instead of opening up.

The democracy voucher program collides with this in a way nobody intends. Vouchers and ranked-choice both cut the cost of running, so the field swells, more names, more advertised voices. August then winnows the swollen field back to the same two-faction result, decided by the same summer electorate. The proliferation is real and the choice is not. And vouchers get redeemed disproportionately by the engaged older homeowner, so the public-financing system built to broaden participation reinforces the skew it was meant to cure.

Incumbents gain. A summer tournament rewards name recognition and a standing relationship with the reliable voter, which is the incumbent’s home ground, so turnover slows and accountability dulls. The promised drop in negative campaigning mostly relocates. Inside a faction you stay friendly to harvest each other’s transfers; across factions you can still swing hard, but the cross-faction race is the foregone one, so the attacks land in the contest that no longer decides anything.

### The bifurcated city

The clearest long-run signature is a split personality: a moderate-leaning council elected in August, and a steady run of progressive ballot measures passed in November by the broader electorate. The shape is already visible. The Social Housing Developer arrived by initiative in 2023 and got funded by a payroll-tax measure in 2024, both over the preference of the council’s moderate center. Project that forward and Seattle governs in two registers, the legislature it elects pointing one way and the policy it enacts by referendum pointing the other, with the gap policed by a standing initiative war. The activist energy that cannot win the August council fight pours into the citywide ballot box, the one venue where the renter majority’s turnout shows up. Government by initiative is what a city does when its representative channel gets captured upstream of the vote it notices.

### Ten to twenty years out

The first cycles, 2027 into the early 2030s, are confusion and disappointment. Ranked-choice debuts in the worst available context, an odd-year August primary, and throws off a few jarring results: a moderate taking a district that leans left on paper, a well-liked consensus figure who never reaches November, a progressive base that turned out in the fall to find the choice already closed. The left’s institutions diagnose it before the pundits do and start building August-specific turnout machines, chasing renter and student ballots in July. A summer turnout arms race follows, the moderates defending a structural edge and the left chasing a demographic gap the calendar keeps reopening.

The honest mitigant in this story is the move to even-year elections, and it deserves weight rather than a wave of the hand. Even-year turnout in King County runs close to double odd-year turnout, and enlarging the electorate is the strongest available lever against the skew. Seattle has edged toward it through special elections and is maneuvering toward a fuller shift. If regular council races land in even years, the November electorate grows younger and more renter, the foregone-general problem eases, and the split narrows. The catch is that the deciding winnowing still happens in the lower-turnout August primary even in an even year, and that primary electorate is still older and more moderate than the general it feeds. Even-year migration softens the worst of it. It cannot move the elimination out of the primary, because bottom-up ranked-choice puts it there by design.

By the late 2030s the system settles: a stable council, moderate at the margin and protective of incumbents, a renter majority permanently under-weighted in the contest that decides, and a city whose elected body sits visibly to the right of its own ballot-measure behavior. That dissonance corrodes in a specific way. It produces a constant supply of grievance with no legible target, which feeds cynicism and the search for villains. It also sets up the next reform fight. The representation argument returns, because the renter majority eventually works out that it cannot win single-winner district races filtered through a homeowner primary, and the structural answer to that is proportional multi-member representation, the system that would seat the city’s factions in proportion to their size. Having been told in 2022 that ranked-choice was the upgrade, Seattle spends the 2040s relitigating whether it picked the right one.

### The diagnosis

The fair case for ranked-choice is real, and stating it correctly only sharpens the problem. Its genuine advantage over plurality is vote-split mitigation: eliminate a flank spoiler, transfer the votes, and a divided faction is no longer punished for fielding more than one candidate. That is a true improvement, and it is the one the simulation honors in full. But that advantage is mechanically inseparable from the center-squeeze. Consolidating each faction onto its champion is the same act as advancing both champions and discarding the consensus candidate between them, which is why ranked-choice squeezes that candidate out more often than the plurality system it replaces under sincere voting, and nearly as often under strategic voting, where most of its distinction from plurality disappears. The virtue and the vice are one operation. You cannot keep the spoiler resistance and lose the squeeze, because they are the same move.

The indictment that survives every framing is the one a critic cannot dodge. The decision has been relocated into a low-turnout summer primary whose electorate skews old, wealthy, and homeowner, which is a property of Washington’s top-two structure that any reform inside it inherits. On top of that shared problem, ranked-choice adds the squeeze and then, under realistic voters, surrenders the very distinction from plurality that was its reason for existing. Seattle reformed its way into a homeowner-amplifying, consensus-squeezing primary, felt more modern for having done it, and will spend the following decade blaming its own electorate’s preferences for results the counting rule produced. The August electorate did not get more conservative. The machine that listens to it got louder, and the machine that would have listened to everyone else got switched off until November, by which point the choosing was done.

### What Seattle turned down

Approval voting was on the same ballot, and it was there first. The citizens’ initiative, I-134, qualified by petition in July 2022, backed by Seattle Approves and the Center for Election Science, which put about $313,000 into the campaign. Before that initiative was even printed onto ballots, the City Council referred a competing measure, ranked-choice voting, as Proposition 1B, with the approval initiative relabeled Proposition 1A. FairVote Action, a Maryland group, funded the ranked-choice campaign with about $390,000. The ballot then asked two questions: whether to change the system at all, and, if so, which of the two. Voters approved a change and picked ranked-choice over approval by roughly 75 to 25.

The case the ranked-choice campaign made against approval is the part worth checking, because the simulation can now adjudicate it. The Progressive Voters Guide warned that approval could “give an outsized electoral say to wealthier and whiter primary voters,” and the campaign argued approval was untested and might invite a Voting Rights Act challenge. The proven case sat right there: St. Louis, a majority-Black city, had adopted the same top-two approval structure in 2020 and used it in 2021 to elect Tishaura Jones, its first Black woman mayor, on a base of support from the communities approval was said to harm.

This comparison runs the way elections actually run: about 85 percent of voters strategic, the rest sincere. Approval voters approve every candidate they prefer to the expected winner, drawn from a noisy pre-election poll. Ranked voters polarize the two perceived frontrunners and rank the rest honestly, and they are given the benefit of the doubt that ranked-choice’s transfers let more of them rank an honest favorite without splitting their faction’s vote, so they compromise onto frontrunners somewhat less than plurality voters do. Plurality voters compromise onto a frontrunner. Three things fall out, and all three cut against ranked-choice.

_The three systems on the same August electorate with 85 percent strategic voters. Left: approval squeezes the consensus candidate out 5 percent of the time against ranked-choice’s 27, seats the consensus winner 95 percent against 73, and leaves fewer Novembers foregone; under strategy ranked-choice and plurality converge. Right: approval’s two finalists sit far closer to the electorate’s median, the “two broadly appealing candidates” outcome._

First, ranked-choice collapses toward plurality, for the reason given above: strategic first preferences are plurality votes, and the frontrunners they name are never eliminated. Strategic ranked-choice squeezes the consensus candidate out 27 percent of the time and plurality 29, with welfare losses that round together.

Second, approval moves the opposite way, and strategy makes it better. The strategic rule, approve everyone above the expected winner, is precisely what surfaces the broadly-supported candidate, so approval seats the November consensus winner 95 percent of the time and squeezes it out only 5. Its two finalists sit at a mean distance of 0.37 from the electorate’s median against ranked-choice’s 0.57, which is the quantitative form of a Spencer-versus-someone runoff rather than a firebrand-versus-firebrand one. Because the two it advances draw support across the whole electorate, its Novembers are the least foregone of the three, and the fall electorate gets a real contest instead of ratifying a factional blowout.

Third, the objection that was supposed to sink approval does not survive the strategic model. The worry was that a cohesive bloc could bullet-vote its way into capturing both finalist slots. Bullet voting is not a robust strategy. If you are a progressive in a moderate-leaning August electorate and you approve only your two favorite progressives, you are betting they can reach a finalist slot. Denying the moderates your approval barely dents them; what keeps a moderate out of the second slot is a rival outpolling them, and the rival positioned to do that is the broadly-supported centrist. Bullet voting withholds support from exactly that candidate, so if your favorites are not viable the moderates advance on everyone else’s ballots and the one move that could have blocked them goes unspent. The rational move under approval is to approve every candidate they prefer to the expected winner, which is why the strategic equilibrium produces two broadly-supported finalists rather than a captured pair, and why with strategic voters approval’s finalists get more central and its squeeze rate falls, the opposite of what capture predicts. The St. Louis cast-vote records say the same thing in the field, where the average ballot approved 1.4 candidates and the broadest-coalition candidate advanced and won.

The one honest concession survives, and it belongs to the structure rather than to the rule. Approval does not broaden the August electorate. The decisive winnowing still happens in a low-turnout summer primary that skews older, wealthier, and more homeowner, and that is a property of Washington’s top-two design that every reform inside it inherits. What approval changes is what August produces. Ranked-choice hands November two factional frontrunners and a foregone result, and under realistic voters does it no differently than plurality would. Approval hands November two candidates with the widest support across the electorate and a contest worth holding. Seattle was told it was choosing the more representative reform. Under realistic voters it chose the one that, in contested races, behaves like the plurality system it replaced, and it turned down the one that seats the consensus candidate 95 times in 100 and gives the fall electorate a real choice between the two people the whole city could live with.
