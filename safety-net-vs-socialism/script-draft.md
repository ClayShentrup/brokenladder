# every socialist policy is a gift card (draft script v2)

## cold open
O1 This week, Amazon warned New York shoppers that a proposed city law could add up to five dollars to every order. The mayor, Zohran Mamdani, had a one-word answer. "It's greed."
O2 Think about what that implies. If charging the market price is greed, then charging less than the market price is generosity. Charging less than you could is giving money away. It's charity.
O3 We don't call every Amazon shareholder greedy for not writing checks to strangers. But demanding that a company quietly eat a cost is exactly that: a demand that its owners donate.
O4 And if you're going to demand charity, why this charity? A discount on delivery goes to everyone who orders online in New York, including some of the richest people in the richest city in the richest country on earth. If the point is helping people who need it, there are a few billion better candidates.
O5 This is the pattern behind nearly every socialist economic policy. It delivers help as a gift card instead of cash, and someone you didn't expect pays the difference.
O6 This video won't tell you how much to help the poor. More, less, none at all. That's a question of values, and you can answer it however you like. This is about how. Because whatever amount you pick, the socialist toolkit picks the most wasteful way to deliver it.
[title card]

## a note on terms
S1 A quick note, because someone in the comments will raise it. At its core, socialism means replacing market decisions with political ones. Rent control is a city official deciding what an apartment should cost. That's central planning, one building at a time.

## the concept nobody in this fight uses
P1 Here's the idea most of this debate is missing. It's called deadweight loss, and the easiest way to see it is a gift.
P2 Say I buy you a sweater for ten dollars, but to you it's only worth seven.
P3 I'm down ten. You're up seven. Three dollars just vanished, and nobody got it. That gap, between what help costs and what it's worth to the person receiving it, is the whole game.

## exhibit a: rent control
R1 Now, rent control. An apartment worth three thousand dollars a month, capped at two thousand.
R2 The tenant gets a thousand-dollar discount. Who pays for it? Not the city. Not taxpayers. Whoever happened to own that particular building.
R3 So it's a housing subsidy, funded by a tax on one landlord, chosen more or less at random. Nobody would propose that out loud. And it gets worse.
R4 The discount only works in this one apartment, so tenants stay put long after it suits them. And landlords respond. In San Francisco, rent control led landlords to cut the supply of rental housing by fifteen percent, which pushed up rents for everyone who didn't already have a controlled unit.
R5 When leading economists were surveyed on whether rent control had made housing more affordable in the cities that tried it, almost none agreed.

## the gift card fallacy
G1 Rent control is one case of a pattern. Call it the gift card fallacy: helping people with restricted benefits and price controls instead of money.
G2 Win a public housing lottery, and you get one particular apartment, almost certainly not the one you'd have picked with the money. Food stamps that won't buy a hot meal. Free street parking: a gift card for drivers, paid for by everyone.
G3 Every one of these is the sweater. The family gets less than the program costs, and the difference disappears.
G4 And no, the poor don't blow the cash. A World Bank review of nineteen studies found that when poor families get cash, spending on alcohol and tobacco doesn't go up. If anything, it goes down.

## where the usual critics go wrong
C1 Now, the usual critics of socialism. They spot these flaws and conclude: scrap rent control, scrap the minimum wage, and replace them with nothing.
C2 That's why the argument never lands. To a voter who wants to help people, "replace it with nothing" just sounds like "I don't want to help." The problem with rent control is the mechanism, and that's true whether you want to help people a little or a lot.
C3 Take the minimum wage. The evidence on moderate increases is mixed. Some studies find job losses, others find almost none. It's at least plausible that a modest minimum wage helps low earners more than it costs.
C4 Even so, it's the wrong comparison. The real question is whether the same help could be delivered more cheaply. It can.

## the alternative
A1 Economics has a theorem for this: the second welfare theorem. In its ideal form, it says any amount of redistribution can be done without shrinking the economy, as long as the money moves in ways that don't depend on what anyone does.
A2 Real life never gets that perfect. But you can get far closer than the socialist toolkit does.
A3 First, tax pollution. A factory that dumps smoke on its neighbors makes too much, because someone else is paying part of the bill. Charging that bill back actually grows the economy.
A4 Second, tax land. Nobody can make more of it, so taxing it doesn't make less of it. The bill is the same whether the owner builds, sells, or does nothing. No trade gets blocked.
A5 How far do those two go? Further than most people think. Henry George argued a land tax alone could fund the government, and scrapping every other tax would push land values higher still, because all that freed-up income ends up bidding for the same fixed supply of land. To the extent it still falls short, add a flat tax on consumption, with as few loopholes as possible.
A6 Then hand the money back as cash. The same check for every citizen. Even with a flat tax in the mix, say forty percent with a ten-thousand-dollar dividend, the system ends up about as progressive as most bracket systems, with no welfare cliffs and no bureaucrat deciding what you're allowed to buy.
A7 Milton Friedman, not exactly a socialist, called this a negative income tax.

## close
Z1 Back to Amazon. If New York wants delivery workers to have more money, it can send them money, out in the open, paid for by taxes that don't block useful trades, where voters can see the price. Instead it hands the bill to everyone who orders a phone charger, and calls it greed when the bill arrives.
Z2 Pick any amount of redistribution you like. Zero, a little, a lot. Whatever it is, cash delivers it cheaper.
Z3 Whatever you think people deserve, give it to them in cash.
