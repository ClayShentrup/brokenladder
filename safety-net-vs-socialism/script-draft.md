# i support the safety net. that's why i oppose socialism. (draft script)

## cold open
O1 Socialists like to say their critics hate poor people. "You think people shouldn't be able to afford rent? You think kids shouldn't eat?"
O2 And a lot of the time, they've picked the right opponent. The usual case against socialism comes from people who want less help for the poor. Less redistribution, a smaller safety net, or none at all.
O3 This video is different. I want a safety net. I'm fine with a big one. And that's exactly why I think the socialist toolkit is a disaster.
O4 The goal is fine. The machinery is the problem. These policies take money meant for the poor and set a good chunk of it on fire along the way.
[title card]

## what we mean by socialism
S1 A quick word on terms. Strictly, socialism means the state owning the means of production. But that's rarely what people mean online. There, socialism means rent control, price caps, public housing, free this and free that: policies that try to help people by overriding prices.
S2 That's the version this video is about. The full version, where the state runs the whole economy, fails for the same reason, only bigger.

## the concept nobody in this fight uses
P1 Here's the idea most of this debate is missing. It's called deadweight loss, and the easiest way to see it is a gift.
P2 Say I buy you a sweater for ten dollars, but to you it's only worth seven.
P3 I'm down ten. You're up seven. Three dollars just vanished, and nobody got it. That gap, between what help costs and what it's worth to the person receiving it, is the whole game.

## exhibit a: rent control
R1 Now, rent control. An apartment worth three thousand dollars a month, capped at two thousand.
R2 The tenant gets a thousand-dollar discount. Who pays for it? Not the city. Not taxpayers. Whoever happened to own that particular building.
R3 So it's a housing subsidy, funded by a tax on one landlord, chosen more or less at random. Nobody would propose that out loud. And it gets worse.
R4 The discount only works in this one apartment, so tenants stay put long after it suits them. And landlords respond. In San Francisco, rent control led landlords to cut the supply of rental housing by fifteen percent, which pushed up rents for everyone who didn't already have a controlled unit.
R5 When leading economists were surveyed on whether rent control had made housing more affordable in the cities that tried it, almost none agreed.

## the gift card fallacy
G1 Rent control is one case of a pattern. Call it the gift card fallacy: helping people with restricted benefits instead of money.
G2 Win a public housing lottery, and you get one particular apartment, almost certainly not the one you'd have picked with the money. Food stamps that won't buy a hot meal. Free street parking: a gift card for drivers, paid for by everyone.
G3 Every one of these is the sweater. The family gets less than the program costs, and the difference disappears.
G4 And no, the poor don't blow the cash. A World Bank review of nineteen studies found that when poor families get cash, spending on alcohol and tobacco doesn't go up. If anything, it goes down.

## where the usual critics go wrong
C1 Now here's where the usual critics of socialism blow it. They find these flaws and conclude: so scrap rent control, scrap the minimum wage, and replace them with nothing.
C2 That's why the argument never lands. If the choice is a leaky safety net or no safety net, plenty of people will take the leaky one. And some of them are right to.
C3 Take the minimum wage. The evidence on moderate increases is mixed. Some studies find job losses, others find almost none. It's at least plausible that a modest minimum wage helps low earners more than it costs.
C4 But that's the wrong comparison. The real question is what else that same help could look like. And there's a much better answer.

## the alternative
A1 Economics has a theorem for this: the second welfare theorem. In its ideal form, it says any amount of redistribution can be done without shrinking the economy, as long as the money moves in ways that don't depend on what anyone does.
A2 Real life never gets that perfect. But you can get far closer than the socialist toolkit does.
A3 First, tax pollution. A factory that dumps smoke on its neighbors makes too much, because someone else is paying part of the bill. Charging that bill back actually grows the economy.
A4 Second, tax land. Nobody can make more of it, so taxing it doesn't make less of it. The bill is the same whether the owner builds, sells, or does nothing. No trade gets blocked.
A5 Those two won't cover everything. For the rest, use one simple tax on spending or income, with as few loopholes as possible. That part does shrink the pie a little. Keep it small.
A6 Then hand the money back as cash. The same check for every citizen. A forty percent flat tax with a ten-thousand-dollar dividend is about as progressive as most bracket systems, with no welfare cliffs and no bureaucrat deciding what you're allowed to buy.
A7 Milton Friedman, not exactly a socialist, called this a negative income tax.

## close
Z1 So here's the challenge for anyone defending rent control because people deserve a home. Agreed. Now explain why you'd rather hand them a lottery ticket for one apartment than the money to choose their own.
Z2 Pick whatever amount of redistribution you like. A little, a lot. Whatever it is, cash gets it there cheaper, and the poor end up with more.
Z3 Wanting a safety net doesn't make you a socialist. Wanting one that works is a pretty good reason not to be one.
