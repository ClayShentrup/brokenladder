# the economic illiteracy of socialism

full script, final. about 4,000 words, roughly 27 minutes at a measured pace. line ids are for production.

---

## 1. cold open: "it's greed"

O1 In November twenty twenty-six, Amazon warned New York shoppers that a proposed city law, the Delivery Protection Act, could add up to five dollars to every order.

O2 The mayor, Zohran Mamdani, answered on X. "Amazon is a multi-trillion dollar company. If Jeff Bezos adds five dollars to your order, it's not because of City Council's efforts to protect workers' rights and keep our streets safe. It's greed."

O2b Notice the move. New rules about how deliveries must be run cost money to follow. But according to the mayor, the law can't be the reason prices go up. And if they go up anyway, that's greed.

O3 Sit with that for a second. To the mayor, absorbing the cost is just the decent thing to do, the default. But look at what it actually is. Charge a customer five dollars less than you could, or charge full price and mail them a five-dollar check. Run the books both ways, and the company ends the year with exactly the same money, and so does the customer. Absorbing a cost you could pass on is a donation.

O3b So the accusation isn't that Amazon takes something from anyone. Nobody ends up worse off than they were. The accusation is that its owners failed to give enough away. We don't usually call people greedy for skipping a donation. And if we're going to demand charity, why aim it at whoever happens to be shopping online, rather than people who need it far more?

O5 And notice who's being drafted to fund it. Not taxpayers in general. Whoever happens to own shares in one particular company, whether that's a retired couple living off a pension fund or someone fresh out of college putting their first paycheck into an index fund. That's a tax, just an invisible one. Hold that thought. We'll see the exact same move again, with a landlord.

O4 And the fact that Amazon is a multi-trillion dollar company doesn't help the mayor's case. A company's size says nothing about how rich any one of its owners is. A cost forced onto Amazon lands on Jeff Bezos and that new grad's index fund alike. If the goal is for rich people to pay more, then tax rich people, openly, where voters can see the bill, and design the tax to do as little damage as possible. A hidden mandate does neither. It's a speeding ticket on every delivery in the city.

O6 This is the mayor of the largest city in America. And this is how he thinks about prices.

O7 Here's the claim this video is going to defend. Nearly every policy on the socialist menu is the same mistake, repeated over and over. Once you see the mistake, you can't unsee it.

O8 But first, a warning, because you're going to think you know where this is going. You probably don't.

[title card: the economic illiteracy of socialism]

## 2. this is not the argument you think it is

N1 When someone attacks socialism, you know the script. Taxes are theft. Handouts make people lazy. Cut the safety net.

N2 That's not the argument we're making.

N3 This video takes no position on how much we should help the poor. More than now, less than now, none at all. That's a question of values, and you're entitled to your own answer.

N4 Our question is narrower, and a lot harder to wriggle out of. Whatever amount of help you've decided on, what's the least wasteful way to deliver it?

N4b Picture the economy as a pie. Growing the pie is one goal. Sharing it is another. Most political fights assume you have to trade one for the other. This video is about sharing the pie, however much you want to share, without shrinking it any more than you have to.

N6 If at any point you think you hear the case for cutting welfare, you don't. That's not the argument we're making, and we'll keep saying so.

## 3. what socialism even means

M1 First, a word on terms, because someone in the comments is already typing.

M2 What is socialism, exactly? Even its founders weren't tidy about it. Marx and Engels used socialism and communism loosely, and over their careers applied both words to their own ideas. The neat version, where socialism is a waystation on the road to communism, came later, mostly from Lenin.

M3 People use the word for everything from Soviet five-year plans to Scandinavian welfare states. So forget the dictionary. Look at what self-described socialists actually run on. Mamdani's platform is a good sample: a rent freeze, city-run grocery stores, fare-free buses, and mandates like the delivery law.

M4 Add the rest of the usual menu. Price controls, like rent control and the minimum wage. Nationalized industries. Benefits handed out in kind instead of in cash.

M5 Underneath every item is the same move: replace a price the market would set with a decision made politically. Rent control is a city official deciding what an apartment is allowed to cost. That's central planning, one building at a time.

## 4. the three dollars that vanished

D1 The concept is called deadweight loss. The clearest way to see it is a gift.

D2 Say I buy you a sweater for ten dollars. How much is it worth to you? We don't have to guess. Offer you a choice: keep the sweater, or take cash instead. The smallest amount of cash you'd accept in its place is what it's worth to you. Say it's seven dollars. Economists call that revealed preference: your own choice tells us the value.

D3 So I've spent ten dollars, and you've gained something worth seven. The other three dollars just disappeared. I didn't get them. You didn't get them. Nobody did.

D4 This isn't a thought experiment. The economist Joel Waldfogel asked people what cash they'd have accepted in place of each holiday gift they received, and found that somewhere between a tenth and a third of what givers spent simply evaporated.

D4b To be fair, a holiday gift carries things cash can't: the thought behind it, the ritual, the people around the tree. Nobody's saying you should mail your grandmother a check. But none of that sentiment comes with a housing voucher. You're not sipping eggnog with Uncle Sam.

D6 If I'd handed you a ten-dollar bill instead, nothing would have been lost. That missing three dollars is deadweight loss. Value that could have existed, and doesn't.

## 5. the roof that never gets fixed

T1 It gets bigger. When two people trade freely, both expect to come out ahead, or they'd walk away.

T2 Economists have a name for a change that makes someone better off and nobody worse off. A Pareto improvement. It's the one kind of change you can call good without weighing one person's happiness against another's.

T3 Every voluntary trade is a little Pareto improvement. Say Dana would fix your roof for twenty dollars an hour, and you'd happily pay twenty-five. The job happens, and five dollars of value appears out of nowhere, split between you.

T4 Now put an eight-dollar tax on that hour of work. Dana needs twenty-eight to make it worthwhile. You'll only pay twenty-five. The job never happens.

T5 The government collects nothing. Dana loses the work. Your roof still leaks. And five dollars of value never comes into existence.

T6 And notice this isn't a judgment call. Nobody forced either of you into that deal. The fact that you both chose it is proof that you both came out ahead. That's revealed preference again. Which means a tax that blocks a useful trade like that one makes the world worse, by definition.

T7 Hold onto those two ideas. Benefits that are worth less than they cost. Taxes that kill trades that would have happened. Those are the two leaks. And the socialist toolkit is built out of them.

## 6. exhibit a: rent control

R1 Rent control. The flagship. Mamdani campaigned on freezing the rent on a million stabilized apartments.

R2 So take one. Market rent, three thousand dollars a month. Controlled rent, two thousand.

R3 The tenant saves a thousand dollars a month. Where does that thousand come from?

R4 Not from the city. Not from taxpayers. It comes out of one person's pocket: whoever happened to own that particular building.

R5 So rent control is a thousand-dollar housing voucher, funded by a thousand-dollar tax on one landlord, chosen more or less at random. Propose that policy in those words, and almost nobody would vote for it. Rent control is the same policy, under a friendlier name.

R5b And notice what rent control can't live without. If the landlord could simply decline to renew the lease, the discount would vanish at the end of the year. So rent control always comes with eviction control. In New York, a rent-stabilized tenant has a legal right to renew, and the landlord can't end the lease just because the rent is below market. The thousand-dollar tax never expires.

R6 And it gets worse, because people respond.

R7 The tenant's discount only works in this one apartment. So they stay put long after it suits them. The job across town, the bigger place for the new baby, the smaller place after the kids leave. All of it now costs a thousand dollars a month in lost discount.

R8 Landlords respond too. When economists studied rent control in San Francisco, they found landlords cut the supply of rental housing by fifteen percent, by converting buildings to condos and redeveloping them. Which pushed up rents for everyone who didn't already have a controlled unit.

R9 When leading economists were surveyed on whether rent control had made housing more affordable in the cities that tried it, almost none agreed. This is about as close to consensus as economics gets.

R10 So rent control fails both tests. It's a benefit worth less than it costs, and it's a tax that kills trades. A double leak.

## 7. the gift card fallacy

G1 Rent control is one example of a pattern so common it deserves a name: helping people with one specific thing instead of money. Call it the gift card fallacy.

G2 And it usually isn't a belief anyone states out loud. It's a reflex. People need housing, so give them housing. People need food, so give them food. The alternative, the same help with no strings attached, never even comes to mind. So the gift card gets measured against the wrong baseline: not against cash, but against nothing. Call it the false baseline.

G2b Put another way, a benefit in kind is just cash, with a restriction on how you can spend it. Handing you a ten-dollar sweater is the same as handing you ten dollars, plus one rule: you may only spend it on this sweater. And a rule can never give you more options. It can only take them away.

G3 Every gift card is the sweater. The family receives something worth less to them than what it cost to provide. The gap vanishes.

G3a This part isn't a matter of opinion. Give the family the cash instead. If they'd have bought exactly what the gift card buys, the gift card was only as good as cash. If they'd buy something else, then by their own choice, that something else was worth more to them. Either way, cash is at least as good. It is never worse.

G3b Here's a test that makes it concrete. Say you land a rent-stabilized apartment, and you'd rather live somewhere cheaper and keep the difference. So you sublet it at the market rate, move, and spend the extra on food, or childcare, or whatever your family needs most.

G3c That's against the rules. In New York, subletting a rent-stabilized apartment for more than the legal rent, plus a small surcharge if it's furnished, can get you evicted and leave you owing your subtenant up to three times the overcharge. Try it with a public housing unit, and you can lose the unit.

G3d Think about what that means. Refusing to give people cash comes with an enforcement arm. When someone converts their gift card into cash on their own, you punish them for it. The policy only works if you stop people from spending their own benefit the way they think best.

G5 The usual objection to cash is that the poor will waste it. That's been tested, a lot. A World Bank review of nineteen studies found that when poor families get cash, spending on alcohol and tobacco doesn't go up. If anything, it goes down.

## 7b. the short answer

Q1 So before we go further, here's the alternative, in four sentences.

Q2 Whatever amount of help you want to give, give it as cash. The same check to every citizen.

Q3 Pay for it with taxes that don't block useful trades: taxes on pollution, and on the value of land. If that's not enough, one flat tax on spending.

Q4 That's it. Same help, far less waste. The rest of this video is about why that works, and why almost every policy you hear called socialist does the opposite.

## 8. the parade

P0 Now watch how far the pattern goes.

P1 A public housing lottery. Win it, and you get one particular apartment, in one particular building, in one particular neighborhood. Almost certainly not the one you'd have picked if you'd been handed the money. Gift card.

P2 Food stamps. To be fair, for most families they work almost like cash, because most families spend more on groceries than the benefit is worth anyway. The waste comes from the rules, like the ban on hot meals, and only when those rules change what a family actually buys. A partial gift card. Partial credit.

P2b And notice who else adds rules. Starting in twenty twenty-five, the Trump administration approved state after state's request to ban soda and candy from food stamps. Progressives called it paternalism, and they were right. And notice how easily they saw it. Nobody compared the soda ban to having no food stamps at all. They compared it to the same food stamps, without the ban. The right baseline was obvious.

P2c Now use that baseline everywhere. Compare rent control, city grocery stores and free-but-only-for-this programs, not to nothing, but to the same help with no strings attached. Republicans didn't trust poor families to pick their own groceries, and progressives rightly objected. So why trust them any less to pick their own apartment?

P3 Free street parking. Curb space in a city is some of the most valuable land on earth, handed out free to whoever owns a car. Paid for by everyone, including the people who don't. A gift card for drivers.

P4 Price gouging laws. We'll come back to these, because they deserve their own section.

P5 Mamdani's city-owned grocery stores, which wouldn't pay rent or property tax. Those waived costs are a subsidy, delivered only to people who live near one of the stores and want to shop there. Why not give people the money and let them shop wherever they like? Gift card, with a location attached.

P6 Fare-free buses, another Mamdani promise. This one gets partial credit too. Buses take cars off the road, and fewer cars means less traffic and pollution for everyone. That's a real case for subsidizing them. But zero fares go to every rider, rich or poor, and the cleaner fix is charging drivers for the congestion they cause. A gift card with a half-decent excuse.

P8 Which brings us back to Amazon. The Delivery Protection Act is meant to help delivery workers. Fine. But it doesn't hand them money. It loads costs onto every online order in the city, including orders from people poorer than the workers it's meant to help. A hidden sales tax, paid in the dark.

P9 Policy after policy, take it apart and you find the same machine inside. Help delivered in kind instead of cash. Paid for by a tax on some trade that would otherwise have happened.

## 8b. happy hour in reverse

H1 Every time there's a storm, a strike, or a wave of cancelled flights, the same outrage cycle kicks off. Uber triples its prices. Gouging! Greed! Somebody pass a law.

H2 So think about the night the flights get grounded. Thousands of stranded travelers open the app at once. There are only so many cars.

H3 Who should get one? The traveler stuck at the airport at midnight with a sick kid, or the guy running an errand he could just as easily do tomorrow?

H4 You can't tell them apart by looking. Neither can Uber. But the price can. At triple the fare, the errand waits until tomorrow, and the car goes to someone whose trip can't wait.

H5 And the price does something else. It tells the driver who was planning to stay home tonight that tonight is the night to work. A surge rations the cars, and it summons more of them.

H6 Cap the price, and both effects vanish. The cars go to whoever taps first. The extra drivers stay home. The stranded family waits.

H7 Here's the thing nobody says. Surge pricing is just happy hour, viewed from the other direction. A bar cuts its prices at four in the afternoon because the place is empty. Which means the regular price on a Friday night is the surge price. Nobody calls that greed. It's one signal, pointing both ways: come now, there's room, or only come now if it matters. And drivers, please show up.

H8 And if what bothers you is that a poor family can't afford the surge, fine. That's a real concern about equity. Which is a case for giving that family more money, every day of the year. Breaking the one signal that gets cars to the people who need them helps nobody.

H9 A price cap is a discount for whoever's quickest, paid for by everyone left stranded. Gift card fallacy. Again.

H9b Economists call this rationing by price versus rationing by waiting. Free roads ration by waiting. That's why Houston widened the Katy Freeway to as many as twenty-six lanes, and within a few years, rush-hour trips took longer than before. Free lanes just fill up. When New York started charging drivers to enter the busiest part of Manhattan in twenty twenty-five, traffic dropped and trips got faster, and plenty of progressives cheered. That's surge pricing, for roads.

## 9. where the usual critics blow it

C1 Now, here's the part where conservatives and libertarians usually take a victory lap. See? Rent control is a disaster. Scrap it. Scrap the minimum wage. Scrap the food stamps.

C2 And replace them with nothing.

C3 That's why their argument never lands. To a voter who wants to help people, "replace it with nothing" sounds exactly like "I don't want to help people." And the socialist on social media gets to say: you think kids shouldn't eat?

C4 Take the minimum wage. The evidence on moderate increases is mixed. Some studies find meaningful job losses. Others find almost none. It's at least plausible that a modest minimum wage helps low earners more than it costs, up to a point.

C5 We're not going to pretend otherwise. If your only choices were a modest minimum wage or nothing, the minimum wage might win.

C6 But those aren't your only choices. That's the whole point.

## 10. what "trickle-down" means

K1 You'll hear a phrase thrown at anyone who criticizes these policies. Trickle-down economics.

K2 Let's be precise about what it means, because almost nobody is. The trickle-down claim is this: if you remove some distorting tax or subsidy, the economy grows enough that even the people who were being helped come out ahead. The efficiency gain outweighs the lost equity.

K3 Sometimes that's true. For rent control, it may well be true. Scrap it, more housing gets built, rents fall, and many tenants end up better off.

K4 But it isn't true everywhere, automatically. And that's where the usual left-right fight gets stuck. One side says grow the pie, even if it's shared less evenly. The other says share the pie, even if it shrinks. They argue about where to set one knob: more sharing and a smaller pie, or a bigger pie and less sharing.

K5 We're turning a different knob entirely.

K6 Hold the amount of redistribution fixed. Exactly the same amount of help, going to exactly the same people. Now ask: can we deliver it with less waste?

K7 Almost always, yes. And nobody loses their help.

K8 We'll say it once more, for the people already drafting an angry comment. Same redistribution. Less waste. If you hear that as an argument for cutting welfare, you've just made the exact mistake this video is about.

K9 In twenty eighteen, the Niskanen Center published a paper by Samuel Hammond with a title that sounds like an oxymoron: The Free-Market Welfare State. Look at the two knobs, and it isn't one. Turn the how-much knob as high as you like. Keep the how knob on open markets and cash.

## 11. the theorem

W1 Economics has a result for this, and it's been sitting in the textbooks for decades. The second welfare theorem.

W2 In its ideal form, it says you can reach any distribution of resources you like, a little sharing or a lot, without shrinking the economy at all, as long as the money moves in ways that don't depend on what anyone does.

W3 Real life never gets that perfect. But we can get much closer than any gift card ever will.

W4 And this splits one fight into two. How much to share is a question of values. How to share is a question of engineering. And engineering has better and worse answers.

---

## 12. taxes that don't leak

X1 Start with taxes that don't block useful trades. There are two big ones.

X2 Pollution first. When a factory dumps smoke on its neighbors for free, it produces too much, because someone else is paying part of the bill. Economists call that a negative externality.

X3 A pollution tax hands the bill back to the polluter. And here's the remarkable part: this tax removes deadweight loss. The economy was producing too much smoke, and the tax pulls it back toward the right amount. The economist Arthur Pigou worked this out over a century ago, which is why these are called Pigouvian taxes. A tax that makes the pie bigger.

X4 Next, land. Picture an empty lot. A train station opens nearby. Then shops, then a hospital, then thousands of neighbors. The lot is suddenly worth far more.

X5 The owner didn't do any of that. The value soaked into the lot from everything around it. It's a positive externality, created by everyone else, captured by whoever holds the deed.

X6 Now tax that land value. Look at who pays: the owner. Now look at who created the value: the train station, the shops, the hospital, the neighbors. The person paying the tax isn't the one whose work leaked into the land. And the bill is the same whether they build, sell, work, or sit in a lawn chair. Nothing they do changes it. No trade gets blocked. No deadweight loss.

X7 How far can these two go? Further than most people think. Henry George argued in eighteen seventy-nine that a land tax alone could fund the government. Here's why that's plausible. Scrap the taxes on wages and sales, and people have more money to spend. Much of it ends up competing for the same places to live and work, and that competition pushes up the rental value of land. So the land tax brings in more, and it still doesn't block a single trade.

X8 To the extent that still falls short, add one flat tax on consumption, with as few loopholes as possible. That part does block some trades. It shrinks the pie a little. So keep it as small as you can.

## 13. marginal versus effective

E1 At this point someone always asks: but don't we want progressive taxes? Shouldn't the rich pay a bigger share?

E2 Maybe. But first, which kind of progressive do you mean? Because there are two, and almost nobody separates them.

E3 Your marginal tax rate is what you pay on the next dollar you earn. Your effective tax rate is what you pay overall, as a share of everything you earn.

E4 Most people assume you need progressive marginal rates, higher brackets for higher incomes, to get progressive effective rates. You don't.

E5 Watch. Say there's a flat forty percent income tax. We're not proposing an income tax; it's just the simplest example. And every citizen gets a ten-thousand-dollar dividend.

E6 Earn twelve thousand dollars. You pay four thousand eight hundred in tax, get ten thousand back, and take home seventeen thousand two hundred. Your effective tax rate is negative forty-three percent.

E7 Earn fifty thousand: an effective rate of twenty percent. A hundred thousand: thirty percent. A million: thirty-nine percent.

E8 One marginal rate. Effective rates that climb from deeply negative to nearly forty percent. About as progressive as most bracket systems, with no brackets at all.

E9 And the marginal rate is the one that shapes incentives. It's what you weigh when you decide whether to take the extra shift, or whether to work for someone else at all, versus spending that time caring for your kids or an aging parent. Which brings us to the ugliest number in the welfare state.

E10 Here's what that looks like in practice. PolicyEngine, which builds an open-source model of the American tax and benefit system, ran the numbers for a single parent in California with two kids, paying rent and childcare on a low wage. Double their pay. After lost benefits, lost tax credits and higher taxes, the family keeps only about a fifth of the extra. An effective marginal tax rate of nearly eighty percent.

E10b In other words, a single parent near the poverty line can face a higher marginal tax rate than a billionaire. And at some incomes, losing a benefit all at once means earning more leaves you with less. That's the welfare cliff.

E10c Remember the minimum wage fight? Under an eighty percent marginal rate, a worker keeps a fifth of every extra dollar. A fifteen-dollar minimum wage becomes a three-dollar one. People spend years fighting over the first number and never mention the second.

E11 A flat dividend never shrinks when you earn more. No cliff. Every extra hour leaves you better off.

E12 Which raises the obvious objection. Why should a millionaire get a ten-thousand-dollar check?

E13 On net, they don't. You can't look at the check by itself. You have to look at the taxes that pay for it. The millionaire gets ten thousand dollars and pays four hundred thousand. Most of any tax goes to everything else government does, so count only the share that pays for the checks: everyone above roughly average income pays in more than they get back, and everyone below gets back more than they pay. Net, it's a transfer from richer to poorer. The millionaire's check is just bookkeeping.

E13b And even if you decided the rich shouldn't get the check, how would you stop it? Pick an income cutoff, and everyone just above it falls off a cliff. Phase it out gradually instead, and you've built a higher marginal rate into the middle of the income ladder. Either way, you're back to means testing.

E11b And notice what the check doesn't need. No means test. No caseworkers verifying your income every few months. No paperwork, no penalty for a good month. Means testing is what creates the cliffs in the first place, and it takes a bureaucracy to run. The dividend replaces that patchwork. Stack it on top of the old phase-outs, and you'd keep the cliffs.

E11b1 Max Ghenis of PolicyEngine has a phrase for this: means testing is a poor substitute for taxation. Phasing out a benefit as you earn more is a tax, aimed squarely at the poorest workers. If the goal is for richer people to pay more, the tax system can do that openly, without docking the first extra dollar a struggling family earns.

E11b2 And the old worry that handouts make people lazy? The Congressional Budget Office reviewed the research in twenty twelve. By its central estimates, people respond roughly five times more strongly to how much of the next dollar they get to keep than to simply having more money. So what kills the incentive to work is the high marginal rate built into means testing, far more than the cash itself.

E11c Graduated brackets have a quieter problem. They punish uneven income. Take a simple system: ten percent on your first fifty thousand dollars, thirty percent on anything above that.

E11d Earn fifty thousand this year and fifty thousand next year, and you pay ten thousand dollars in total. Earn a hundred thousand this year and nothing next year, the same money, and you pay twenty thousand. Twice the tax for the same two years of income.

E11e Who earns like that? Freelancers. Farmers. Construction workers. Artists. Anyone who works double shifts one year and cares for a newborn the next. The bracket system charges them extra for the timing of their paychecks, which has nothing to do with how rich they are.

E11f A flat rate with a dividend doesn't care. Earn it all in one year or spread it across two, and over the two years you pay exactly the same.

E15 By the way, if you still object to rich people getting a check, then you should object far more to every price cap and every "don't you dare raise prices." Hold Amazon's prices down, and every millionaire ordering online gets the discount too, with no tax on the other side clawing it back. Same objection. Much worse offender.

E15b Rent control is worse still. In San Francisco, your discount depends on how long you've lived in your apartment and how much rents have risen since, with no check on what you earn. A tenant with a six-figure income who moved in a decade ago can pay a fraction of market rent, while a struggling family that just arrived pays full price.

E16 Milton Friedman, not exactly a socialist, proposed something close to this dividend in nineteen sixty-two. He called it a negative income tax: a payment that shrinks as you earn more. Set the rate it shrinks at equal to the flat tax rate, and it's mathematically the same as a dividend plus a flat tax. Make it shrink much faster than that, and you've rebuilt the cliff.

## 14. close

Z1 So, back to New York.

Z2 If the city wants delivery workers to have more money, it can give them money. Out in the open, where voters can see the price, paid for by taxes that don't block useful trades.

Z3 Instead it hides the bill inside every online order in the city. And when the bill arrives, the mayor calls it greed.

Z4 That's the socialist toolkit in one move. Good intentions, delivered by gift card, paid for by a tax nobody can see, and blamed on someone else.

Z5 So here's our challenge, to anyone who defends rent control because people deserve a home. Fine. Let's take that as given: that's the values question. Now the engineering question. Why would you rather pin them to one apartment than give them the money to choose their own?

Z6 Pick any amount of redistribution you like. Zero, a little, a lot. Whatever it is, cash delivers it cheaper, and the people you meant to help end up with more.

## 14b. a name for it

L1 So what do you call this? That's harder than it sounds.

L2 Neoliberalism has been thrown around as an insult for so long that it barely means anything. Market socialism sounds close, but it still has socialism in the name, and it means something else entirely: worker-owned firms competing in markets. The clearest name might be the one from that Niskanen paper. The free-market welfare state.

L3 And that naming problem is the whole problem in miniature. Without a word for sharing the pie without shrinking it, people assume there are only two teams: the one that wants to help people, and the one that wants markets to work. Most voters never hear the third option named, let alone explained.
