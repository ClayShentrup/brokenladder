# Naive Bundling, the Great Economic Fallacy

clay shentrup2 min read·Dec 20, 2015

--

I’m convinced that one of the most devastating economic fallacies affecting public policy is one that virtually no one is even aware of. I call it “naive bundling”. To describe it, let’s start by looking at a case of *non*-naive (intentional) bundling. Imagine we are asked to vote on a bill which would do two things:

- Build a “Bridge to Nowhere”
- Tax CO2 emissions

The immediately obvious problem is that these are two independent considerations. It’s likely for people to support one provision but not the other. This is related to the concept of [germaneness](http://www.ncsl.org/research/about-state-legislatures/germaneness-requirements.aspx). While germaneness isn’t always a good thing, it *usually* is, and we would expect backlash if such unrelated items were bundled into a single bill.

But what if we fail to independently consider distinct provisions because we *do not even recognize* that they are distinct? This is *naive bundling*.

Consider a bill which institutes a minimum wage, as [Germany did](http://www.bbc.com/news/business-28140594) in the summer of 2014. While this superficially appears like a single provision, it is in fact two distinctly different policies. This is more obvious if we rephrase the bill as follows:

Policy #1: Employees will be subsidized the difference between their hourly wage and the amount of $12 per hour worked.

Policy #2: Employers will be taxed the difference between each employee’s wage and the amount of $12 per hour worked.

This is economically equivalent to instating a $12 per hour minimum wage. But unbundling these policies allows us to consider them independently on their own merits, which is tremendously valuable.

Let’s start with the subsidy outlined in Policy #1. Let’s assume for the sake of argument that most people agree these hard working individuals should make more money, so that they can have a better quality of life and not have to [struggle to put a roof over their heads and food on the table](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P_OsuGL3ouQ).

So then what about Policy #2? It’s effectively a tax on employing people. Considering the myriad of *bad* things we could tax (like carbon emissions), is employing people really the best thing to tax? My point isn’t to cast a verdict on this particular case, but simply to point out that naive bundling prevents us from even *asking that question*. This leads to a massive amount of inefficiency across the global economy.

How might we fix this, short of requiring elected officials to have economics degrees? As a thought experiment, suppose we were to enact a law which would strictly forbid any expenditure to be based on any particular revenue stream. One could only specify a fixed amount, or a function of total revenues, or some combination of the two. But you could not, for instance, stipulate that schools would receive 40% of the revenues generated by a soda tax. I’m curious to hear readers’ thoughts on other approaches.

# The Problem with Price Controls

clay shentrup7 min read·Sep 20, 2018

--

It’s not what you think

“assorted bunch of fruit lot” by [Rajiv Perera](https://unsplash.com/@rajivperera?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral)

First off, what exactly *is* a price control?

> price con·trol
> 
> 
> 
> prīs kənˈtrōl/
> 
> 
> 
> *noun*
> 
> 
> 
> a government regulation establishing a maximum price to be charged for specified goods and services, especially during periods of war or inflation.

Timely examples of price controls include [rent control](http://www.tenantstogether.org/campaigns/rent-control-all) and [minimum wage](https://www.bbc.com/news/business-28140594).

## Concepts

Before we proceed, it’s crucial to establish base concepts. The concepts we need here are *orthogonality* and *equivalent transformation*.

### Orthogonality

Suppose we have a proposed law that does two things:

- Bans liquor sales on Sunday.
- Increases teacher salaries.

These provisions are [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonality#Statistics,_econometrics,_and_economics), meaning they each can (and should) be considered independently. One might support the first provision, but oppose the second, or vice versa.

Suppose Alice supports the increase in teacher salaries, but opposes the Sunday liquor ban. Consider how bizarre it would be to tell her, “You don’t care about teachers!” But if we are *forced* to consider and/or vote on these two separate provisions as a single package, then they could easily *feel* like a single policy, and a criticism of one component might feel like a criticism of the whole package, inviting exactly that sort of reaction.

But if Alice votes yes, that doesn’t mean she supports the liquor ban. And if she votes no, that doesn’t mean she’s against increasing teacher salaries. Her package vote is a function of the relative importance of these two provisions. Really, the right argument should be, why don’t we just unbundle these provisions and vote on them separately to begin with? (Interestingly, it’s actually theoretically possible for it to be better to vote on multiple provisions as a single package.)

Now let’s look at another example. Our new proposed law says:

- We’ll spend an extra 100k/mo. on teacher salaries.
- We‘ll raise an extra 100k/mo. from a sales tax.

Again, these are two *orthogonal* provisions. We might support the salary raise for teachers, but prefer to fund it with an increased income tax, or property tax, or any number of other kinds of tax. It’s hard to imagine a reasonable person attacking us for simply considering more appropriate taxes.

### Hypothecation

It’s also crucial to realize that the orthogonality of these two provisions means we should consider each in complete isolation and avoid any [hypothecation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothecated_tax) (“earmarking”). In other words, when considering how to raise that 100k/mo., it doesn’t matter how it’s going to be spent. Whether it’s spent on teacher salaries or digging ditches, the impact of the tax doesn’t change, and so is irrelevant to the thing we choose to tax.

This works in reverse too: We want to spend the money on whatever maximally improves human welfare. It doesn’t matter how we got the money; we want to spend it as if it appeared in a magic briefcase.

### Equivalent Transformations

One powerful tool for analyzing complex concepts is to use *equivalent transformations*. For example, in circuit analysis we have the [wye-delta transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y-%CE%94_transform). We can debate till the cows come home about the long term *macro* performance of two competing circuit designs, but with respect to whether a particular component is a delta or wye, it makes *absolutely no difference—*these components are *equivalent*.

A wye-delta transformation

Likewise, a price control can be transformed into an equivalent bundled tax and subsidy pair. Take an apartment whose market value is 3000$/mo., but which rent control restricts to 2000$/mo. To the tenant this is equivalent to a 1000$/mo. subsidy, whereas to the landlord it’s equivalent to a 1000$/mo. tax. We can state it in the same policy provision framework we used above:

- The tenant shall receive a 1000$ deposit into his bank account each month, the instant his rent check clears.
- The landlord shall face a 1000$ withdrawal from his bank account each month, the instant the rent check is deposited.

It’s crucial to understand that this really is an *exact equivalence*, just like a wye-delta transformation in circuit design. From the tenant’s perspective, her finances are exactly the same either way; and the same goes for the landlord. So no macro differences can exist.

### What’s the Point?

Okay, so we can perform equality-preserving transformations, but what purpose does this serve? In the case of the wye-delta transform, the purpose is that one version of the component is easier to analyze, e.g. for computing the effective resistance or voltage drop between two nodes.

In the case of price controls, converting to a bundled tax and subsidy allows us to see that what *appears* to be a single policy (e.g. “rent control”) is really *two orthogonal *policies. And knowing this allows us to analyze each of those policies *independently*, just like we did above. We might agree that it’s good to subsidize a tenant, but we can ask, “Why get the money from a tax on the landlord?” We could get the money from a gross receipts tax, or a sales tax, or a wealth tax on the proverbial 1%. So how should we decide where to scrounge up 1000$/mo?

I’ve previously written about the two reasons for a tax. But let’s briefly revisit.

## Tax the Rich

A pervasive theme throughout progressive economic rhetoric is the idea of [taxing the rich](https://www.commondreams.org/views/2018/04/17/why-should-we-tax-rich). The simple justification is that “they can afford it”. In formal economic parlance, we say it a little differently: Money has a [diminishing marginal utility](http://www.mrmoneymustache.com/2012/02/09/brave-new-life/). So the more money you have, the less it hurts you to have a dollar taxed out of your wallet. This means that if we want to dig for funding to cover any government expense, it’s better to take it from a billionaire than a millionaire, and from a millionaire than a thousandaire, and so on.

## Tax Sin

When taxing *people*, we want to tax the richest. But when taxing *activities*, we only want to tax those which are [negative externalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Externality#Negative), e.g. emitting CO2 into the atmosphere. In a nutshell, the point is to make the market function correctly. (There are also [positive externalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Externality#Positive), which are addressed via subsidies rather than taxes.)

Note that I am not making a value judgement here. I understand that there are those who are [ideologically opposed](https://www.cato.org/blog/fundamental-fallacy-redistribution) to the idea of wealth redistribution, but the fact of diminishing marginal utility means that redistribution can obviously improve net human welfare.

## Landlords

Now that we’re equipped with the guidelines for taxation, let’s revisit this tax on landlords. Clearly the tax component of a rent control isn’t a wealth tax, since it doesn’t even take the landlord’s wealth into account. (Yes, we may assume that landlords are *generally* wealthier than their tenants, but so are lots of other entrepreneurs and investors.) And it doesn’t address a negative externality either. So there’s no logical reason to tax landlords, QED. It would make just as much sense to pick a random name in the phone book and tax that person to cover a tenant’s rental subsidy.

It can be helpful to think about food stamps. The government gives some citizens financial assistance to buy food. This subsidy is funded from different sources depending on what state the recipient lives in. But where it *doesn’t* come from is a tax on grocery stores. That is, grocery stores aren’t forced to sell below-market-rate food à la price controls. And generally, this seems to make perfect sense to people. Yet this hypothecation fallacy is strangely pervasive in rental policy discussions. I suspect this is because a) housing takes up a much larger share of a person’s expenses and isn’t discretionary, and b) housing is artificially constrained by [NIMBY policies](https://nextcity.org/daily/entry/ways-affordable-housing-developers-deal-with-nimbys), creating an especially strong price gouging effect. (If there was an analog to zoning laws that restricted the number of loaves of bread that could be produced, people would probably be complaining about having to pay 20$ for a loaf of bread too.)

## Not Getting It

Let’s return to circuits for a moment. Suppose someone argues that, for the sake of human life, some medical device *must* use a delta instead of a wye (or vice versa). We can simply point out that these two components are *equivalent* and can be swapped out *with no consequences whatsoever* to any patient. But suppose someone doubles down and says something like this:

> You want to win a theory battle, when I’m talking about people and lives and families.

The problem here is that the delta-wye equivalence isn’t “theoretical”. It’s not like an economic forecast or a weather forecast. It’s an exact equivalence. That *micro* equivalence results in a *macro* equivalence.

In response to the rent control transformation, I have seen responses along these lines:

> What is wrong with putting a cap on what landlords can charge for rent for a place?

What our transformation shows is that you *cannot* put a cap on what a unit *effectively* rents for. If the market value is 3000$, then the unit effectively rents for 3000$. If you force the landlord to rent it for 2000$, its *effective* rent is still 3000$—it’s just that 1000$ of that is being paid by the landlord instead of the tenant. The economic transformation allows us to see that the question itself is flawed. The *right* question remains, “Why tax the landlord instead of taxing wealth specifically, and/or taxing negative externalities?”

Furthermore, the rental subsidy to the tenant is also flawed in that it’s an example of hypothecation. It would be better to just those tenants money directly, and let them decide whether to spend it on their rent, or on food, or clothes, or whatever they feel most improves their quality of life.

## Conclusion

Price controls *can* in many cases lead to a net improvement in overall human welfare, because their good provisions cause greater benefit than their bad provisions cause harm. This explains how we can see net improvements arising from [minimum wage laws](https://www.bbc.com/news/business-28140594), rent control, and other forms of price control. Nevertheless, we can create *even better* policies by disentangling and improving their individual provisions. If we do this successively, we eventually get to the ideal policy, which is approximately this: taxes on wealth and negative externalities, and subsidies to poverty and positive externalities. If we then factor out disincentives for work, we arrive more or less at a [Universal Basic Income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_income). One subsidy to rule them all.

# Economists Disagree on Instant Runoff Voting

clay shentrup5 min read·Dec 31, 2018

--

The Initiative on Global Markets (IGM) at the University of Chicago Booth School of Business asked a panel of economists the following question about Instant Runoff Voting (aka Ranked Choice Voting).

> We have too many voting impossibility theorems to state flatly that one method is better.
> **Larry Samuelson, Yale**

Criteria, like whether a system is immune from [Arrow’s Theorem](http://scorevoting.net/ArrowThm), are [not a good measure](http://scorevoting.net/PropDiatribe) of voting method performance. It’s kind of like comparing race cars based on horsepower and drag coefficient. Instead you want to put them through timed trials and get an average race speed, which simultaneously incorporates *all* criteria, with the correct frequency-times-severity of various failures (even failures of criteria you never thought to consider). The analog of a race track for voting methods is [Bayesian regret](http://scorevoting.net/BayRegsFig), and there is indeed a clearly [best voting](http://scorevoting.net/UniqBest) method.

> Not a great question to sell this scheme: what exactly does “would be better” mean?
> **Richard Schmalensee, MIT**

The point of making a choice (literally the reason evolution gave you that grey decision-making machine between your ears) is to maximize your expected utility. Ergo the measure of a voting method is the [expected utility it produces](http://scorevoting.net/BayRegsFig). The better the voting method, the lower the Bayesian regret.

> Rather than using second-round runoffs to settle elections in which no candidate wins a first-round majority, it would be better to use ranked-choice voting (as in the state of Maine) in which voters are encouraged to rank all of the candidates.

The answers reveal an astonishing lack of expertise on the subject of voting methods. I address some of them here.

> No voting system is perfect. Conflicts/ambiguities cannot be avoided. But ranked voting is typically better &allows for entry by new parties
> **Daron Acemoglu, MIT**

Actually IRV [maintains two-party domination](http://scorevoting.net/TTRvIRVstats). And of course the fact that no system is perfect is irrelevant to the question of which of two systems is *better*.

> Much voter preference info wasted in dichotomous voting! Rank-choice voting preferable, and could reduce bilateral party monopoly
> **David Autor, MIT**

Again, IRV [maintains two-party domination](http://scorevoting.net/TTRvIRVstats).

> Both are manipulable but they each have different properties
> **Abhijit Banerjee, MIT**

Well, *every* deterministic voting system is manipulable, so this isn’t saying much.

> Saves on the cost of elections, at least.
> **David Cutler, Harvard**

Not that simple. Warren Smith, a Princeton math PhD and world expert on voting systems [explains](http://scorevoting.net/HonestRunoff):

> Yes, one round is cheaper and easier than two, *but* with IRV, that one round is more complicated and it cannot be done on ordinary “dumb totalizing” voting machines, whereas both rounds in delayed runoff can be done with such machines; and IRV is [non-additive](http://scorevoting.net/IrvNonAdd.html) (no such thing as “precinct subtotals”) and [non-monotonic](http://scorevoting.net/Monotone.html); *and* the second round in delayed runoff often does not happen. (Top-two runoff also is non-monotonic, but each of its two rounds, in isolation, of course is monotonic.) In view of those facts, it is not at all clear to us that IRV actually saves money. And in any event, the money spent on elections is negligible compared to other government expenditures, so it is more important to get quality in elections, than to save money. Thus “saving” money would be a false economy that surely would actually cost more in bad government than it saved in election expenditures. For example (2006), a pro-IRV group was recently arguing that Oakland California should switch to IRV because each runoff election under the old delayed-runoff scheme cost Oakland “hundreds of thousands of dollars,” which was their way of saying $200,000. However, they did not mention that Oakland’s annual budget is over $1 *billion* so that the “cost savings” they were lobbying for was of order 0.02% fractionally. Surely there are superior ways to save Oakland’s money! Also they did not mention that it cost (neighboring, comparable size) San Francisco $1,600,000 to upgrade its voting machines to run IRV two years before. So the payback time required to justify this cost “savings,” as you can see, would be very large, perhaps 30–40 years assuming elections every 2 years and runoffs required half the time. Quite probably Oakland would be re-replacing its machines before that time, in which case the costs *never* would be repaid.
> 
> 
> 
> Ranked choice voting is cheaper. Second round runoffs let voters focus on leading candidates. Otherwise they are the same.
> **Robert Shimer**

Regarding costs, see previous response. On the matter of their being the same, this is simply false. In San Francisco’s 2010 District 10 supervisor race, Malia Cohen won with Instant Runoff Voting, despite not being in the top two. I.e. she would not have even made it to the second round of a traditional runoff. These are two distinct algorithms.

> With a run-off, if there are several candidates, the “wrong” two may win the first round. This does not happen with a ranked system.
> **Oliver Hart, Harvard**

Ludicrous. Indeed, there is a ranked method which was used in North Carolina, which simulates a delayed runoff: it eliminates all but the two candidates with the most votes, and chooses the majority preferred between them.

If we restrict “ranked system” to mean only IRV/RCV, then both systems are still identical with three candidates. For example, see these sample preferences:

35% Left > Center > Right
33% Right > Center > Left
32% Center > others

Center is preferred to Left by a 65% landslide majority, and to Right by an even bigger 67% majority — so clearly the strongest candidate. And indeed, a traditional delayed runoff eliminates Center due to her having the fewest votes (if everyone sincerely votes for their favorite candidate in the first round). But IRV/RCV *does the same thing.*

> Current System has worked well for 200+ years. Not a good idea to change.
> **Steven Kaplan, Chicago**

This is a blatant [appeal to tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_tradition). The [vast majority of experts](http://www.lse.ac.uk/website-archive/newsAndMedia/newsArchives/2011/04/VotingSystems.aspx) consider the status quo to be the worst system on Earth, for [good reason](http://scorevoting.net/Plurality.html).

> Yes, but experience has been that it [IRV] is complex and we would need to ensure that it does not lower participation.
> **William Nordhaus, Yale**

Participation is actually a fairly [minor issue](http://scorevoting.net/RelImport).

> This [IRV] seems better than the status quo with 3rd party candidates altering outcomes.I don’t think strategic voting would be a problem.
> **Richard Thaler, Chicago**

But IRV can still allow minor party candidates to alter outcomes. See this short video by Andy Jennings, a co-founder of the Center for Election Science who did his math PhD thesis on voting methods.

And a century of use of IRV in Australia (starting in 1918) suggests that tactical voting is [indeed a problem](http://scorevoting.net/AustralianPol).

# The Allais Paradox: a paper tiger

## How one of the most common arguments against utilitarianism fails

clay shentrup2 min read·Jan 20, 2019

--

Photo by [Robert Anasch](https://unsplash.com/@diesektion?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral)

> The Allais paradox is a choice problem designed by Maurice Allais (1953) to show an inconsistency of actual observed choices with the predictions of expected utility theory.
>  — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allais_paradox)

Suppose you ask someone to choose between two lotteries:

a. 11% chance of 1M, 89% chance of X
b. 10% chance of 5M, 1% chance of 0, 89% chance of X

Regardless of which choice they make, virtually anyone you ask will recognize that the value of X should be irrelevant to the decision. You get an 89% chance of X regardless, so it’s not a differentiator between the two options. This is central to the [independence axiom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_utility_hypothesis#The_von_Neumann-Morgenstern_axioms) of expected utility theory.

But now suppose you specify a value of one million dollars, like so:

1a. 11% chance of 1M, 89% chance of 1M
1b. 10% chance of 5M, 1% chance of 0, 89% chance of 1M

This simplifies to:

**Experiment 1**
1a. Guarantee of 1M
1b. 10% chance of 5M, 1% chance of 0, 89% chance of 1M

*Given this choice, most people will choose 1a.*

But now suppose we set X to 5M instead of 1M. We get:

**Experiment 2**
2a. 11% chance of 1M, 89% chance of 5M
2b. 99% chance of 5M, 1% chance of 0

*Here, most people choose 2b.*

So we have an apparent violation of the independence axiom of expected utility theory.

## Rebuttal

How do we explain this contradictory behavior without rejecting the notion that people are utilitarian? Easy. As we said from the start, both experiments can be generalized by using the variable X. And we find that if phrased that way, people *are* consistent. That is, if you ask their choice, they almost never object that “it depends on the value of X”.

The problem with Allais’s phrasing is that he doesn’t tell the test subjects about the equivalence as a generalized formula with the X variable. And it’s very difficult for people to come to that realization on their own without pretty strong math expertise. Indeed, you can go out of your way to ask most people how the two experiments can be generalized, and I’ll bet you fewer than 30% of adults will be able to readily find that equivalence on their own.

Individual assessments of the two experiments are not rigorous. People are vulnerable to built in mental shortcuts conferred to us by eons of biological evolution, in which accuracy is in competition with time. Take too long deciding between fight or flight, and you’re lion food.

# Exploitation

clay shentrup2 min read·Dec 27, 2019

--

Photo by [Mark Hang Fung So](https://unsplash.com/@17somh1?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral)

One of the central tenants of Marxism is that capitalists “exploit” workers by paying them less than the “full value” of their labor. See this excerpt from the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.

> By far the most influential theory of exploitation ever set forth is that of Karl Marx, who held that workers in a capitalist society are exploited insofar as they are forced to sell their labor power to capitalists for less than the full value of the commodities they produce with their labor.
> [https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/exploitation/](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/exploitation/)

This notion of exploitation is simply an amalgam of multiple distinct economic fallacies.

### Thought Experiment

Imagine Alice trades an apple to Bob for an orange. In order for this transaction to occur voluntarily, both parties must by definition agree that the other item is more valuable than the one they begin with. In other words, Alice thinks the orange is more valuable than the apple, and Bob thinks the apple is more valuable than the orange. This [subjective value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subjective_theory_of_value) is what allows both parties to *simultaneously profit*.

But now suppose instead of trading apples, Alice trades her labor for the orange. Let’s say she sweeps Bob’s porch for the orange. Is this exploitation?

Now let’s go one step further and imagine Alice trades her labor for dollars instead of oranges. Is *this* exploitation?

### A Broken Mental Model

In all of these cases, Alice is ending up with something that is, in *her subjective assessment*, of greater value than she started with. This improvement in her welfare cannot be seen as an offense against her. But what if she is very poor and suffering? Well, then the *actual* problem is that her *initial state* was already one of poverty. The broken Marxist mental model essentially blames the antibiotic for the infection. It blames Bob for not paying her *above* what she’s willing to take (i.e. charity).

### The Solution

If the real problem is that Alice is too poor, then the solution is to *give her money*. This can be accomplished with something like a Negative Income Tax (NIT) or Universal Basic Income (UBI). But note that the source of that additional money, beyond the agreed upon market price of her labor, shouldn’t come from the person buying her labor. It should come from the *general fund*. A tax on the random individual who happens to be buying her labor is suboptimal—buying labor isn’t a negative externality! Instead we want to tax the wealthiest individuals, because it hurts them the least (since wealth has decreasing marginal utility).

# against subsidized housing

clay shentrup5 min read·Apr 30, 2022

--

the correct amount of subsidized housing (or [“affordable” housing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affordable_housing)) is *zero*. there are a number of reasons for this, but here are the most clear and obvious.

- cash transfers are better than subsidies. subsidies create **deadweight loss**.
- housing is not a negative externality. we don’t want to disincentivize it.

i’ll unpack this below, but let’s start with a familiar counter-example: food stamps. when someone uses food stamps to buy groceries, the funding for that comes from ordinary taxes of all shape and size. no one ever argues that it should come from a tax on grocery stores. that is, no one demands that grocery stores be forced to eat the cost of food stamps by simply selling their food below the market rate. if you can at least kind of grok what i’m getting at here, then read on.

## cashism > subsidism

imagine you could have either 100$ cash or a 100$ gift certificate. virtually anyone would take the cash over the gift certificate, as it provides everything the gift certificate provides, and then some.

this can even be quantified by asking how much less you’d be willing to pay for the gift certificate. for instance, if someone offered me a 100$ gift certificate to starbucks, i might be willing to spend 60$ on it. thus if someone were to gift me a starbucks card, there would be 40$ worth of waste, which economists refer to as *deadweight loss*.

another way to think of this loss is to realize that such a gift would lead to overconsumption. i would spend more money at starbucks relative to the scenario in which i’m given cash. this leads to a misallocation of resources. i’m depriving other people who like starbucks of whatever i consume, *or* my added demand drives up the price a bit. true starbucks fans may still be able to get the same quantity, but only if they’re willing to pay slightly more. at the level of the individual, this effect is too tiny to notice, but multiply it by thousands or millions of people, and it adds up to something significant.

### housing too

this same phenomenon applies to housing. imagine you can receive a 1000$ check in the mail every month, or a 1000$ subsidy on your rent *provided* you live at 123 plum avenue. plum avenue might rent for 3000$/mo, but it’s not in the neighborhood you’d ideally pick for yourself, and so you only value it at around 2500$/mo. but given you have this subsidy, the rent artificially feels to you like it’s 2000$/mo—far below your 2500$ estimate. thus the subsidy would cause the same misallocation of resources. someone else might experience a full 3000$ of value out of that unit, but instead you consume only 2500$ worth of value, making for a deadweight loss of 500$/mo.

whereas if we were to simply give you the 1000$ cash, you’d then rent a unit whose market value matches your own personal preferences. no more deadweight loss. goods and services going to the people who actually derive the most utility from them.

## housing is not a negative externality

another huge fallacy in how “affordable housing” is often implemented is that the developer is charged with building a certain number/percentage of “affordable” units. from the point of view of a housing developer, this feels identical to a tax. indeed, many programs allow developers to pay an [in-lieu fee](https://inclusionaryhousing.org/designing-a-policy/off-site-development/in-lieu-fees/) to fund “affordable” units elsewhere.

but taxing developers is profoundly irrational. there are three fundamental types of rational tax:

- **pigovian taxes** taxing negative externalities (e.g. carbon emissions) so as to internalize their cost and rationalize consumer behavior (e.g. stop overconsumption of fossil fuels). the goal is to eliminate deadweight loss.
- **windfall taxes** taxing the *receipt* of *positive* externalities. e.g. a land value tax. the goal here is not to eliminate deadweight loss, but to avoid *causing* deadweight loss, relative to our final category…
- **generated wealth taxes** if we tax the “fruit of our labor”, such as income taxes, we have the upside of creating government revenues that can be used to help society. but we have the downside that we’re decreasing the incentive for people to engage in productive work. thus we want to completely exhaust 100% of our pigovian and windfall opportunities, and then implement a simple [flat percentage tax](https://www.ubicenter.org/us-flat-tax) covering everything else. generated wealth taxes are our *last resort*.

so while it’s fine to tax developers’ profit at whatever the standard rate is set to across our society, it’s profoundly wrong to institute a *higher* rate than we would for the production of other types of goods and services, as this drives investment dollars into producing other goods and services instead of housing. this reduces housing options, i.e. drives up housing costs. yet this is exactly what we’re effectively doing when we demand some percentage of units be rented below market rate.

just like with food stamps, any assistance should come from ordinary taxes across society, rather than a tax on the people who happen to be building/selling the good or service you (ironically) are trying to make more available to more people.

## divisability

the other nice thing about cash is that it’s *divisible*. suppose bob and alice are each in a lottery for a specific subsidized housing unit, and alice wins. now she gets the 1000$/mo subsidy, and bob gets nothing. but suppose we could instead just give each of them 500$. that would create a better net improvement to their welfare due to the [decreasing marginal utility of wealth](https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/072815/what-marginal-utility-income.asp).

that’s not to say that subsidies *cannot* be divided. in principle we can give subsidies to an arbitrary number people. but in practice, so called “affordable housing” tends to be implemented as a quota of specifically earmarked housing units. this creates the aforementioned lottery problem.

## conclusion

the optimal public policy is to:

- optimize the free market to create as much wealth as possible (e.g. houses, food, clothes, etc.)
- optimally redistribute wealth to maximize human welfare, understanding that reducing inequality comes at the cost of disincentivizing wealth creation and thus having less wealth to redistribute, making this a very tricky balance to achieve.

the best way to maximize the production of housing is to [eliminate zoning](https://www.forbes.com/sites/rogervaldez/2019/05/16/zoning-is-a-20th-century-solution-to-a-19th-century-problem-lets-end-it/), and “development taxes” like the subsidized housing requirements or in-lieu fees. the best way to minimize inequality and create a welfare floor is via universal basic income. give people cash, not subsidies.

# progressive taxation is bad

clay shentrup4 min read·Dec 12, 2021

--

imagine you could choose between the two following tax formulas.

- `10% * income — 4000`
- `20% * income — 8000`

if you make 40,000$ salary, both formulas are equivalent: you pay zero in tax. if you make more, then the first formula is preferable. if you make less, the second formula is preferable.

let me restate that clearly: the poor will prefer the *higher* tax rate.

for instance, say you make 39,000$. then your tax “bill” under these two formulas is:

- `10% * 39,000 — 4000 = -100` (i.e. a 100$ refund)
- `20% * 39,000 — 8000 = -200` (i.e. a 200$ refund)

the negative numbers mean you get a refund. this is called a [negative income tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_income_tax) (NIT), and is effectively the same thing as a universal basic income (UBI). clearly someone making below 40,000$ prefers the higher 20% tax rate to the lower 10% tax rate.

but suppose you make 41,000$. then the amounts are:

- `10% * 41,000 — 4000 = 100`
- `20% * 41,000 — 8000 = 200`

these are *positive* values, meaning you pay taxes rather than getting a refund. someone making *above* 40,000$ prefers the lower 10% tax rate to the higher 20% tax rate.

## the poor prefer higher interest rates

we just saw that a 20% tax rate is preferable for poorer people and undesirable for wealthier people, compared to the lower 10% tax rate. this directly contradicts progressive taxation dogma, which says that it’s better for the poor to pay *lower* tax rates.

as we see from these simple examples, there’s more to your tax bill than merely the *percentage rate* you pay. by offsetting that percentage with a *fixed size* “negative income tax”, we can produce a tax system which is progressive in the aggregate, even tho it uses *flat* percentages.

## graphing it

i made a quick approximation of the effect of a flat tax vs progressive tax, with all revenues *in excess of government expenditures* going back evenly to all citizens in the form of the aforementioned *negative income tax*.

**blue** is the real net worth percentiles from the USA, which gives us a realistic sense of inequality regardless of whether we’re speaking in terms of wealth or income. the **red** line is the new distribution if we enact a 40% flat tax. the **yellow** line is where we end up with a progressive tax using a formula of `40% / (1 + (100,000 / net_worth))`; this starts at a 0% rate and gradually increases to 40% at the top percentile.

what jumps out here is that the flat tax (red) is better than the progressive tax (yellow) for everyone under the 48th wealth percentile! that is, the flat tax is more “progressive” than the progressive tax!!! (note that the red and yellow lines almost perfectly overlap, so you’ll want to click on the image to zoom in to get a clear look.)

### simplified

imagine we have 3 people making 0, 200,000, and 1,000,000 per year. with the flat tax at a maximum 100% rate, they each net 400,000 (1.2M/3).

with a progressive tax, the total revenue is 0 + 40,000 + 1,000,000 = 1,040,000, so the nets are 346,667, 426666, and 346,667. so at a certain point, you have a disincentive to work.

## practicality

furthermore, all this is to say nothing of the practical logistical concerns of implementing progressive taxation. for instance, finding all wealth (outsmarting tax avoidance) and objectively assessing assets in order to determine net worth. normal “transaction” taxes, such as income tax, sales tax, and value added tax (VAT) are convenient because they come with an objective market price to which we can simply slap a flat percentage tax. wealthier people will pay more of these taxes in total, merely by virtue of the fact that they consume more goods and services. these types of taxes are far cheaper to administer.

and what about illiquid assets? how much do we tax illiquid holdings in a pre-money venture startup? and how to we extract the money given there’s not yet a market for those assets?

and if we attempt a progressive income tax based purely on income and not net worth, that defeats the entire utilitarian point of progressivity in the first place. total wealth—not merely income—is what has *decreasing marginal utility*.

## conclusion

progressive taxation advocates have their hearts in the right place. but they’re stuck on a basic error of arithmetic, thinking only in terms of the percentage rate, while ignoring the power of a negative constant in the formula.

thanks to [@wvaughn3](http://twitter.com/wvaughn3) for suggesting a better progressive tax formula than the exponential one I initially employed.

this piece focuses on pure math. i highly suggest this [much deeper dive](https://www.ubicenter.org/us-flat-tax) if you’re looking for more empirical comparative international data about the long-term effects of progressivity.

# carbon tax vs cap-and-trade

clay shentrup3 min read·Dec 21, 2022

--

in order to compare a carbon tax to cap-and-trade, we must first describe what a [negative externality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Externality) is.

imagine a single unit of two products or services, X and Y.

X costs 2$ and Y costs 3$.

alice values X and Y both at 4$.

thus if she buys X, that increases GDP by 1$ more than if she buys Y. (note, this ignores the sellers’ subjective value for the sake of simplicity.)

and if she buys X, she personally earns that 2$ of profit. whereas if she buys Y, she earns only 1$ of profit. so she’s incentivized to make the purchase that *also* maximizes GDP. she buys X.

but suppose the producers of Y find a way to transfer 2$ of the cost to the public in the form of a negative externality, switching the costs to 1$ for Y, so now the relative costs are 2$ for X and 1$ for Y.

thus if she buys X, she earns 2$ of profit. whereas if she buys Y, she earns 3$ of profit. so *now* she’s incentivized to buy Y, and reduce GDP by 1$ relative to where it would have been if she’d bought X. we have [deadweight loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deadweight_loss). that is, GDP is lower than it could be.

## pigou

but what if we add a tax to Y in the exact amount of that negative externality of 2$? we thus make Y cost 3$ again, so she buys X, and GDP is maximized.

this is the point of [*pigovian taxation*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pigouvian_tax). to optimize GDP.

## caps

another way to get this same behavior would be to set production limits on X and Y, such that the same amount would be produced (and the same market price would be set) as if we’d implemented the tax. we’d be back to optimal GDP just the same. but the problem with that idea is that we don’t know what that optimal cap *would be*. the only way to know would be to add the tax and see what amount the market produces. in which case, we might as well just set the tax and let the market *objectively* determine the output that would flow from that price.

## the cost uncertainty fallacy

it’s common to see an objection like this.

the fallacy here is that in order to calculate the correct cap, you’d do the following:

- estimate the correct SCC (social cost of carbon).
- estimate how much carbon the economy would emit at that cost.

observe that step 2 is an *additional* source of estimation error above and beyond the SCC estimation error that you’d get with a carbon tax. this is strictly worse.

## two ceilings

a great point raised by this reddit user, [M4mb0](https://www.reddit.com/user/M4mb0/), however is that the *effective* cost of a negative externality is whichever is lower between either its real cost *or* the cost to address it. so if the cost of carbon sequestration is lower than the SCC, we can just use the sequestration price. which is radically easier to determine with certainty. some estimates put the SCC around 180$ per ton, while the cost of carbon sequestration using current technologies can range from 50$ to 100$ per ton of CO2 captured and stored. this is remarkable, because it means objections that it’s too hard to measure the SCC evaporate with this knowledge.

## hypothecation

it is generally incorrect to associate a specific income source with a particular expenditure, known as “hypothecation.” for example, even if the social cost of carbon (SCC) is $50 and the cost of sequestration is $100, it would not be cost-effective to use carbon tax revenue specifically for sequestration. instead, the money should be added to the general fund.

but in the case where the SCC exceeds the sequestration cost, we actually get alpha (excess returns) through sequestration. in this example, we spend 50$ to get 100$ worth of value.

## conclusion

cap and trade is brain dead stupid. just set correct prices via a pigovian tax.

# cost

clay shentrup3 min read·Jan 23, 2023

--

“cost” is defined as *an amount that has to be paid or spent to buy or obtain something*.

suppose you can buy a cookie at the local bakery for 3$. the cost of that cookie is thus, by definition, 3$. that’s what you pay to obtain it.

but what if you *steal* it? what is the cost of that *negative externality?* of course, it’s still 3$. the cost has nothing to do with whether you purchase it or steal it. **internal vs external is orthogonal to cost.**

but what about the cost of causing harm with no nominal price? for instance, what if you punch someone? in principle, it’s no different. the *cost* is whatever the person would charge you to voluntarily undergo that treatment. **you would technically be “obtaining” the right to punch them in the face, so the “price spent to obtain something” definition still applies.**

**also note that it doesn’t matter whatsoever what the *reason* is for someone to accept a given price.** suppose for instance that you key someone’s car. the cost for a local auto body shop to repair it, plus the cost of your time, is 200$. by this standard, the cost of the damage is 200$. but now suppose they tell you they’d accept 100$. what this means is that they would just pocket the money and not fix the damage, because there’s something else out there they’d rather have then an unblemished paint job.

but *now* suppose a new auto body technology is invented that can fix the scratch for merely 50$. in that case, the cost becomes only 50$. you might intuitively say, “wait, the cost is the *harm* you’ve done, not the price to fix it.” but remember, the cost is just the price at which that person would sell you the right to cause that harm. if it can be fixed for 50$, then this is the price at which you could obtain the right to key the car. and we can *prove* this via revealed preference. we could simply ask this person, “how much would you accept to let me key your car?” the answer would be 50$.

**again, cost is just whatever price someone would charge you to let you obtain something from them. this is the *definition* of cost.**

## carbon

so then, what is the social cost of a negative externality such as carbon emissions? it is simply the sum of the cost to each individual. and as we just observed, the cost to an individual is just the amount of money that individual would charge you for you to *obtain* the right to subject them to a given amount of harm.

for instance, imagine i asked you how much i’d have to pay you for you to agree to let me release a 8 billion tons of carbon into the atmosphere. you say, “100 dollars”. then the cost *to you* of releasing that carbon is—by definition—100 dollars. the social cost would then just be the sum of that answer for every individual on earth.

### sequestration

but now suppose a carbon sequestration technology is invented that removes carbon from the atmosphere for only 50$ per ton. this means that the cost to you of releasing that carbon is now merely 50$, not 100$. because you can just agree to have the government tax you 50$ to remove all that carbon. (8 billion tons times 50$ per ton, divided by 8 billion people in order to get your individual share of the cost.)

this is *exactly* like the auto body shop that can fix your keyed car for 50$. this new technology changes the price someone has to pay you to obtain the right to emit carbon that affects them.

## conclusion

- internal vs external is orthogonal to cost. so the “social cost of carbon” (SCC) has nothing to do with the fact that it’s an externality.
- the cost of causing harm to someone is just the price they’d accept for allowing that harm. (you “obtain” the right to harm them.)
- it doesn’t matter whatsoever what the *reason* is for someone to accept a given price. you might accept a given price to be harmed by climate change because you live somewhere only mildly affected by it. or you might accept that price because it’s the cost to sequester that carbon back out of the air, thus neutralizing the harm. but the reason doesn’t matter. it’s an irrelevant implementation detail. all that matters is the price you’ll accept.

classical liberalism, which emerged in the 18th and 19th centuries, emphasizes the importance of individual liberty and limited government intervention in the economy. it advocates for free market capitalism, minimal government regulation, and a laissez-faire approach to economic policy.

neoliberalism, on the other hand, emerged in the 20th century as a response to the perceived failures of classical liberalism. it also emphasizes the importance of individual liberty and limited government intervention, but it also acknowledges that markets can create distributional problems that need to be addressed. neoliberalism advocates for market efficiency, and advocates for the use of market-based solutions to solve societal problems.

in practice, classical liberalism is more focused on the ideals of individual liberty, minimal government intervention and free market capitalism, while neoliberalism is more focused on the practical application of these ideals, with a focus on market-based solutions and economic growth.

additionally, classical liberalism is more skeptical of government intervention, while neoliberalism is more open to government intervention as long as it is done in a way that promotes economic growth and efficiency.

it's worth noting that the term 'neoliberalism' has been used in different ways over time, and it has also been used to describe a wide range of economic and political policies, so it's meaning can be somewhat ambiguous.

# shorting

clay shentrup2 min read·Jun 26, 2022

--

in a traditional short sell, or *short* for…short, you borrow an asset that you believe will decline in value, sell it, then buy it back before it’s due to be returned. for instance, suppose acme stock is at 2300 and you believe it will be at 2000 in one month. you borrow x shares and sell them. then you buy them back in a month and pay back the shares, pocketing the difference if you’re right, or potentially losing money if you’re wrong. note that you also pay a fee anywhere from 0.3% to 3% per year for borrowing the stock.

## #math

this can be numerically simplified so that you don’t even have to *buy* any shares. imagine the owner of the shares simply agrees to pay you `p - a`in one month, where `p` is his predicted price and `a` is the actual price a month from now (plus the aforementioned fee of course). so supposing the owner thinks that the asset will be at 2400 in a month, and you believe it’ll be at 2000. then if you’re right, the owner pays you `2400 - 2000 = 400`.

what’s really interesting is that the other party need not even *own* any shares! any two individuals could simply gamble on the future price in this way.

### keeping the asset

but what if you want to short the asset and *keep it?* in other words, bob is going to give you the asset right now, and you’re going to pay him some amount in the future. what should that amount be? it turns out it’s just `2p - a`. if you’re right, you’ve effectively purchased the asset for `4000 - 2400 = 1600`, pocketing the 400 savings off its actual market value of 2000. whereas if bob’s right, you’ve purchased the asset for `4800 - 2400 = 2400`, which means bob’s lost nothing (you paid him exactly what he thought it was worth) and you’ve gained nothing (save for the shorting fees just like in a normal short.)

# Effective Economic Policy

clay shentrup5 min read·Oct 9, 2017

--

In the late 2000s, an ethical system emerged known as [effective altruism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_altruism) (EA), described as “a philosophy and social movement that applies evidence and reason to determine the most effective ways to benefit others.” Effectiveness is judged in essentially [utilitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism) terms. For instance, GiveWell is a non-profit which [recommends charities](https://www.givewell.org/charities/top-charities) “according to how much good *additional* donations can do.” Their top charity, the Against Malaria Foundation, says:

> Insecticide-treated nets prevent deaths and many other non-fatal cases of malaria and are relatively inexpensive — about $5 per net.

The core EA principle of scientifically achieving the utilitarian ideal of most possible good can be applied to a variety of fields. A specific one that comes to mind is economic policy. I therefore propose the economic policy analog: **Effective Economic Policy**. Some background follows.

## Externalities

Suppose we have two two companies, Acme and Bantam. Acme pumps CO2 into the air as part of its business, at a unit profit of 1€ for every -2€ of negative societal impact (i.e. climate change). For Bantam, the unit profit is 3€. As policy makers, we want Bantam’s activities but not Acme’s. Acme’s activities produce a net loss, whereas Bantam’s produce a net profit.

The way to solve this is to tax CO2 emissions such that companies are forced to pay the true full cost of their activities. This is called “internalizing” the externality. If Acme has to pay this cost, they‘ll cease this particular activity because their unit profit will then be -1€, reflecting the true costs that were previously borne by society. That is, they won’t act in a way that loses money. Whereas Bantam will still see a unit profit of 1€. Their business model is good for society because it causes more benefit than harm.

A common misunderstanding of such Pigovian taxes is that they’re meant to stop activities such as CO2 emissions. But as we see, that’s incorrect. We only want to [stop activities that don’t make economic sense](https://www.coursera.org/learn/microeconomics/lecture/NTHBQ/5-1-negative-externalities). We want to kill Acme, not Bantam.

CO2 emissions are a negative externality, but the opposite is a positive externality. Suppose Acme has the ability to create 2€ of public benefit at a cost of 1€. We’d ideally like them to do that, but it doesn’t make economic sense for them if they can’t capture that public benefit. Thus it could be beneficial to subsidize them by 2€ in order to spur them to create that net societal benefit.

**Key point:** you address a negative externality with a tax, and a positive externality with a subsidy.

### Hypothecation

It shouldn’t escape the reader’s attention that when taxing to correct a negative externality, the government ends up holding the tax proceeds. But that was never the *point* of the tax. The point was merely to make the market more efficient by making net-negative activities unappealing. The tax revenue generated is merely a coincidence. But regardless, we must then ask how the money should be spent.

There’s a tendency for people to intuitively expect (or demand) this money be spent rectifying the negative externality — in this case mitigating the harm of CO2 emissions. But herein lies a pervasive economic fallacy. The rational utilitarian approach would be to ignore the source of any revenue, and spend it on whatever does the most good. Treat it as if an anonymous philanthropist handed it to your government in a briefcase. This **briefcase rule** forms one of the core tenets of Effective Economic Policy.

But even if we carefully search for the highest-impact expenditure, it’s still a fallacy to link a specific revenue source to a specific expenditure. This is known as [hypothecation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothecated_tax). Here’s the problem. Suppose we have two revenue streams going to address Issue X and Issue Y, and we’ve decided that X is more important than Y. If the tax that funds our response to Issue X decreases, then we’ll decrease funds to X instead of Y, even though we believe that X is of greater importance. **Therefore Effective Economic Policy requires a prioritization mechanism, and a prohibition against specifying the funding of any expenditure as a function of anything other than total revenues. **Hypothecation should be outlawed.

## Wealth

We just covered one reason for taxes: improving net economic output. The second reason for a tax stems from the fact that money has a decreasing marginal utility. That is, the wealthier you are, the less each additional dollar helps you. So if you transfer a thousand dollars from Warren Buffett to a poor person, you can increase net welfare. Because that transaction causes less harm to Warren Buffett than it causes benefit to the poor person. So the **second reason to tax** is simply in order to improve net welfare directly via redistribution (being careful, of course, of how much we distort incentives).

These are the **two reasons** to tax or subsidize anything.

## Practical Application

These core principles give us a rational lens through which to evaluate policy. One interesting example is the [corporate income tax](https://www.nytimes.com/2014/01/06/opinion/abolish-the-corporate-income-tax.html). This tax cannot be justified on either of the grounds we just established. It can’t possibly address an externality, because it isn’t tied to any specific activity. And it can’t be a tax on wealth, because it applies equally to all shareholders (on a per-share basis) regardless of their wealth. Shares owned by blue collar pensioners are taxed just the same as shares owned by Warren Buffett.

### Liberal Sacred Cows

It follows from some of these core tenets that we must critically evaluate liberal sacred cows like rent control and minimum wage laws. In certain circumstances these policies may be preferable to the status quo, but they contain substantial flaws. Both policies hypothecate; they link a tax and a subsidy. E.g. in the case of rent control, we effectively tax the landlord and subsidize the tenet.

In addition, both policies fail our two tests. E.g. the effective tax paid by a landlord under rent control is not tied to any specific activity (being a landlord is not a negative externality), and it has nothing to do with his personal wealth.

Effective Economic Policy also holds important lessons for two other en vogue policies. It seems to support a [Universal Basic Income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_income), while deprecating the idea of a Land Value Tax.

## Vision

My hope is to get these ideas circulating in the Effective Thinking circles and to possibly advance a simple microeconomic policy framework to that might make the rounds in à la Modern Monetary Theory. I enjoy feedback.

The fee is expected to generate between 200$ and 300$ million annually for subsidized housing projects. This contains multiple fallacies. It treats real estate transactions as a negative externality equal to 75$, which I’ve seen no justification for. And it hypothecates this tax to fund a specific thing: subsidized housing.

# tickets

clay shentrup10 min read·Sep 3, 2024

--

here’s something i wrote and sent to my management when i was a software engineer at live nation in april of 2008, prior to their merger with ticketmaster.

Revolutionizing the Ticketing Industry

How Live Nation could sell out every show, eliminate the economic incentive in ticket scalping, even out spikey site loads, and increase average customer satisfaction, all by utilizing the same economic concepts that drove **Google Adwords** to legendary success

(See an explanation by Google’s Ivy League economic luminaries here: [http://video.google.com/videoplay?docid=126302100433476950)](http://video.google.com/videoplay?docid=126302100433476950))

**Game Theory and Economics**

William Spencer Vickrey was a Columbia University professor who won the Nobel Prize in economics, three days before his death on October 11, 1996. He authored the seminal 1961 Journal of Finance paper, “Counterspeculation, auctions and competitive sealed tenders.”, which was the first instance of an economist using the tools of game theory to understand auctions. The paper dealt with the underpinnings of auction theory, and suggested a simple yet revolutionary auction system called the “second-price auction” (also called the Vickrey auction, after him), in which the highest bidder wins, but only pays what the second-highest bidder bid. Here is why that is so brilliant.

Imagine we were selling tickets by asking customers to place a bid, knowing that the *n* available tickets for any given seating tier would go to the *n* highest bidders. A potential buyer for whom a ticket is worth $50 dollars could artificially bid, say, $40. If he doesn’t get a ticket, he gains nothing, but also spends nothing. His *expected value* is zero. But if he does get lucky enough to get a ticket, he then has spent $40 for something that is worth $50 to him. This means that in an economic sense, the artificially low bid has a *positive expected value*, and is strategically advisable. It also means there is an exceptional incentive for e.g. ticket brokers (a.k.a. scalping agencies) to place numerous artificially low bids, knowing that every time they win, they make a profit.

In a traditional “English auction”, this problem is addressed by allowing participants to know what other participants are bidding. Say that Bob thinks a Coldplay ticket is worth $50, and Alice thinks it is worth $60. Bidding begins at some amount, and then Bob and Alice play leap-frog, progressing in whatever increments they have the patience for (theoretically, two cents at a time), until the bid is greater than $50. Alice then gets the ticket, that is really worth $60 dollars to her, for $50.

A second-price “Vickrey” auction accomplishes the exact same result, except without the cost in time and labor of a real auction. Bob and Alice, and the other participants, simply write down their real bids in sealed envelopes (or on a form on a web site), and when bidding stops, Alice wins but only pays what Bob bid. This saved the hassle of the English auction, but also prevented the strategic “gaming the system” that we saw with the first-place-bid system above. For instance, there is no reason for Alice (or a scalper) to under-bid, because she’ll only have to pay the second highest price anyway.

**What About the House’s Bottom Line?**

Many would look at this scenario as sub-optimal for the auction house. If only they had just put a sticker price of $60 on the ticket, Alice would have bought it, and they would have increased their profit. But there are two reasons this isn’t a problem.

For one, whenever the number of bidders is large, it’s a sure bet that the next-highest bidder wouldn’t be $10 behind, but more like 20 cents. Imagine 1000 people were bidding for the ticket — certainly someone would have bet just shy of Alice’s $60. Realistically, she’d probably end up paying something like $59.93. Or if you’re really talking about *thousands* of bidders, $59.99. So the house isn’t loosing much, especially again considering the time-labor savings of avoiding an English auction.

Secondly, we have to consider all the money the house *loses* if they decide to guess the best selling price on a volume of many items, and as a consequence of trying to be sure to set the price high enough, they lose some sales. In the concert business, we call this “empty seats”. We don’t want rotting bananas in our stands because we asked a dollar more for them than someone out there was willing to pay.

**Being First In Line**

In a system where winning is all about being first in line, it is a natural unavoidable consequence that buyers will flood the sales floor the moment the doors open. In a brick and mortar establishment this poses staffing problems. In an online environment those huge spikes in traffic pose a unique challenge, and require us to spend 100% of the time equipped with resources that we only need perhaps 1% of the time.

In a system where winning is all about bidding the most, this abominable engineering challenge is slain. Instead of tickets for a show going on sale June 1, they go on auction May 20, and the auction *closes* on June 1. No need to rush, except in the edge case that the show will probably sell out, and you assume your bid will be tied with the lowest successful bid. But in that case, a lottery tie-breaker can eliminate even *that* early bird incentive.

**Selling Out Every Show**

For most reputable acts playing an appropriately-sized venue, it’s entirely conceivable that there is some low-enough price at which the show would sell out. But as just discussed, if a ticket vendor were to sell tickets at that price, the loss incurred would be greater than the loss incurred by failing to sell all tickets at a higher price. Vickrey auctions provide the best of both worlds, by selling as many tickets as would be sold if the tickets were free, but selling them all at just a little less than what each concert-goer thought they were worth. Let’s contemplate that. Every single ticket-buyer gets a ticket for as much, or *less*, than he thinks it is worth, and we sell as many tickets as could be sold for free. That point serves as a nice segue into an economic discussion of utilitarianism.

**Customer Satisfaction — An Issue of Utility Theory**

One of the more esoteric facets of economics is the concept of utility, a measure of real subjective value that transcends strict monetary measures. Utility has been defined by various thinkers (e.g. Jeremy Bentham) as satisfaction, happiness, pleasure, etc. The difference between monetary value and utility can be demonstrated by the fact that a fine has a smaller deterrent effect to someone with greater disposable wealth, whereas jail time or community service has a more equitable nature.

Alternatively, for any two people with congruent disposable wealth, the one who is willing to spend more for some product demonstrably *values* it more. This has enormous consequences for average customer satisfaction in instances in which demand exceeds supply. For instance, imagine our Coldplay show, in which tickets sell for $50, and Bob won the “click page-refresh furiously” lottery, and Alice did not. Bob, by sheer dumb luck, got to see a show that Alice would have gotten more enjoyment from. If we implement a system in which Alice would have instead gotten this ticket by paying more (because it really mattered that much to her), then Bob will be compensated for missing out on Coldplay when he comes to our site looking to buy tickets for his favorite band in the world. Instead of his success or failure being largely the product of randomness, so that the buyer often gets tickets to shows he “sort of” wants to see, but fails to get tickets to the shows he *really* wants to see, he will now be most likely to get the tickets he *most wants*. This explains a certain sense of satisfaction I’ve personally experienced after buying an expensive scalped ticket to a show that sold out in minutes. I got to see a show that I passionately wanted to see, because I was willing to prove my enthusiasm with my wallet. If you multiply this effect by the millions of fans out there, this is an absolutely enormous increase in the total/average satisfaction of our customer base. And our ability to generate the maximum possible revenue will make even the venues we do not own want to come solely to us for their ticketing needs. At least, that’s my inexpert prediction.

**What About Artist Backlash?**

One of the most predictable results of implementing this kind of policy is that those numerous musicians for whom the art matters more than the business (I count myself among them), will complain that we are “gouging” the fans. They might argue, so *what* if zillionaire X got to see Coldplay because he genuinely did like them more passionately than zillionaire Y?! What about all those *non-zillionaires* out there? This is where we would need to give the artist a gentle economics lesson, as well as a concession of sorts.

See, if there are lots of people who wanted to see a show and didn’t get to, that simply means there is more demand than supply. More people want to go see U2 than there are copies of U2. We only get one Bono, unfortunately. Limited supply is just an inescapable fact of economics, and if the artist is really so bothered by it, then he needs to play more shows (or become less popular).

The reality that artists really need to look at is that when demand exceeds supply, and tickets are priced below market value (to ensure that the kids can go see their heroes), the market demands that scalpers fill their niche. And they do. And stopping them as long as there is an economic incentive for their trade is a monstrous engineering challenge of its own. And what’s even more pernicious about scalpers is that they both take money that should belong to the artist and the legitimate ticket broker, and *waste* money. They waste money because it takes money for them to operate, but they are just re-selling tickets for a higher cost — which we could have done in the first place, and at least given it to the artist or even a charity of the artist’s choosing. This whole racket would make an economist want to tear hi hair out. We have to educate artists into understanding that wasted money doesn’t do anyone any good. It’s hugely, vastly, *incomprehensibly* in our best interest (and theirs, and the fans’) to do that. We are talking about billions of dollars worth of sales and customer satisfaction that is otherwise being wasted.

**Auction-Lottery Combination**

Still, many artists will feel at least some seats should be available to devoted fans who don’t happen to be rich. This is an unfortunate kind of naïveté, because what they don’t realize is that if little Timmy lucks his way into a ticket that costs him $40 dollars, but is actually worth $200 to him (even if he doesn’t have $200), then he will have every reason to scalp that ticket (e.g. on Craigslist) if he can get more than $200 for it. And he *will* be able to do that if he was allowed to buy a ticket for less than someone else would have paid for it. You just can’t beat the market. My friend Milton Friedman taught me that much.

Admittedly there will be artists who simply won’t be able to grasp this, or just won’t want to because it violates their altruistic leanings. And to a certain degree, it’s essential to our artist relationships and company image that our system assuage their fears that we might be another Ticketmaster, trying to bleed them for every last penny (literally). The “solution” is a hybrid system in which a certain number, *S*, of seats are set aside at a set price, *P*, (e.g. $30) for a lottery among people who bid at least *P*. All lottery winners are limited to buying a maximum of, say, 4 tickets, and the person whose name is on the credit card must show up at will call, and actually show the card and an ID to get the tickets, and must either go directly from there into the venue, or re-present his ID when he does. [This actually happened at a Coldplay show I tried to see at the Fillmore a few years back. This is an additional step to help prevent scalping, although the worst that could happen with this system, even if scalping were unchecked, is what would happen if we implemented the system identically, except auctioned 100% of the tickets with no lottery at all.]

These lottery contestants could be ordinary ticket-buyers, or even members of the artist’s fan club, like the massive one that Pearl Jam maintains. The people who end up winning these tickets may have bid $30 or $30.01… or *$400*. That may violate the spirit of wanting to give these tickets to less wealthy people, but if we try to make a cutoff point, so that the lottery only applies to bidders within a relatively low range, then we simply give wealthier people an incentive to place artificial low bids. Strategic/insincere low bidding is bad, so we have to just make the lottery blind to bid size (so long as the bid at least meets the minimum, *P*). The idea is that the probability dictates something like half of the lottery tickets will go to non-rich fans, and so we simply reserve around twice as many seats as the artist (and our P.R. advisors) want to make available for them, knowing that sometimes more will go to the non-rich fans, and other times more will go to the rich fans — this is more of that troublesomely inescapable economics stuff.

Some implementation notes are:

- Lottery winners’ bids must be removed from the bid pool. (I.e. if bids exist for $20, $25, and $30, and the $25 bidder wins the lottery, then if the $30 bidder wins a normal auction ticket, he only pays $20 for it, not $25 — this is mathematically necessary for the system to work.)
- Seats should *never* go empty, so customers should be free to bid e.g. 10 cents if they want (or whatever minimum is necessary for us to break even on their bandwidth usage from our site, and the show cleanup/usher costs they incur), and if there are seats for them, they get to go — courtesy of Live Nation, the music company they now worship, even though we are just giving them a product that we were going to “throw out” anyway.
- That scheme could be utilized by giving concert-goers “frequent rocker miles”, which they could use to bid on shows, so that potentially wasted empty seats are at least used to build fan loyalty.
- This entire scheme requires re-training the minds of artists and fans, but it seems to me to be a hugely economically advantageous undertaking, that could, with some skill, be spun into a narrative of our revolutionary departure from the old school “Ticketmaster” approach

Hopefully you have found this diatribe stimulating. It was great getting a chance to talk with you about your college days and your family this past December. I look forward to hearing your thoughts should any of the ideas I’ve presented here strike you as remotely viable.

# ubi and inflation: a matter of consistency

clay shentrup2 min read·Sep 3, 2024

--

universal basic income (u.b.i.) sparks debates, with a common concern being its potential to cause inflation. critics worry that u.b.i. recipients will reduce their work hours or stop working altogether, driving up labor costs and triggering price hikes. however, this argument often lacks logical consistency when it comes to how we view u.b.i. spending.

## the berry picker and the bread buyer

imagine a world with u.b.i.. critics fear berry pickers, now financially secure, might work less, leading to labor shortages and pricier berries. this seems plausible, but let’s consider another scenario: someone uses their u.b.i. to buy bread. this increases demand for bread, nudging its price up. this is standard [demand-pull inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demand-pull_inflation) (not to be confused with [cost-push inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost-push_inflation), which is the bad kind of inflation, which means *decreased supply*). but you don’t generally hear of people complaining u.b.i. pushing up bread or clothing prices. people might complain about the *cost* of ubi and the taxes that go into funding it, but not that it be literally used for its intended purpose.

## the logical disconnect

here’s the key: whether you buy bread, hire a handyman, or choose to work less due to u.b.i., you’re increasing demand for something. increased demand raises prices, whether it’s for goods or labor. the inconsistency arises when we criticize u.b.i. for potentially inflating labor costs while accepting inflation caused by u.b.i. spending on goods. it’s like saying, "it’s okay if u.b.i. makes bread more expensive, but it’s unacceptable if it makes labor more expensive." this distinction lacks logical grounding.

## working less is like buying your own time

think of it this way: when you choose to work less because of u.b.i., you’re effectively "buying" your own time. just as buying bread increases demand for bread, "buying" your time increases demand for labor, potentially leading to higher wages and thus greater costs for your berries. but there’s no reason it’s okay to buy someone else’s time but bad to buy your own time. chatGPT agrees.

## conclusion: u.b.i.’s impact on inflation is complex

u.b.i.’s impact on inflation is nuanced and depends on many factors. it’s crucial to remember that people are motivated by more than just money. many find fulfillment in their work, and u.b.i. could even enable them to pursue more meaningful careers. and this could *reduce* inflation of the [cost push variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost-push_inflation) — i.e. the kind of inflation that’s actually bad.

but the core argument isn’t about whether u.b.i. will increase or decrease net inflation; it’s about applying consistent logic. if we’re comfortable with u.b.i. recipients using their income to buy goods and services, leading to potential price increases, we should also accept the possibility of u.b.i. influencing labor markets and wages. it’s all part of the same economic picture.

for instance, a person whose gross income is 40,000$ pays 15% of their income in taxes. whereas someone who earns a million pays 39%. this sure does look [progressive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_tax), doesn’t it?

## surprise

but what if i told you this table represented a *flat* marginal tax rate of 40% for all individuals, combined with a 10,000$ refundable tax credit. (“refundable” means that you get the difference back as a refund if it’s greater than your amount owed in taxes.)

what this example illustrates is the distinction between *marginal* tax rates and *effective* tax rates. this structure gives us the benefits of flat taxes, such as administrative simplicity and time-neutrality (e.g. you shouldn’t pay more taxes making 200,000$ in year one and zero in year two than you would making 100,000$ in both years), while also retaining the social aim of reducing inequality — which is beneficial due to the decreasing marginal utility of consumption.

# the gift card fallacy: misunderstanding value in economic transactions

clay shentrup3 min read·Dec 1, 2024

--

Photo by [Intenza Fitness](https://unsplash.com/@intenzafitness?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral)

gifts are a cornerstone of human relationships, but when it comes to economics, they can create inefficiencies. this “gift card fallacy” occurs when the giver spends resources on a gift that the receiver values less than its cost. understanding this concept can help us make smarter decisions about how we give.

## what is the gift card fallacy?

the gift card fallacy highlights a common inefficiency: resources are allocated in a way that doesn’t maximize value. for example, when you purchase a sweater for someone that costs 50€, but they would only pay 30€ for it, the extra 20€ is “wasted” in terms of economic utility. economists refer to this mismatch between cost and value as “dead weight loss.” the gift creates less happiness than its monetary cost, leading to inefficiency.

## the gift card fallacy in public policy

this isn’t to say that gifts are inherently bad. the emotional and symbolic value of gifts can be significant in personal relationships. however such sentimentality has no place in social welfare programs. consider in-kind government benefits, like food boxes, public housing, or energy subsidies. while these programs are intended to help, they make assumptions about what recipients most want. a family receiving food they don’t like or being tied to a lottery-assigned subsidized apartment far from their friends or family finds less value in these benefits than the government spent to provide them. the result is a mismatch between resources allocated and utility gained. the effect is the same as setting useful goods on fire.

## why cash transfers are the solution

cash transfers sidestep the gift card fallacy entirely. unlike in-kind benefits, cash empowers recipients to prioritize their unique needs and maximize the value they receive.

governments don’t need to guess what people need; individuals know best how to spend money to improve their lives. research consistently shows that cash transfers lead to better outcomes: recipients use the money for essential goods, services, and investments tailored to their circumstances.

not only are cash transfers more efficient, but they also uphold dignity by treating recipients as capable decision-makers rather than passive beneficiaries.

## conclusion

the gift card fallacy reminds us that good intentions can lead to wasteful outcomes if we fail to account for the differences between cost and value. while gifts might make sense in personal contexts for their emotional significance, governments have no reason to make this mistake in their social safety nets. by providing cash transfers instead of in-kind benefits, we can ensure that public resources are used in ways that truly support those in need.

# Market Equitism: A New Approach to Freedom and Equality

clay shentrup3 min read·Nov 2, 2024

--

Economic inequality is one of the most pressing challenges of our time. The gap between the rich and the poor has been widening in many countries, leading to social unrest, political polarization, and human suffering. How can we address this problem without sacrificing economic efficiency, innovation, and growth?

One possible answer is market equitism, a philosophy that aims to balance the goals of productive market economies and fighting inequality within a comprehensive system. Market equitists believe that efficient markets maximize the wealth available to society, and that freedom in all its forms should be maximized while reducing inequality as much as possible — but, crucially, *without* creating [deadweight loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deadweight_loss). This notion of freedom also recognizes that true freedom encompasses not just economic freedom, but also personal liberties like free speech and human rights.

## The Two Pillars of Market Equitism

## 1. Maximizing Market Efficiency

Market failures are situations where free exchange fails to allocate resources efficiently. These arise from:

- Negative externalities (like pollution)
- Monopoly power
- Non-excludable goods (public and common goods)
- Asymmetric information

Market equitists argue that addressing these failures actually makes markets more free by enabling efficient voluntary exchange. They support:

- Carbon taxes to internalize environmental costs
- Antitrust laws to prevent monopolistic restrictions on trade
- Public funding for non-excludable goods
- Regulation of information asymmetries

Importantly, this isn’t anti-freedom — it’s pro-freedom. Well-designed regulation enables rather than restricts free exchange.

## 2. Redistributing Without Deadweight Loss

Market equitists recognize that even perfectly efficient markets may produce unfair outcomes due to luck, inheritance, or different individual needs. However, they insist on addressing this through methods that don’t create economic distortions:

**Preferred Tools:**

- Taxes with negative deadweight loss (like carbon taxes)
- Taxes with neutral deadweight loss (like land value taxes)
- Universal basic income (which doesn’t distort market incentives)

**Avoided Tools:**

- Income taxes (distort labor markets)
- Sales taxes (distort consumption)
- Wealth taxes (distort investment)

The ideal implementation is a social state funded entirely by efficient taxes, distributing resources primarily through universal basic income (UBI). The only exception is funding for non-excludable goods like national defense that can’t be efficiently provided through private markets.

## Beyond Markets: The Full Scope of Freedom

A truly free society requires:

- Free speech protection
- Human rights guarantees
- Personal autonomy
- Democratic governance
- Rule of law

These freedoms reinforce economic freedom — markets function best in societies with strong institutions and personal liberties.

## Why Price Controls Fail

Some advocate for direct interventions like minimum wages or rent control to address inequality. Market equitists oppose these because they create deadweight loss — they prevent mutually beneficial transactions from occurring.

For example:

- Minimum wages may help some workers but create unemployment for others
- Rent control may help some tenants but reduces housing supply and quality

Instead of distorting prices, market equitists advocate addressing inequality through efficient taxation and direct cash transfers.

## Conclusion

Market equitism offers a comprehensive framework for maximizing both freedom and equality. By addressing market failures systematically, using efficient redistribution methods, and protecting personal liberties, we can build a society that is both more free and more equal.

This isn’t just theory — it provides clear guidance for policy:

- Replace distortionary taxes with efficient ones
- Fund a UBI through these efficient revenue sources
- Address market failures systematically
- Protect personal freedoms vigorously

In doing so, we can achieve what many consider impossible: reducing inequality while enhancing rather than restricting freedom.

# Dialing in Economic Policy: Growth and Equality

clay shentrup5 min read·Apr 19, 2025

--

Imagine we had two dials to control the economy: one would increase the size of the pie (economic growth), and the other would make the slices more equal (wealth distribution). In this simplified scenario, the optimal strategy would be straightforward — we would simply turn both dials to their maximum settings. This would create a wealthy, egalitarian society where prosperity is both maximized and shared equitably.

However, real economic policies rarely offer such clean, independent controls. Our policy choices typically involve more complex trade-offs.

## A More Complex Set of Dials

In practice, imagine we have these four dials instead:

- A dial that makes slices more equal without changing pie size
- A dial that increases equality while also growing the pie
- A dial that increases equality but shrinks the pie
- A dial that decreases equality while also shrinking the pie

How should we set these dials? Let’s analyze each one carefully:

- **Dial #4** should obviously never be turned up since it worsens both metrics we care about — it makes society both poorer and more unequal.
- **Dial #3** is the critical case that requires careful consideration. Even though this dial improves equality, we should avoid turning it up entirely. Why? Because of a fundamental asymmetry in our options:
- We already have two excellent ways to address inequality through dials #1 and #2.
- If we need more equality, we can simply turn up dial #1 (which makes society more equal without economic cost) or dial #2 (which makes society more equal while growing the economy).
- But once we shrink the pie with dial #3, that economic output is permanently lost — there is no dial that can specifically undo pie shrinkage without other effects.
- **Dial #2** should be maximized since it improves both metrics simultaneously — it grows the economy while making it more equal.
- **Dial #1** should be used freely to achieve whatever level of equality we desire after maximizing dial #2. It improves equality without any negative impact on economic growth.

## The Critical Asymmetry

This creates a profound insight: we should never accept pie shrinkage to achieve equality because we have better tools at our disposal. If a policy grows the pie but makes it more unequal, that’s fine — we can use dials #1 and #2 to correct the inequality without sacrificing growth.

However, if we shrink the pie to achieve more equality using dial #3, we permanently lose economic output that cannot be recovered. There is no “pure growth” dial that can undo the damage of pie shrinkage without affecting other variables. We’ve already maximized the growth-promoting policies (dial #2), so any pie shrinkage represents a permanent, irreversible loss.

## Real-World Economic Tools

These abstract dials correspond to actual economic policies:

## Dial #1 (Equality Without Affecting Economy Size)

- **Land Value Taxes**: Tax only the unimproved value of land, which doesn’t discourage productive activity
- **Universal Basic Income**: Provides direct transfers without distorting work incentives

## Dial #2 (Equality While Growing the Economy)

- **Pigovian Taxes**: Taxes on activities with negative externalities (like pollution), which can actually improve economic efficiency

## Dial #3 (Equality at the Cost of a Smaller Economy)

- **Income and Sales Taxes**: Create “deadweight loss” by discouraging economic activity
- **Subsidies for Specific Industries**: Distort markets and create inefficiencies

## Dial #4 (Less Equality and a Smaller Economy)

- **Price Controls**: Function as implicit taxes and subsidies, creating market distortions

## The Optimal Policy Approach

Now we can see why economists often advocate for a specific policy mix:

- Use Pigovian taxes (dial #2) to correct market failures and grow the economy
- Implement Land Value Taxes (dial #1) to raise revenue without economic distortion
- Fund a Universal Basic Income (dial #1) with this revenue to achieve desired equality
- Minimize or eliminate policies that shrink the economic pie (dials #3 and #4)

If someone suggests exempting poor people from paying Pigovian taxes or Land Value Taxes, this is equivalent to turning dial #3 — attempting to increase equality but at the cost of economic efficiency. Instead, we can achieve the same equality goal more efficiently by keeping the broad-based taxes and adjusting the UBI amount.

This framework reveals a profound insight: the apparent trade-off between equality and growth often disappears when we choose the right policy tools. We can create both a larger economic pie and ensure everyone gets a fair slice. But we must remember that economic shrinkage cannot be undone, while inequality can always be addressed through efficient redistribution mechanisms.

## Beyond Left and Right: A Third Way

This dial framework helps us see why both traditional economic left and right perspectives contain fundamental flaws.

## Where Both Sides Fall Short

The economic left often makes the fallacy of assuming that addressing inequality requires shrinking the pie. They propose policies like strict employment protections, price controls, and mandatory worker ownership that create deadweight loss. For instance, laws making it difficult to fire employees in countries like Spain might seem compassionate, but they ultimately shrink the economic pie. Companies that can’t adjust their workforce during downturns might go out of business entirely, causing even more unemployment in the long run.

Meanwhile, the economic right correctly values market efficiency but often rejects redistribution entirely. They recognize that many left-wing policies create market distortions but offer no alternative solution to inequality. This leads them to be characterized as callous or uncaring, further polarizing the debate.

## A Better Synthesis: The Nordic-Style Approach

What we need is a third way that combines the best insights from both perspectives:

- **Free and efficient markets** that maximize productivity and growth
- **Robust, non-distortionary redistribution** through universal programs

This closely resembles the Nordic model — countries with relatively free markets (including flexible labor markets where companies can hire and fire easily) combined with strong social safety nets funded through broad-based, efficient taxation.

## Reframing the Debate

We need to teach the right that it’s possible to provide a comprehensive social safety net without damaging the productive output of the economy. And we need to teach the left that many socialist policies are deeply flawed because they attempt to address inequality through means that distort the economy and crush productivity.

For example, forcing employees to own the “means of production” effectively tells workers they can only be paid in a specific form that may have substantial deadweight loss. A worker might receive a benefit they value at $20,000 that costs their employer $30,000. Most would prefer the cash so they could decide for themselves how to spend it.

This “gift card fallacy” lies at the heart of many inefficient policies. Why restrict people’s choices when you could give them resources directly and let them decide what they need?

By understanding the dial framework, we can break free from the false dichotomy between unfettered capitalism and inefficient socialism. We can carefully tune our economic policies to get the best of both worlds: a thriving, innovative economy where prosperity is broadly shared.

# The Selfish Origin of Apparent Altruism: A Natural History of Ethics

clay shentrup8 min read·Apr 25, 2025

--

In the vast theater of evolution’s grand design, a remarkable story unfolds — one that elegantly explains the paradoxical emergence of what we call morality from fundamentally selfish origins. This journey begins not with lofty philosophical ideals, but with a humble molecular structure that changed everything.

## The Dawn of Replication

At some distant point in our planet’s history — its precise moment lost to time — there emerged the first replicator. This molecular structure possessed two revolutionary properties: it could make copies of itself, and it could undergo structural changes (mutations) that would be preserved during replication.

The structure of each replicator determined both its ability to copy itself and its longevity — together defining its “fecundity,” or how prevalent it would become in the world. By simple mathematics, we encounter replicators in direct proportion to their success at maximizing copies. Thus, these molecules appear to “strive” toward maximizing their numbers, though this is merely the inevitable result of differential reproduction rather than conscious intent.

## From Simple Replicators to Sexual Reproduction

Some 385 million years ago, these replicators — having evolved into what we now recognize as DNA — developed sexual reproduction. This watershed moment introduced pervasive genetic remixing and gave rise to our modern understanding of genes: specific loci where different alleles (variants) might be found. The blue eye allele, for instance, is one possible variant at the eye color gene locus.

This mixing and matching enabled selection to operate at the level of individual alleles rather than whole organisms. These alleles, which we often simply call “genes,” exist in proportion to their reproductive success, creating the appearance of “selfishness” as they maximize their representation in subsequent generations.

## The Misnomer of Altruism

What appears as altruism from the perspective of whole organisms often masks genetic selfishness. Consider a bird sitting on an egg in its nest — this behavior, seemingly altruistic toward offspring, actually represents genes selfishly protecting copies of themselves within that egg. This phenomenon, misleadingly labeled “kin altruism,” isn’t altruism at all but rather selfish genetic strategy operating through family relationships.

Similar mechanisms explain our evolved preferences for sugar, salt, and sex — not as linear desires but as systems with diminishing marginal benefits. Once basic hydration needs are met, our attention naturally shifts to food. This diminishing utility explains why most humans would prefer a guaranteed million dollars over a 50% chance at three million. If utility increases approximately as the logarithm of wealth (utility = log₂(money)), this preference maximizes expected utility despite reducing expected wealth by a third.

The gambling preferences point directly addresses objections about “cardinality” in economic theory. Whether economists model utility as cardinal or ordinal doesn’t change the fact that our preferences evolved through natural selection to maximize reproductive fitness in uncertain environments. The logarithmic relationship between resources and survival benefit explains our risk-aversion without requiring any metaphysical assumptions about the nature of utility itself.

## The Economics of Genetic Preferences

A sophisticated understanding of utility theory reveals why assumptions of diminishing marginal utility aren’t necessary for standard economic results. Economists have known for about a century that diminishing marginal rate of substitution — not diminishing marginal utility — is all that’s required for core economic principles to hold. However, this distinction only strengthens the connection between evolutionary biology and economic behavior.

When we observe diminishing marginal rates of substitution in human behavior, we’re witnessing the mathematical expression of gene-driven preferences optimized for survival across various environmental contexts. The seeming contradiction between increasing marginal utility for certain goods and still wanting to trade them (like a corn farmer with increasing returns to scale) dissolves when we consider the full preference landscape across multiple goods. A corn farmer with increasing marginal utility for both corn and wheat will still trade corn if wheat offers even greater increasing returns.

This analysis directly addresses critiques about “incomplete” economic examples. The reality is that our brains evolved to navigate complex multi-dimensional preference landscapes where absolute utility for individual goods matters less than comparative advantage across the entire system — precisely what trade economics predicts. This isn’t a refutation of the biological basis of preferences; it’s a confirmation of how elegantly evolution has shaped our decision-making circuits to optimize for gene propagation in complex social environments.

The assertion that “the ‘size of the pie’ is measured by prices, which in turn are a function of the distribution of income” actually reinforces the evolutionary argument. Price mechanisms emerged as an efficient way to navigate the complex landscape of human preferences, which themselves emerged from gene-level selection pressures. The causal arrow runs from genes to preferences to economic systems, not the other way around.

## The Illusion of Ethics

Sufficiently intelligent organisms can communicate their genetically-instilled preferences. This communication creates the illusion that such preferences transcend biology to become something supernatural — what we call ethics. Yet ethics remains, at its foundation, verbalized subjective preferences.

This understanding helps us address the confusion in critiques distinguishing between “utilitarianism” and “economic agents maximizing utility.” While technically correct about the formal distinction, both concepts share the same mathematical foundation. Critics of utilitarianism are often attacking a strawman version that doesn’t acknowledge its roots in subjective preference and expected utility theory.

The economic distinction between “cardinal utility” versus “diminishing marginal rate of substitution” is a technical one that doesn’t undermine the core argument. Whether economists use cardinal or ordinal utility frameworks, the underlying reality remains: our preferences emerged from genetic selection pressures and manifest in ways that can be mathematically modeled. The economic debate over which mathematical tools best capture these preferences doesn’t change their evolutionary origins.

## From Individual Interest to Social Contracts

Humans think systematically. We recognize that dark alleys present mugging risks, so we selfishly prefer laws and enforcement mechanisms that protect us. Ideally, we might protect only ourselves and those genetically related to us, but since everyone has similar incentives, we can’t establish dictatorial laws. Instead, we create systems that appear designed for collective welfare — though those in power often exclude children, foreigners, and others when feasible.

We might conceptualize our ideal laws as “morals.” Since we rationally seek to maximize our expected utility, preferring some moral systems over others follows naturally from self-interest. Economist John Harsanyi elegantly demonstrated that from behind a “veil of ignorance” (where one doesn’t know their position in society), a utilitarian ethical system represents what each individual would rationally choose. This resembles altruism because of our many shared interests, such as not wanting to be victims of crime.

Harsanyi’s insight reveals expected utility theory and utilitarianism as two sides of the same coin — like mass and energy, virtually identical concepts in different forms. The technical terminology around “hedonism” in economics is not pejorative but a technical term that simply gives name to the gene-driven pleasure-pain circuitry that guides our choices. Claims that expected utility theory fails have spawned elaborate workarounds — modern epicycles — like prospect theory. Critically, even when people seemingly violate expected utility theory in the Allais scenarios, they are still behaving as utility maximizers when we account for the negative utility of decision costs themselves. The computational burden of determining optimal choices is itself a cost that rational actors must factor into their utility calculations. # don’t tax the rich, pay the poor: rethinking economic equality

clay shentrup7 min read·Apr 27, 2025

--

people often call for taxing the rich to address inequality. curiously, they almost never seem to realize the problem can be solved from the other direction: instead of taxing the rich, pay the poor.

this might seem both mathematically obvious and simultaneously paradoxical. if you’re going to pay the poor, where are you going to get the money from? the most simple, immediate, obvious answer is, *everyone — *yes, including the poor. let me explain.

imagine we enact a tax of x percent on earnings and evenly distribute the proceeds to all citizens. this even distribution would effectively be a refundable tax credit. (“refundable” just means if it exceeds what you owe, you get the remainder back as cash.) this would leave the bottom income deciles better off — they would receive more back from that universal lump sum distribution then they would pay as a percentage of their income.

to visualize this, consider the following table of tax rates.

```
┌──────────────┬────────────┬────────────────┐
│ Gross Income │ Net Income │ Effective Rate │
├──────────────┼────────────┼────────────────┤
│ 12,000$      │ 17,200$    │ -43.33%        │
│ 20,000$      │ 22,000$    │ -10.00%        │
│ 25,000$      │ 25,000$    │ 0%             │
│ 40,000$      │ 34,000$    │ 15.00%         │
│ 80,000$      │ 58,000$    │ 27.50%         │
│ 160,000$     │ 106,000$   │ 33.75%         │
│ 320,000$     │ 202,000$   │ 36.88%         │
│ 640,000$     │ 394,000$   │ 38.44%         │
│ 1,000,000$   │ 610,000$   │ 39.00%         │
└──────────────┴────────────┴────────────────┘
```

for instance, a person whose gross income is 40,000$ pays 15% of their income in taxes. whereas someone who earns a million pays 39%. this sure does look progressive, doesn’t it?

in fact, people grossing below 25k actually have a negative tax bill — they receive a refund. so while the *marginal tax rate*, the additional amount you pay for each dollar you earn, might be flat, the *effective tax rate* — the percent you pay in total as a function of your income, is extremely progressive.

now you might readily enough acknowledge the soundness of the math here, but then the question arises, why do this? why not just have progressive *marginal* tax rates?

well, first of all i could ask, why not? why favor one approach to the other in the first place? but it turns out there are a lot of advantages to the “pay the poor” approach versus the “tax the rich” approach.

flat marginal tax rates are administratively much simpler than progressive ones. there’s another profound advantage: they’re neutral with respect to time. with progressive marginal tax rates, you’d pay different taxes if you worked intensely one year and took the next year off compared to if you worked part-time and earned 50% in the first year and 50% in the second. this time-based distortion doesn’t make logical sense — why should the government care about when you earn your money? a flat rate with a universal dividend maintains effective net progressivity without this arbitrary timing penalty.

aside from allowing for non-distortionary flat marginal rates, this “pay the poor” approach allows us to more intelligently choose not just *whom *to tax, but *what* to tax. and this is crucial, because different taxes have a different effect on the *size* of the economic pie. if we can accomplish a certain amount of redistribution to make the slices of the pie more even, then we should ideally couple that with an approach that maximizes the overall size of the pie. it turns out there are some pretty straightforward ways to do this.

in economics, we have the notion of deadweight loss, which is an avoidable inefficiency in productivity. that is, the pie is smaller just because we made bad decisions. this can occur in the social safety net. suppose the government spends 100$ to buy you some sort of in-kind benefit that you only value at 70$. now there is 30$ of dead weight loss. essentially, it’s like we took 30$ of value and just tossed it in a furnace. but we can also get the same effect when we apply taxes to wealth that is generated by humans. suppose a working mother can earn 30$ an hour, and pays for child care provider 20$ an hour. but now we tax her income such that her take-home pay is below 20$ an hour. now it makes more financial sense for her to stay home and work, even though the most productive option was for her work and pay for child care. of course we’re simplifying a bit here because we’re not factoring in the cost and benefit of spending more quality time with her child, versus advancing her career, which she might be quite passionate about. we could certainly make the example more realistic by factoring some hypothetical numbers in for this, but it would just make the example more complicated and we could easily still arrive at the same result: a situation where the tax pushes the decision over the threshold and reverses it causing economic inefficiency also known as dead weight loss.

but there are certain types of tax that don’t have any dead weight loss. most notably is land value taxes, which are kind of like property taxes except that they only tax the land, which isn’t created by the human owner who is paying the tax and thus can’t affect their incentives. while a person can build new buildings on their property and that can increase the overall property values, it’s very little they can do to directly change the value of the land. the value of land is predominantly driven by external factors like access to jobs, stores, and other people. put simply, when we tax land we don’t get less land. now of course here’s where someone might object, if they came into this conversation at this point without the preceding parable, that a tax on land penalizes a small homeowner or retired old lady in the same percentage terms as it does a billionaire. but as we have seen, we can make up for this with a simple universal subsidy which produces a net progressive distribution. instead of taxing the rich we pay the poor. but since we used a tax that doesn’t shrink the economic pie, because as we said taxing land can’t make less land the same way taxing work can lead to less work, then there’s actually a bigger economic pie to go around.

we can take this in an even more pronounced direction through what is called pigovian taxation. pigovian taxes have not neutral dead weight loss but negative dead weight loss. these are essentially taxes on pollution, though the more general economic term is negative externalities. those activities which harm third parties. imagine a factory engaging in a much greater level of production than they would because they aren’t paying the cost of the environmental harm they’re doing that affects other people. if the factory had to pay those costs, they might produce a lot less of their widgets and that capital might flow to other business activities which have greater net productivity when accounting for the externalized cost that they currently don’t account for. thus, by taxing these activities, we can actually make the economy more productive.

by combining the holy trinity of pigovian taxes, land value taxes, and universal basic income (u.b.i.), we can share the pie without shrinking it.

this approach has several additional advantages over traditional progressive taxation. first, it’s simpler to administer. rather than a complex web of tax brackets, deductions, and exemptions that require armies of accountants and tax attorneys to navigate, we have a straightforward system: tax what we want less of (pollution, land monopoly) and distribute the proceeds equally.

second, it eliminates poverty traps. traditional welfare systems often create perverse incentives where earning more can actually leave recipients worse off as benefits phase out. this phenomenon, known as the “welfare cliff,” traps people in poverty by punishing advancement. with a u.b.i. funded by land and pigovian taxes, additional earnings are always beneficial, preserving the incentive to work and contribute.

third, it acknowledges the philosophical reality that land and natural resources are not products of human labor but common inheritances of humanity. when we tax income from work, we’re taxing something someone created. when we tax land value or pollution, we’re charging rent on using or abusing what belongs to everyone.

fourth, it separates the questions of “how much redistribution” from “how we tax.” these are distinct policy questions that should be decided independently. we can debate what percentage of g.d.p. should be redistributed while simultaneously optimizing our tax system to maximize efficiency.

critics might argue that land value taxes would hurt homeowners. but remember our table above — most homeowners would receive more in the universal dividend than they pay in land taxes. only those with very valuable land holdings would be net contributors. this actually addresses the current problem where young people are increasingly priced out of homeownership in desirable locations.

others might worry about implementation challenges. how do we accurately assess land values separate from improvements? how do we measure and price pollution? these are legitimate concerns, but they’re technical problems with technical solutions. modern data analysis and satellite imagery make land valuation more accurate than ever before. and while pollution measurement isn’t perfect, even imperfect pigovian taxes are better than none at all.

the most profound shift in this approach, however, is psychological. “taxing the rich” frames the conversation as taking from one group to give to another, inherently divisive and adversarial. “paying everyone” from our common resources reframes the conversation around our shared inheritance and collective prosperity. it’s not about punishment, but about proper stewardship of what belongs to all of us.

consider that alaska has been running a small-scale version of this system for decades through its permanent fund dividend, which distributes oil revenue equally to all residents. it’s consistently one of the most popular government programs in the state, supported across the political spectrum.

we can learn from this example and think bigger. by taxing not just oil but all land value and pollution, and distributing the proceeds universally, we could create a system that reduces inequality, eliminates poverty, preserves work incentives, and maximizes economic efficiency — all without the divisive rhetoric of class warfare.

the math is clear. the economics is sound. the philosophy is just. the only question is whether we have the political imagination to move beyond the tired paradigm of “tax the rich” and embrace a more universal vision of shared prosperity built on our common inheritance.

# The Schöntrup Tax: A Market-Based Revolution in Land Economics

clay shentrup10 min read·May 21, 2025

--

*Reimagining property ownership through continuous market discovery*

*Pronounced /ˈʃɜːrntruːp/ (SHURN-troop)*

For over a century, economists have grappled with the challenge of fairly taxing land while encouraging productive use. From Henry George’s single tax movement to modern Harberger taxation proposals, each system has attempted to solve the fundamental problem: how do we capture community-created land value without stifling development or creating administrative burdens?

While land value taxation (LVT) itself solves the deadweight loss problem — the supply of land is perfectly inelastic, so taxing it doesn’t reduce the quantity of land — implementation has always stumbled on a critical issue: **how do we accurately determine land values?** Government assessors are expensive, potentially biased, and create ongoing disputes and compliance costs.

The Schöntrup Tax offers a novel solution: **it’s essentially a Harberger tax applied specifically to land values rather than total property values**. This harnesses continuous market discovery to determine land values while allowing flexible, subjective valuation of improvements. However, as we’ll explore, this mechanism faces fundamental theoretical challenges that reveal deep problems with land value taxation itself.

## The Mechanism

The system operates through dual account holdings and competitive bidding:

**Account Structure** Every property owner maintains two dollar-denominated accounts with the government:

- **Land Value Account**: Inflation-adjusted, represents their assessment of pure land worth
- **Improvements Account**: Any assets, represents their valuation of buildings and improvements

**The Bidding Process**

- All bidders (including current owner) compete with their land value accounts
- Top two land value bidders become finalists
- Current owner chooses which finalist to sell to based on improvements offers
- Winner’s improvements account transfers to current owner
- Winner’s land value account becomes their ongoing LVT obligation with the government

**Ownership Transfer** Upon sale:

- New owner’s land account becomes their inflation-adjusted government account (LVT base)
- Former owner receives winner’s improvements account plus their original land account balance back
- System resets with new baseline values

## Why This Approach

**Perfect Price Discovery** Unlike traditional taxation systems that rely on periodic assessments, the Schöntrup Tax creates continuous market-based price discovery. Land values reflect what people are actually willing to commit in ongoing payments, backed by real money and competitive pressure.

**Administrative Efficiency** The government’s role is purely administrative — holding accounts and executing transfers when sales occur. No assessments, appeals, or valuation disputes. The market determines all values through revealed preferences.

**Development Neutrality** Traditional property taxes actively discourage development by taxing improvements — every building upgrade increases your tax bill. The Schöntrup Tax removes this anti-development bias by taxing only land values, not improvements. This eliminates the deadweight loss created when taxes on Pareto improvements reduce the quantity of improvements. Landowners already have natural profit-maximizing incentives; the system simply stops penalizing them for acting on those incentives.

## The Fundamental Challenge: A Crystal Clear Example

To understand why the Schöntrup Tax represents a significant advance despite its limitations, we need to first grasp the fundamental mathematical problem that plagues all land value taxation attempts.

**The Setup: Three Simple Numbers That Don’t Add Up**

Alice owns a property and values it as:

- Land: $400k
- House: $600k
- **Total: $1M**

Bob wants to buy the property and values it as:

- Land: $1.1M (he’ll build apartments)
- House: $0 (he’ll demolish it)
- **Total: $1.1M**

**The LVT Goal**: Tax only land value, not improvements

- Bob should pay Alice $600k for improvements
- Bob should pay government $1.1M/year in land rent
- **Total Bob pays: $600k + $1.1M/year**

**The Mathematical Problem**: Bob is only willing to pay $1.1M total (one-time), not $600k + $1.1M/year. The numbers literally don’t add up.

**Why This Breaks LVT**:

- If Bob pays market price ($1.1M total), Alice gets underpaid for improvements she values at $600k
- If Alice must be fairly compensated ($600k), Bob can only pay $500k/year in land rent
- But this undertaxes the land value ($1.1M) that LVT theory says should be captured

**The Impossibility**: You cannot simultaneously achieve:

- Efficient allocation (property goes to Bob who values it more)
- Fair compensation (Alice gets $600k for improvements)
- Full land value capture (government gets $1.1M/year)

The math requires $1.7M total ($600k + $1.1M) from someone willing to pay only $1.1M.

**This Isn’t About Assessment — It’s About Mathematics**

Your economist friend’s confusion (“The government assesses your property. It doesn’t care what you think your house is worth”) misses the point entirely. This isn’t an assessment problem — it’s a mathematical impossibility.

Even if government could perfectly assess every value:

- Alice’s improvement value: $600k
- Bob’s land valuation: $1.1M
- Bob’s total willingness to pay: $1.1M

The math still doesn’t work. Perfect information doesn’t create money that doesn’t exist.

**Why Traditional Responses Fail**

“You made a bad investment” — No, Alice made a perfectly rational investment in improvements worth $600k to her. The problem isn’t bad investment, it’s that LVT forces her to sell improvements for less than their value to her.

“Just sell to developers” — Bob IS the developer, and he values the house at $0. Alice can’t get fair compensation because the buyer doesn’t value what she built.

“Pay the tax and keep the house” — If government charges $1.1M/year land rent (based on Bob’s valuation), Alice is forced out despite valuing the property at $1M total. She can’t afford to pay more for land than she thinks it’s worth.

**The Deadweight Loss Reappears**

This forced under-compensation for improvements creates exactly the deadweight loss LVT was supposed to eliminate. If Alice fears losing improvement value when selling, she won’t invest in improvements — defeating the entire purpose.

**The Schöntrup Solution**

The Schöntrup Tax doesn’t solve this mathematical impossibility, but it handles it better than alternatives by:

- Using market mechanisms instead of assessment guesswork
- Allowing Alice to choose between competing improvement offers
- Creating competitive pressure for fair improvement compensation
- Accepting that some edge cases will be imperfect rather than pretending perfect solutions exist

## Gaming and Manipulation: The Value Allocation Problem

The mechanism faces sophisticated gaming attempts that expose fundamental design challenges.

**The Total Value Gaming Strategy** Bidders could attempt to put their entire property valuation into land value accounts to guarantee winning:

*Scenario*: Property worth $1M total

- Honest Bidder A: $400k land + $600k improvements
- Gaming Bidder B: $1M land + $0 improvements

Bidder B wins despite potentially valuing land lower, because they structured their bid to dominate the land value ranking.

**The Market Response** However, the system contains natural corrections:

- If Bidder B truly values land at only $400k, they’re committing to massive ongoing overpayment
- Current owner can recruit competing bidders: “Offer $401k land + $599k improvements instead”
- Both parties benefit from honest splits rather than wasteful land value inflation

**The Coordination Problem** Yet sophisticated coordination could still break the system:

- Multiple colluding bidders all bid $1M land + $0 improvements
- Current owner forced to choose among $0 improvement offers
- Legitimate bidders (A’s $600k improvements offer) get excluded from competition

**The Existential Question** This reveals a deeper issue: if multiple legitimate bidders genuinely prefer to structure bids as “all land value,” then **the market is revealing that improvements have zero value** to willing buyers. The mechanism isn’t broken — it’s working correctly, but the result seems unfair to current owners who invested in improvements.

## Comparison to Alternatives

**Traditional Property Taxation**

- **Advantages**: Familiar, captures some land value
- **Disadvantages**: Taxes improvements (deadweight loss), requires assessments, disputes, gaming
- **Assessment burden**: Expensive, biased, contentious

**Pure Harberger Taxation**

- **Advantages**: Market-based pricing, eliminates assessment
- **Disadvantages**: Taxes improvements equally with land, forced sales based on total value
- **Development impact**: Discourages improvements through taxation

**Government Land Value Assessment**

- **Advantages**: Could theoretically separate land from improvements
- **Disadvantages**: Requires omniscient assessors, politically contentious, expensive
- **Practical reality**: Assessment quality varies wildly, appeals consume resources

**Status Quo (No LVT)**

- **Advantages**: Simple, familiar
- **Disadvantages**: Community-created land value captured privately, inefficient tax base
- **Missed opportunity**: Foregoes deadweight-loss-free revenue source

## The Pragmatic Case: Why This May Still Be Optimal

Despite its theoretical challenges, the Schöntrup Tax may represent the best available compromise for several reasons:

**Market-Based Foundation** The mechanism relies on genuine market discovery rather than government guessing. Even with gaming possibilities, market forces provide more accurate pricing than bureaucratic assessment.

**Performance on Average** While extreme gaming scenarios are possible, they likely represent edge cases. In typical situations with reasonable competition and normal value distributions, the system should perform well.

**Improvement Taxation Minimization** Unlike pure Harberger taxes or property taxes, the system creates strong incentives to compete on improvements offers rather than rolling improvement value into ongoing tax obligations. This preserves the key LVT benefit of not discouraging development.

**Dynamic Efficiency** Properties flow continuously toward highest-value users through ongoing market pressure, rather than being locked in by assessment cycles or owner inertia.

**Elimination of Assessment Infrastructure** Removing the entire assessment bureaucracy — with its costs, biases, and political battles — may outweigh the theoretical inefficiencies the market-based system introduces.

**Theoretical Impossibility of Perfect LVT** Our analysis suggests that perfectly separating land and improvement values may be theoretically impossible due to their bundled nature. If perfect LVT is unachievable, a market-based approximation may be superior to assessment-based approximations.

## Addressing the “Impossibility” Critique

Recent analysis has argued that land value taxation may be theoretically impossible due to the “bundled value problem” — the idea that land and improvements cannot be meaningfully separated because their values are interdependent and subjective. This critique deserves serious consideration.

**The Bundled Value Argument** The critique uses an “orange juice and milk” analogy: when you force-bundle goods with different appeal to different buyers, you cannot extract full value from both components. Similarly, when Developer Mike values land at $900k but improvements at only $100k, while Owner Sarah values land at $400k but improvements at $600k, there’s no way to satisfy both valuations from a $1M total property value.

**The Measurement Problem**
 The critique argues that there’s no way to determine subjective improvement valuations without creating gaming incentives — any self-reporting system will be manipulated to minimize tax obligations.

**Why the Schöntrup Tax May Still Be Optimal** However, this theoretical impossibility argument may prove too much. It essentially argues that because perfect LVT is impossible, we shouldn’t attempt market-based approximations. But this overlooks several key points:

**Market-Based vs. Assessment-Based Approximations** If perfect LVT is indeed impossible, we face a choice between different imperfect approaches:

- Government assessment (expensive, biased, politically contentious)
- Market-based mechanisms (imperfect but responsive to actual preferences)

The Schöntrup Tax represents the latter approach. Yes, the system can be gamed in edge cases — the scenario where multiple bidders coordinate to bid $1M land + $0 improvements can indeed leave current owners uncompensated for improvements they value highly.

**But consider the alternatives:**

- Traditional property taxes discourage all improvements equally
- Pure Harberger taxes would force sales based on total property value, ignoring improvement investments entirely
- Government assessment requires bureaucrats to guess at values they cannot possibly know

**Performance in Typical Cases** The extreme gaming scenario (multiple coordinated bidders all valuing improvements at zero) likely represents an edge case. In typical situations:

- Bidders will have different land/improvement valuations

# the problem with socialism (and why free-market fundamentalists get it wrong too)

clay shentrup4 min read·Jun 21, 2025

--

our political discourse has trapped itself in a toxic binary. socialists demand worker ownership and wealth redistribution. free-market advocates counter with calls for economic growth and market freedom. both sides miss the point entirely, creating a false choice that obscures how economic policy actually works.

the truth is that economic policy operates along two completely separate dimensions. the first is market freedom — the policies that determine how large we can grow the economic pie. the second is redistribution — how evenly we slice that pie among citizens. understanding this distinction reveals why both traditional socialism and laissez-faire capitalism fail to maximize human welfare.

## the donut trap

imagine your employer is mandated to give you a $1,000 holiday bonus — but it has to be in donuts. sounds generous, right? this scenario illustrates the fundamental flaw in how socialists think about economic policy.

first, this mandate doesn’t actually increase your total compensation. your salary reflects the market equilibrium of supply and demand for your labor. if your employer must spend an additional $1,000 on you in donuts, they’ll simply reduce your cash salary by $1,000. the market doesn’t care about good intentions — it cares about equilibrium prices.

second, you’re not receiving the full value of what’s being spent on your behalf. if you only value those donuts at $400, then $600 of economic value has been destroyed. economists call this “deadweight loss” — the waste that occurs when we force people to receive benefits in forms they don’t prefer rather than giving them cash.

## why “worker ownership” misses the mark

this donut problem reveals the core issue with socialist prescriptions. the rallying cry for “worker ownership of the means of production” sounds revolutionary, but in practice it typically means receiving company stock as compensation. you’re not literally getting handed factory equipment — you’re getting equity that may or may not align with your actual needs.

company stock can be valuable compensation when it makes sense for both parties. but when mandated by government rather than chosen through market negotiation, it creates the same problems as our donut bonus. if that $1,000 worth of stock would be better spent on rent, food, or investments you trust more, then forcing you to receive it as equity actually makes you worse off. and crucially, it doesn’t increase your total compensation — market equilibrium ensures that.

socialists naively assume their opponents are simply greedy defenders of inequality. but the real problem isn’t redistribution itself — it’s inefficient redistribution that destroys value while failing to actually redistribute anything.

## why conservatives get it wrong too

classic conservative opposition to socialism comes across as mere defense of the wealthy. this plays right into socialist hands by making it seem like the only choice is between greed and compassion. conservatives focus so much on protecting market freedom that they ignore the legitimate concerns about inequality that drive people toward socialist solutions.

the result is a political discourse where one side champions inefficient redistribution while the other rejects redistribution entirely. both approaches leave enormous amounts of human welfare on the table.

## the elegant solution

we can have both market freedom and effective redistribution — but only if we use the right tools. three policies stand out as particularly powerful:

**land value taxes** operate without deadweight loss because taxing land doesn’t reduce the supply of land. unlike taxes on income or sales that discourage productive activity, land value taxes simply capture the value created by community development and public infrastructure.

**pigovian taxes** actually have negative deadweight loss — they grow the economic pie while generating revenue. by taxing activities that create harmful externalities (like carbon emissions), these taxes simultaneously discourage destructive behavior and fund beneficial programs.

**universal basic income** provides redistribution in its most efficient form: cash. rather than restricting recipients to specific goods or services through programs like food stamps or housing vouchers, u.b.i. allows people to spend money on what they value most, eliminating deadweight loss while providing genuine economic security.

## the holy trinity

this combination — pigovian taxes, land value taxes, and universal basic income — represents what economists call “efficient redistribution.” it grows the pie while making the slices more equal, avoiding the false choice between prosperity and fairness.

this approach was once called “neoliberalism” before that term was hijacked in the 1980s to describe the reagan-thatcher model of free markets without social safety nets. whatever we call it today, this framework offers a path beyond our current deadlock.

## beyond the binary

the beauty of this approach is that it satisfies the legitimate concerns of both sides. socialists get meaningful redistribution that actually improves working-class welfare. free-market advocates get policies that enhance rather than undermine economic efficiency. everyone wins except those who profit from the current system’s inefficiencies.

the problem with socialism isn’t its concern for equality — it’s the inefficient methods it uses to pursue that goal. the problem with free-market fundamentalism isn’t its concern for prosperity — it’s the callous indifference to how that prosperity gets distributed.

we need policies that maximize both the size of the pie and the fairness of how we slice it. that’s not naive idealism — it’s just good economics.

the pursuit of an equitable and efficient economic system often leads us down fascinating rabbit holes, especially when it comes to land. as a long-time advocate for utility-maximizing cardinal voting methods and a student of land value taxation (l.v.t.), i’m always on the lookout for fresh perspectives on how to capture the unearned increment of land value for public good.

that’s why i was particularly impressed with the progress and poverty institute’s recent piece, [decoupling land and improvement values](https://progressandpovertyinstitute.org/decoupling-land-and-improvement-values/). it’s a truly thoughtful exploration of a market-based approach to land value capture, aiming to streamline the process of assessing and taxing land while leaving improvements untouched — a georgist dream, in essence. their proposed system of public land rentals, continuous bidding, and “hostile takeovers” is a genuinely innovative framework for dynamically allocating land and capturing its economic rent.

what i find especially elegant is their commitment to ensuring that “the market itself will be utilized to determine both values of the unimproved land and the improvements, and thus appraisals are made unnecessary.” this commitment to market-based valuation is crucial for efficiency and transparency, addressing a common criticism of traditional l.v.t. implementations.

however, as i read through their brilliant proposal, a familiar echo resonated with me. the p.p.i. piece, while eloquently laying out its unique mechanism, implicitly relies on a concept i explored in my own article, ”the convergence of harberger taxation and land value capture: how destructive rights transform property tax into pure land rent”.

the p.p.i. article states that upon a hostile takeover, the incumbent tenant “must be compensated for their improvements at their market value.” this is absolutely necessary and just. but the critical question, which the p.p.i. piece doesn’t explicitly detail, is: *how is that “market value” enforced if the incumbent owner is effectively forced to sell their land lease?*

in a scenario where a higher bidder takes the land lease, and the previous tenant has no choice but to vacate, their leverage in negotiating for their improvements seems nonexistent. without some form of power, the new tenant could simply lowball them, knowing they have no recourse.

this is where the underlying, unspoken mechanism comes into play: **the threat of destruction.**

my argument, building on insights from the harberger tax, is that for any system like this to truly ensure “market value” compensation for improvements, the incumbent owner must retain the credible *option* to destroy or remove their improvements. they don’t have to actually do it. the mere fact that they *could* — that they could turn their valuable building into rubble — is sufficient to force the incoming tenant to offer a fair price. why would a new land leaseholder pay for a building if they could get it for free, or for a pittance? they wouldn’t. the only reason they would pay market value is if the alternative (having to build anew on a cleared lot) is more costly.

the p.p.i.’s genius is in designing a seamless, market-driven system for land value capture. and while it doesn’t explicitly name it, its effectiveness in compensating for improvements at market value inherently relies on the underlying principle that i’ve called “destructive rights.” it’s a silent concession to a fundamental aspect of property rights that ensures fairness in a dynamic land market.

it’s a testament to the convergence of ideas in this space. whether through harberger tax or a public rental exchange, the core challenge remains: how to capture land’s unearned value without penalizing human effort and investment in improvements. the p.p.i. offers a brilliant solution, subtly affirming the power of that crucial, often overlooked, right.

# the “built-in inflation” myth: why expectations aren’t market failures

clay shentrup7 min read·Nov 13, 2025

--

in economics textbooks, you’ll often find inflation categorized into three types: demand-pull, cost-push, and “built-in” (or adaptive) inflation. the first two make intuitive sense. but “built-in inflation” — the idea that inflation expectations themselves become a self-perpetuating cause of inflation — sounds suspiciously like economic voodoo.

recently, i had a debate with alexander about whether governments should intervene to “break” inflation expectations. his argument: when people expect inflation, they act in ways that perpetuate it (demanding higher wages, spending immediately), creating a coordination problem only government can solve. my response: if this isn’t a market failure, there’s nothing to fix.

## the stock market analogy

consider google stock. if millions of investors irrationally believe google will skyrocket in value, they bid up the price. other investors see the rising price and jump in. the momentum builds. everyone “expects” google to keep rising, and their collective behavior makes it rise — temporarily.

should the government intervene? should the s.e.c. publish “corrected” valuations to prevent this bubble? should the federal reserve set a target price for google stock and use policy tools to achieve it?

of course not. we recognize this as normal market behavior. eventually, intrinsic value reasserts itself. as warren buffett would attest, fundamentals eventually pop speculative bubbles. people who overestimated lose money. they learn. the market corrects. this is price discovery working as intended.

**so why do we treat inflation expectations differently?**

## expectations don’t create real constraints

alexander’s argument goes like this: if workers expect 10% inflation, they demand 10% raises. companies grant these raises and raise prices 10% to cover costs. actual inflation becomes 10%. the expectation became self-fulfilling, creating a cycle that needs breaking.

but this analysis misses something fundamental: **workers can only bargain harder if they have an alternative source of income.**

expectations are just beliefs. they don’t change underlying economic reality. if workers think their $50,000 salary is worth less due to expected inflation, they can demand $55,000. but if their marginal productivity only justifies $50,000 in real terms, and they have no better alternatives (starting a business, farming, taking another job), then they either:

- accept the $50,000, or
- become unemployed

the “expectation” doesn’t create bargaining power out of thin air. you can’t beat the market through belief alone.

## what about coordinated expectations?

the counterargument: “but what if *everyone* expects inflation? then all employers expect to raise prices 10%, so they’re all willing to pay 10% higher nominal wages.”

this seems like coordination at first glance, but ask yourself: **where does the actual inflation come from?**

employers can’t just raise prices 10% unless there’s actually 10% more money chasing the same goods. if the money supply is constant:

- employers who raise prices lose customers to competitors
- employers who can’t afford 10% raises hire fewer workers
- unemployment rises until wage demands fall to sustainable levels
- expectations adjust to reality

the expectations were just *predictions* about what the central bank would do with money supply. if those predictions turn out to be wrong, the market corrects — just like with stock prices.

## the real cause: monetary policy

let’s trace what actually happens in so-called “built-in inflation”:

- **central bank expands money supply** (demand-pull inflation)
- prices rise
- workers notice and negotiate higher wages (cost-push response)
- this feeds back into higher prices
- people *expect* this pattern to continue

but the **root cause** is the monetary expansion in step 1. the “expectations” in step 5 are just people learning the pattern — correctly predicting what the central bank will do.

if the central bank stopped expanding money supply, the cycle would break immediately. unemployment would rise until wage demands fell back to what’s economically sustainable.

calling this “built-in inflation” makes it sound like expectations have some autonomous causal power independent of monetary policy. but really, expectations are a *symptom* of past monetary policy, not an independent disease.

## the historical evidence

alexander cited historical examples of expectation-breaking: the u.s. panics of the 1870s-1890s, weimar germany’s currency replacement, the gold standard’s discipline, and japan’s meiji-era deflation. but look closely at these cases:

**the 1870s-1890s panics:** these were caused by bank failures, credit contractions, and real supply disruptions — not just “expectations that needed breaking.”

**weimar hyperinflation:** this ended when germany stopped printing money to pay war debts and introduced a new currency backed by real assets. the “expectations” were just people correctly predicting continued money printing.

**gold standard discipline:** this was literally a hard monetary constraint. when britain returned to gold at pre-war parity, they had deflation because money supply was constrained relative to real output.

**meiji-era japan:** finance minister matsukata deflated by *contracting money supply*, causing bankruptcies and poverty. the mechanism was monetary contraction, not psychological “expectation management.”

in every single case, adjustment came from **actual changes in money supply or real economic constraints** — not from some ritual of “breaking expectations.”

## the government-as-stock-picker fallacy

when alexander argues that government should intervene to “break inflation expectations,” he’s essentially arguing that government should:

- determine what the “correct” expectations should be
- take actions to shift public beliefs toward this correct view
- coordinate everyone around this improved expectation

this is exactly like saying the government should:

- determine the “correct” valuation for google stock
- publish information to shift investor beliefs
- coordinate everyone around this improved stock price

we recognize the latter as absurd. government doesn’t have superior information about google’s intrinsic value. even if it did, attempting to manage market expectations would interfere with price discovery and create moral hazard.

**why is inflation different?**

the answer can’t be “because inflation expectations are especially painful when wrong.” stock bubbles are painful too when they pop. unemployment from wage adjustments is painful, yes — but it’s the market correction that realigns wages with productivity. that pain is the signal that teaches people their expectations were wrong.

## what about market failures?

to be clear: i’m not arguing against all government intervention. there are legitimate market failures where intervention makes perfect sense.

**pollution:** when a factory dumps toxic waste into a river, there’s a negative externality. the problem isn’t that people don’t *know* pollution is happening — it’s an incentive issue. the factory doesn’t bear the full cost of its pollution, so it over-pollutes. the government can correct this with pigovian taxes to internalize the externality. this makes economic sense.

**monopolies:** when a single firm can restrict output and charge above-competitive prices, there may be a role for antitrust enforcement. again, this addresses a real structural problem in how markets function.

but notice what these interventions have in common: **they fix incentive problems, not information problems.**

the “built-in inflation” argument is fundamentally different. it claims the government needs to intervene because the public has *wrong expectations* — an information problem. the government supposedly knows better what inflation will be, and needs to “educate” or “coordinate” the public toward correct expectations.

this is absurd. if central bankers were so brilliant at predicting economic outcomes, they’d quit government work and become the next warren buffett. they’d make billions in the market rather than earning bureaucrat salaries.

the reality is that market participants — workers negotiating wages, businesses setting prices, investors allocating capital — have every incentive to get their inflation predictions right. they win or lose real money based on accuracy. a worker who demands too high a wage becomes unemployed. a business that raises prices too much loses customers. an investor who misjudges inflation sees real returns evaporate.

what superior information does the government have? the only “information” they have is what they plan to do with monetary policy. but if the market doesn’t believe their promises about future policy, maybe that’s because the government has a track record of breaking those promises. the “expectation problem” is just the market correctly predicting the government won’t follow through.

treating this as a market failure requiring intervention is backwards. if you want to argue for central banking, argue it on the basis of actual market failures in money creation — the difficulty of private coordination on a monetary standard, network effects in currency adoption, or genuinely structural issues.

don’t argue it on the pseudo-scientific notion that the government needs to perform expectation therapy on the populace because bureaucrats somehow have superior forecasting abilities to millions of people with skin in the game.

## the real coordination problem

alexander frames this as a “coordination problem only government can solve.” but markets solve coordination problems every day. prices coordinate millions of individual decisions without central planning.

if inflation expectations are misaligned with reality, the market has a correction mechanism: people who expected wrong lose money, adjust their behavior, and learn. just like with stocks. just like with any other price.

the government “managing expectations” doesn’t solve a coordination problem. it interferes with the coordination mechanism — the very price signals that would teach people to update their beliefs.

## conclusion

“built-in inflation” isn’t a meaningful economic category separate from demand-pull and cost-push inflation. it’s just a label for what happens when people learn to predict the central bank’s behavior.

inflation expectations are predictions, not market failures. when predictions are wrong, markets correct them through unemployment, lost profits, and adjusted behavior — the same way markets correct wrong predictions about stock prices.

the government shouldn’t try to “fix” inflation expectations any more than it should try to “fix” expectations about google stock. both would interfere with price discovery. both would prevent the learning that happens when expectations meet reality.

if we want stable prices, the solution is simple: constrain money supply growth. the expectations will follow. people aren’t stupid — they’ll learn what the central bank actually does, not what it says it will do.

the alternative — treating expectations as an autonomous force requiring government management — is economic voodoo dressed up in academic language. it anthropomorphizes what’s really just: people learning patterns and making predictions.

and in a free market, people should be free to make predictions, act on them, and learn from their mistakes — even about inflation.

# Against Public Libraries

clay shentrup6 min read·Oct 25, 2025

--

Consider the following thought experiment: You serve in the legislature of a nation that provides each citizen $100 monthly in unrestricted cash — fungible currency they may allocate according to their individual priorities, whether housing, nutrition, or clothing.

A proposal emerges to substitute this cash transfer with an equivalent monetary value in flowers. While the scenario borders on the absurd, I invite you to contemplate it seriously. Most legislators would, I trust, vote against such a measure. The intuition is sound: the typical household has minimal use for $100 worth of flowers monthly. Picture a struggling single mother attempting to provide adequate nutrition and winter clothing for her children — flowers offer her negligible practical utility.

This intuition, while correct, reflects a precise economic phenomenon known as deadweight loss. The flowers retain some value to our hypothetical recipient — perhaps she would willingly spend $5 on them given her constrained budget and competing necessities. This valuation reveals that the government has expended $100 of public funds to deliver merely $5 of utility to the recipient. The difference — $95 — represents pure deadweight loss, value effectively consigned to oblivion.

I begin with this deliberately preposterous example because its absurdity commands near-universal assent. Observe, however, what occurs when we incrementally approach domains we hold sacrosanct.

Consider instead a monthly allotment of $100 worth of film media — whether physical discs or streaming access. Applying identical economic principles, we must ask: what is this recipient’s subjective valuation? If she would voluntarily pay $10 monthly for such entertainment, we face $90 of deadweight loss. Even for a more affluent cinephile who might value the benefit at $70, $30 of deadweight loss persists. Only those already consuming more than $100 monthly in film entertainment experience no welfare loss — yet these individuals likely need assistance least. With direct cash transfers, deadweight loss vanishes entirely. Obviously, if our single mother desired films, she could purchase them with unrestricted funds. Converting fungible currency into a restricted voucher for any specific commodity — flowers, films, or otherwise — necessarily diminishes her welfare.

This principle operates independently of ownership structures. Whether the films are purchased outright or accessed through rental — be it physical media from a modern descendant of Blockbuster or streaming services with 48-hour viewing windows — the fundamental economics remain unchanged. Deadweight loss arises whenever we substitute in-kind benefits for cash, regardless of the underlying property arrangements.

Nearly everyone, when empathizing with benefit recipients, immediately recognizes the cruelty of restricting their purchasing discretion. Why impose additional constraints on those already struggling? Even affluent recipients of universal benefits suffer needless paternalism. What possible benefit justifies dictating their consumption choices?

## The Paternalism Problem: Why Bureaucrats Can’t Know What You Need

The standard defense of paternalism proceeds as follows: some people make such poor decisions that a well-informed bureaucrat could genuinely improve their welfare by overriding their choices. To understand why this argument fails in nearly all cases, we must distinguish between two types of preferences.

**Intrinsic preferences** are what you actually care about deep down — your fundamental goals and values. Perhaps you intrinsically value spending time with your children, maintaining your health, or pursuing creative projects. These are your true ends.

**Instrumental preferences** are the means you believe will achieve those ends — your best guess about which actions will satisfy your intrinsic preferences. You might instrumentally prefer buying lottery tickets because you believe this will make you wealthy (serving your intrinsic preference for financial security), even though statistically this choice undermines your actual goal.

The paternalist argument assumes that for some substantial portion of the population, there exists a large gap between intrinsic and instrumental preferences — that people systematically choose means that fail to serve their actual ends. And yes, this gap exists for some individuals. But paternalism fails for two fundamental reasons:

**First, the epistemic problem**: The bureaucrat does not and cannot know your intrinsic preferences. Your intrinsic preferences live inside your head. They emerge from your particular life circumstances, your relationships, your psychology, your values. The best available mechanism for revealing these preferences — for getting people to seriously consider and accurately evaluate what they truly value — is revealed preference in the market. When someone must spend their own limited money, they face real tradeoffs that force genuine prioritization. Observing how people actually allocate their resources across competing needs provides far more reliable information about their intrinsic preferences than any bureaucrat’s assumptions about what they “should” want.

When government substitutes in-kind benefits for cash, it severs this crucial feedback mechanism. The bureaucrat designing a library system cannot observe that you’d gladly trade those books for winter clothing, or that you value cooking ingredients more than DVDs, or that you have idiosyncratic but genuine reasons for prioritizing expenses the bureaucrat never contemplated.

**Second, the alignment problem**: Even if the bureaucrat somehow possessed perfect knowledge of your intrinsic preferences, their incentives are not aligned with your welfare. Bureaucrats answer to political pressures, institutional inertia, and their own career interests — not to your wellbeing. The politician championing public libraries gains votes from library supporters and employees, not from the invisible families who would have been better served by cash. The administrator’s success is measured by library attendance and book circulation, not by whether resources actually improved recipients’ lives according to their own values. These misaligned incentives systematically bias policy away from genuine welfare maximization.

Could paternalism ever be justified? Perhaps — but only for an exceedingly narrow segment of society. Consider individuals so severely impaired in their capacity for self-direction that they require intensive case management to meet basic needs: those with severe cognitive disabilities, advanced dementia, or profound mental illness rendering them unable to manage daily affairs. These individuals — perhaps one percent of the population or less — genuinely cannot translate resources into wellbeing without substantial guidance. For them, some form of paternalistic intervention may legitimately serve their interests better than cash alone.

But this describes a negligible fraction of society requiring specialized support, not a justification for universal in-kind programs. The struggling single mother, the working-class family, the median citizen — these people know their own needs and priorities far better than any distant bureaucrat, and they face properly aligned incentives to meet them.

## Back to Libraries

Yet substantial portions of society have embraced precisely this arrangement, often from ostensibly noble motivations. Consider your local public library.

The public library functions essentially as our hypothetical film rental facility, primarily distributing books — though modern libraries increasingly offer film access through platforms like Kanopy. Every dollar allocated to library maintenance represents a dollar that could have been distributed directly to individuals, who could then purchase books voluntarily — or acquire something they value more urgently. The same principle applies to nutrition assistance programs. If you possess adequate food but require a winter coat for your child, why should the social safety net compound your difficulties by prohibiting expenditure on your most pressing need?

I submit that the primary source of this fallacious reasoning — whether supporting public libraries or municipal parks — is the failure to consider alternatives. People perceive the benefit without contemplating opportunity costs: the foregone alternatives that could have been funded instead.

This critique does not categorically oppose public expenditures. Economics distinguishes between excludable and non-excludable goods. National defense, for instance, cannot be effectively privatized through direct cash transfers — a free-rider problem emerges because one household’s defense spending necessarily protects neighboring properties, eliminating others’ incentive to contribute. Such non-excludable goods resist private market provision. However, book rentals, groceries, and most consumer goods and services are perfectly excludable and readily available through private markets. Consequently, the most efficient method of expanding access to such goods is providing individuals with purchasing power to exercise according to their preferences.

If it provides comfort, imagine recipients submitting monthly wish lists, with government purchasing and distributing those precise items. Direct cash transfers simply eliminate the administrative overhead of this elaborate charade while preserving identical outcomes.

There exists a darkly comic symmetry in this debate: both left and right exhibit identical paternalistic instincts, mistrusting individuals’ spending decisions and preferring centralized management. The same person who recognizes the absurdity of restricting monthly allowances to flowers alone will vigorously oppose converting public libraries into equivalent cash transfers. This reveals that policy preferences emerge predominantly from quasi-religious conviction rather than rational analysis — to society’s considerable detriment.

The stakes could not be higher. We stand at a crossroads between a future of unprecedented human flourishing and a descent into authoritarian chaos. The choice before us isn’t merely theoretical — it’s existential. And we’re getting it wrong in nearly every way imaginable.

## phase-outs are taxes

any benefit that phases out with income is economically identical to a tax. reduce someone’s s.s. benefit by $1 for every $3 earned above a threshold? that’s a 33% implicit marginal tax rate.

stack that on federal income tax (24–37%), state tax (0–13%), payroll tax (7.65%), and medicare surcharges (3–7%). **combined marginal rates easily hit 60–80%.**

## deadweight loss scales with t²

d.w.l. ≈ ½ × ε × t² × q

a 60% marginal rate creates **4x the deadweight loss** per dollar raised as a 30% rate. stacking means-testing on income taxes doesn’t add to d.w.l. — it multiplies it.

## progressive ≠ good (a crucial distinction)

here’s what people miss: **we want progressive effective tax rates, not progressive marginal tax rates.**

progressive marginal rates create escalating deadweight loss (scales with t²). but you can achieve progressive effective rates through:

**flat marginal rate + u.b.i. (universal refundable tax credit)**

example with 30% flat tax + $15k u.b.i.:

- person earning $0: receives $15k, pays $0 → effective rate: -∞%
- person earning $50k: receives $15k, pays $15k → effective rate: 0%
- person earning $100k: receives $15k, pays $30k → effective rate: 15%
- person earning $500k: receives $15k, pays $150k → effective rate: 27%

same progressivity as current system, but:

- constant marginal incentives (always keep 70¢ per dollar earned)
- lower deadweight loss (no escalating rates)
- no phase-outs, no poverty traps
- eliminates administrative complexity

progressive marginal rates are bad policy dressed up as fairness. what matters is the effective rate, which can be highly progressive with flat marginal rates.

## intertemporal neutrality violation

means-testing social security creates:

- **savings distortion:** why save if it reduces benefits?
- **lifecycle labor distortion:** front-load earnings when young, reduce income near retirement to game the system
- **human capital distortion:** future earnings reduce future benefits = tax on education

we already see 90%+ marginal rates in existing means-tested programs (s.n.a.p. + medicaid + section 8 + income tax). adding s.s. means-testing extends poverty traps to middle-class near-retirees.

## social security is already progressive

without phase-outs!

- progressive benefit formula: bottom tier gets ~90% replacement; top tier gets ~32%
- benefits are taxable income for high earners
- no poverty traps, no gaming through intertemporal shifting

## the marketarian solution

**separate redistribution (normative) from efficiency (technical).**

**revenue:** pigovian taxes + land value tax

- pigovian: corrects externalities = negative d.w.l.
- l.v.t.: perfectly inelastic supply = zero d.w.l.
- flat rate on remaining revenue needs

**distribution:** universal basic income (lump-sum, no phase-out)

- zero marginal disincentive
- eliminates poverty traps
- creates progressive effective rates without progressive marginal rates

**comparison:**

*current system:* progressive marginal rates + means-tested benefits = high d.w.l. from escalating rates + phase-outs

*marketarian:* flat marginal rate + pigovian/l.v.t. + u.b.i. = lower d.w.l., same (or more) progressivity

once you have u.b.i. + flat tax + l.v.t., means-testing is **redundant**.

both systems can achieve identical net redistribution (calibrate rates). but means-testing adds pure deadweight loss with no additional benefit.

# no welfare for rich people: why compensating history creates perverse outcomes

clay shentrup5 min read·Dec 29, 2025

--

consider a thought experiment: congress passes a law imposing a special tax on everyone born on january 1st. no rationale is offered beyond the arbitrary fact of their birthdate. this is clearly unjust — a form of oppression that makes people worse off based on immutable characteristics beyond their control that bear no relationship to merit or need.

now imagine two responses to this injustice:

**response a**: a coalition organizes to repeal the federal birthday tax entirely. we would rightly call this the pursuit of justice — the elimination of arbitrary discrimination.

**response b**: a state government, unable to override federal law, decides to compensate all january 1st birthdays for their tax burden. they raid the state treasury to cut checks to everyone affected, dollar-for-dollar offsetting the federal tax.

these responses might seem morally equivalent — after all, both aim to undo the harm of the birthday tax. but they are not equivalent at all. response b is deeply problematic in ways response a is not.

## the hidden costs of compensation

the birthday compensation scheme would inevitably send checks to january 1st babies across the entire economic spectrum. statistical distribution ensures this includes millionaires, perhaps even billionaires. meanwhile, someone born on january 2nd in desperate poverty receives nothing — not because they’re less needy, but because they fail an arbitrary birthday test.

every dollar sent to a wealthy january 1st birthday is a dollar unavailable for someone in genuine need born on any other date. the compensation scheme doesn’t just fail to maximize welfare — it actively creates perverse incentives to maintain the harmful federal law as a prerequisite for state benefits. the wealthy january 1st cohort now has a financial interest in preserving their “victim” status.

## undoing versus reversing

this reveals a crucial distinction: **undoing** a policy eliminates the harm at its source; **reversing** a policy perpetuates group-based thinking while attempting to neutralize effects. the former pursues justice; the latter entrenches categorization.

the obviously superior approach, if federal repeal proves impossible, would be to target economic need directly. [progressive taxation](https://www.ubicenter.org/us-flat-tax) helps those who actually require assistance — the poor born on any date — without regard to why they’re poor. this approach is both more efficient (maximizing welfare per dollar) and more just (treating individuals as individuals, not as members of arbitrary groups).

## extending the logic

now apply this framework to contemporary debates over racial remediation. historical injustices — slavery, jim crow, indigenous genocide, discriminatory housing policy — created persistent inequalities. no honest person denies these harms. the question is: what’s the just remedy?

affirmative action, reparations, and race-based preferences follow the logic of response b: identify groups harmed by historical policy, then compensate based on group membership. but this creates the same perversities as birthday compensation.

does lebron james’ son require affirmative action preferences more than an appalachian child born into intergenerational poverty? does a black software engineer descended from slaves need compensation more than a white child with fetal alcohol syndrome born to an unemployed single mother? when we compensate based on ancestry rather than actual economic circumstance, we inevitably send resources to some who don’t need them while denying help to others in desperate straits.

## the statistical reality

historical oppression distributed disadvantage unequally within victim groups. not every descendant of slaves is poor; not every descendant of slaveowners is wealthy. generations of social mobility, individual choices, geographic variation, and random fortune have created massive heterogeneity within all ancestral groups. to treat “descendants of the historically oppressed” as a monolithic class requiring uniform compensation is to commit a category error — one that becomes harder to justify with each passing generation.

meanwhile, hereditary disadvantage takes countless forms beyond historical group oppression: congenital disabilities, exposure to environmental toxins, abusive households, geographic isolation, random medical catastrophe. why should only certain sources of disadvantage trigger compensation? what principle justifies helping someone because their great-great-grandfather was enslaved while ignoring someone whose mother was exposed to lead paint or whose father died in an industrial accident?

## the progressive alternative

the optimal redistribution system combines efficiency-maximizing revenue sources with universal transfers. pigovian taxes on negative externalities (carbon emissions, pollution, congestion), land value taxes that capture unearned location rents, and universal basic income distributed equally to all citizens. this structure achieves progressive outcomes — the poor receive far more than they pay; the wealthy pay far more than they receive — while eliminating deadweight loss from taxation.

if we’re constrained to income taxation, the solution is straightforward: [flat marginal rates combined with u.b.i.](https://www.ubicenter.org/us-flat-tax) creates progressive effective rates without the efficiency losses of high marginal rates. such a system would disproportionately benefit historically oppressed groups in aggregate precisely because historical oppression created economic disadvantage. but it would do so by actually targeting economic need, not by using race as a crude proxy.

this approach has multiple advantages:

**efficiency**: pigovian taxes and l.v.t. minimize or eliminate deadweight loss while raising revenue. resources flow to those with the greatest need through u.b.i., maximizing welfare gains.

**justice**: treatment is based on actual circumstances, not immutable characteristics or ancestral history.

**sustainability**: no perverse incentives to maintain victim classifications or to police group boundaries.

**universality**: poor whites, poor blacks, poor immigrants, and poor indigenous people all receive equal transfers — fostering solidarity rather than inter-group competition for resources.

**simplicity**: no need for elaborate adjudication of ancestral claims, means-testing bureaucracy, or fractional ancestry calculations.

## the objection from history

defenders of race-based remediation argue that this approach ignores the specific historical debt owed to particular groups. but consider: if a mugger steals your wallet, we don’t pursue justice by making the mugger’s descendants compensate your descendants in perpetuity. we pursue justice by apprehending the mugger and attempting restitution from the actual perpetrator. once the perpetrator is dead, the debt dies too — unless we want to embrace multi-generational collective guilt, a principle fraught with dangers.

the living have no obligation to remedy harms they didn’t commit. they do, however, have obligations to ensure justice in the present — which means addressing current poverty, current discrimination, current inequity. these obligations flow not from ancestral guilt but from basic solidarity with fellow citizens. and they’re best fulfilled through mechanisms that target present circumstances, not historical categories.

## conclusion

the birthday tax thought experiment clarifies what often becomes muddled in identity politics: good intentions don’t always generate good policy. attempting to compensate for historical injustice through group-based redistribution repeats the very error — categorizing people by immutable characteristics — that created injustice in the first place.

if we truly seek equality, the path forward is clear: redistribute based on actual economic circumstance, not ancestral membership in historically wronged groups. this maximizes both efficiency and justice, ensures resources reach those in genuine need, and treats people as the individuals they are — not as avatars for group-based grievances they didn’t personally experience.

the goal should never be to reverse specific historical harms while leaving inequity intact. the goal should be to eliminate inequity itself — from whatever source it flows.

# Marketarianism

clay shentrup39 min read·Dec 30, 2025

--

## Why Everyone — Left, Right, and Center — Should Want Efficient Markets

Breaking the stalemate: The glowing machinery of market efficiency slices through the ideological barriers separating left and right.

## Part I: Breaking the Stalemate

## Chapter 1: The Endless War

Progressives and conservatives have been fighting about economics for over a century. The battle lines feel permanent: one side wants more government intervention and redistribution, the other wants free markets and limited government. Each sees the other as either naive or malicious. Progressives suspect anyone making “free market” arguments is secretly against helping people — a shill for the rich. Conservatives suspect anyone wanting redistribution is a socialist who doesn’t understand how wealth gets created.

This war is exhausting, and worse, it’s largely based on a misunderstanding.

The standard framing presents one axis: more government vs. less government. But this conflates two completely separate questions that deserve separate answers:

- **How much redistribution should there be?** (Equity — how we share the pie)
- **How efficient should markets be?** (Efficiency — how big we make the pie)

These are not the same question. You can want a lot of redistribution AND efficient markets. You can want minimal redistribution AND efficient markets. The efficiency question has a largely technical answer that almost everyone should agree on, regardless of where they stand on redistribution.

Once you see this, the endless war starts to look less like a principled conflict and more like a tragic misunderstanding — people fighting over a false dichotomy while the actual enemy (inefficiency that makes everyone worse off) escapes unnoticed.

## No Villains, Just Interests

Here’s something that might be uncomfortable: there are no villains in this story.

A billionaire who opposes redistribution isn’t evil — they’re acting in their self-interest. A minimum-wage worker who supports redistribution isn’t envious — they’re also acting in their self-interest. Both are rational actors responding to their circumstances.

The philosopher John Rawls asked us to imagine choosing social arrangements from behind a “veil of ignorance” — not knowing which position in society we’d occupy. Behind that veil, pure self-interest would lead us to care about everyone’s outcomes, because any of them could be ours.

But in real life, we’re *not* behind the veil. We know who we are. The billionaire knows they’re a billionaire. The minimum-wage worker knows they’re struggling. Given this knowledge, of course they’ll have different preferences about redistribution.

**If you’re poorer than average, you should rationally favor more redistribution. If you’re richer than average, you should rationally favor less.** Once we understand this, the moral outrage drains away. We’re not dealing with good people vs. bad people — we’re dealing with people in different positions, each advocating for arrangements that benefit them. That’s just… politics. It’s what democracy is for: resolving competing interests through negotiation rather than violence.

The question of *how much* redistribution happens is political — it depends on who has power and how interests get aggregated. I’m not here to tell you the right answer to that question.

But the question of *how* to achieve whatever redistribution level gets chosen? That’s largely technical. And almost everyone is currently getting it wrong.

## The Hidden Agreement

Here’s the claim that should unite left and right: **Whatever level of redistribution you support, you should want it achieved through efficient mechanisms rather than distortionary ones.**

This isn’t a partisan position. It’s just common sense:

- If you want to help the poor, you should want policies that actually help them, not policies that *feel* helpful but create perverse side effects
- If you want economic growth, you should want taxes that raise revenue without destroying the economic activity that generates the revenue
- If you want environmental protection, you should want mechanisms that actually reduce pollution, not bureaucratic theater

The tragedy is that both left and right currently support wildly inefficient policies — not because they’re stupid, but because they’ve confused their goals with particular policy tools. Progressives defend minimum wage and rent control because these *feel* like helping workers, even though direct cash transfers would help more. Conservatives defend tax loopholes and oppose all redistribution because they’ve conflated “free markets” with “no safety net,” when these are actually separate questions.

Separating efficiency from equity lets us see something important: you can be completely on board with redistribution, even enthusiastic about it, while opposing policies like rent control or minimum wage — not because you don’t want to help people, but because these policies *create* market distortions rather than correcting them. That doesn’t make you anti-worker. It makes you pro-efficiency.

## What This Document Does

This document takes a strong position on the efficiency question, but *not* on the redistribution question.

I’ll argue that certain policy tools — land value taxes, Pigovian taxes on externalities, universal basic income — are objectively more efficient than alternatives. This isn’t a values claim; it’s a claim about deadweight loss, which can be measured.

I won’t tell you how much redistribution there should be. That depends on your circumstances, your values, and ultimately on the democratic process that aggregates everyone’s interests. But whatever level gets chosen, it should be implemented well rather than badly.

By the end, you should understand why:

- Progressives who want to help the poor should *oppose* minimum wage and rent control
- Conservatives who want free markets should *support* some form of universal basic income
- Everyone should support land value taxes and carbon pricing

These conclusions sound paradoxical only if you’re stuck in the old “more government vs. less government” framing. Once you separate efficiency from equity, they follow naturally.

## Chapter 2: The Technical Foundations

Before we can talk about policy, we need some conceptual tools. These are dry, apolitical concepts — the equivalent of learning basic physics before engineering. Bear with me; this foundation makes everything else click.

## Pareto Improvements and Why Trade Is Humanitarian

Imagine two people: Alice has an apple, Bob has an orange. But Alice prefers oranges, and Bob prefers apples.

If they trade, both are better off. Alice gets what she prefers; Bob gets what he prefers. No one is worse off, and at least one person (actually both) is better off. Economists call this a **Pareto improvement** — a change that makes someone better off without making anyone worse off.

This is what voluntary exchange does. When two people trade freely, it’s because both expect to benefit. Otherwise, why would they trade? The mere fact that a trade happens is evidence that both parties preferred to make it.

Now here’s the key insight: **“free markets” are just systems that allow Pareto improvements to happen.**

When someone says they support “free trade” or “free markets,” many people hear an Ayn Rand screed — a callous indifference to the poor, a worship of the rich. But that’s not what it means. At its core, “free markets” just means: *let people make exchanges that make them better off*.

This is an incredibly humanitarian concept. Preventing trades that would benefit both parties makes the world objectively worse.

## Deadweight Loss: When We Prevent Good Trades

Now suppose the government taxes trades. Alice and Bob still want to exchange their fruits, but now they have to pay a 50% tax on the transaction.

If the trade was barely worth it before — maybe Alice slightly preferred oranges to apples — the tax might make it not worth it anymore. So they don’t trade. Alice keeps her unwanted apple, Bob keeps his unwanted orange, and both are worse off than they would have been.

The tax collected? Zero — because the trade didn’t happen.

The government gets nothing, and Alice and Bob are both worse off. This lost value — the benefit that would have existed if the trade had happened — is called **deadweight loss**. It’s economic value that simply vanishes because we’ve prevented a Pareto improvement.

This isn’t some abstract theoretical concern. Deadweight loss is real and measurable. Every tax that discourages productive activity creates some of it. The question isn’t whether to have taxes — we need taxes to fund public goods — but how to minimize the deadweight loss per dollar of revenue raised.

## Not All Taxes Create Deadweight Loss

Here’s where it gets interesting: some taxes create a lot of deadweight loss, and some create very little or none at all.

**High deadweight loss taxes** discourage activities we *want* to happen:

- Income taxes discourage work
- Sales taxes discourage consumption
- Capital gains taxes discourage investment
- Corporate taxes discourage business formation

Tax these things, and you get less of them. That’s Pareto improvements prevented. Value destroyed.

**Zero deadweight loss taxes** target things that aren’t produced by human effort:

- Land exists regardless of taxation. You can’t “produce less land” in response to a land tax. The land will still be there, and taxing its value doesn’t discourage any productive activity.

**Negative deadweight loss taxes** actually *enable* Pareto improvements that weren’t happening:

- Carbon emissions impose costs on others (climate change, air pollution). Because emitters don’t pay these costs, they emit more than they would if prices reflected true costs. A carbon tax corrects this, *enabling* efficient trades that weren’t happening because prices were wrong.

This is the foundation of everything that follows. We’re not making value judgments about who deserves what. We’re just observing that some policies destroy value and others don’t. Whatever your politics, you should prefer the ones that don’t.

## Revealed Preference: How We Know What People Actually Want

One more concept: how do we know what people actually prefer?

Here’s a simple but powerful idea: if you want to know what someone values, don’t ask them — watch what they choose when they have to live with the consequences. Economists call this **revealed preference**.

Why not just ask? Because talk is cheap. People say all kinds of things about what they value, but when they have to actually make tradeoffs — when choosing A means giving up B — their true priorities emerge. A person who says they value health but smokes two packs a day is revealing something about their actual preferences that contradicts their stated preferences. The choices are more honest than the words.

This might sound cynical, but it’s actually respectful. It takes people seriously as agents who know their own situation. Instead of some philosopher or bureaucrat deciding what people *should* want, we observe what they *do* want by watching them navigate real tradeoffs.

This matters because the whole framework rests on respecting what people actually prefer, not what we think they should prefer. We’ll use revealed preferences to ground our claims about human welfare.

## Freedom and Redistribution Are Orthogonal

Now we can make precise what people mean — or should mean — by “free markets.”

A “free market” isn’t an ideological commitment. It’s just a system that allows Pareto improvements to happen. When trades are voluntary, both parties benefit. Preventing those trades makes everyone worse off.

People who recoil at “free market” rhetoric are usually reacting to something else: the assumption that free markets preclude redistribution or helping the poor. But this is a conceptual error.

**Redistribution is orthogonal to market freedom.**

You can have:

- Free markets + high redistribution (tax efficiently, transfer generously)
- Free markets + low redistribution (tax efficiently, transfer minimally)
- Distorted markets + high redistribution (minimum wage, rent control, tariffs)
- Distorted markets + low redistribution (regulatory capture, corporate welfare)

The market freedom axis and the redistribution axis are *independent*. You choose your position on each separately.

This means supporting “free markets” doesn’t make you anti-redistribution. It just means you want Pareto improvements to happen — you want the pie to be as large as possible. What you do with that pie — how you slice it — is a separate choice.

Conversely, wanting redistribution doesn’t require supporting market distortions. You can have your redistribution AND efficient markets. In fact, you can have *more* redistribution if markets are efficient, because there’s more pie to redistribute.

The real choice isn’t “free markets vs. helping people.” It’s “efficient redistribution vs. inefficient redistribution.” And that’s not a values question — it’s an engineering question.

## Chapter 3: The Inequality Question

Here’s a question that seems simple but reveals everything: *Do you care about inequality?*

Most people say yes. But let’s test that.

**Scenario A:** Everyone in society makes $20,000 per year. Perfect equality.

**Scenario B:** Half of people make $40,000 per year, and the other half make $40,001. Technically “unequal.”

Which would you prefer to live in?

Unless you’re deeply confused, you’d choose Scenario B. Everyone is twice as wealthy, and the “inequality” is trivially small. So clearly, inequality *per se* isn’t what you care about — you care about something else.

Let’s push further.

**Scenario C:** Everyone makes $20,000.

**Scenario D:** Half make $40,000, half make $80,000.

Now we have real inequality — the top half has double what the bottom half has. But notice: even the *worst off* person in Scenario D ($40,000) is twice as wealthy as *everyone* in Scenario C ($20,000). Which would you prefer?

Most people still choose D. Being in the bottom half of a richer society beats being “equal” in a poorer one.

But wait — what if the tradeoffs get sharper?

**Scenario E:** Everyone makes $30,000.

**Scenario F:** Half make $29,000, half make $80,000.

Now it’s trickier. In Scenario F, if you end up in the bottom half, you’re actually *worse off* than in perfect equality. You’re taking a gamble: 50% chance of being $1,000 poorer, 50% chance of being $50,000 richer.

Would you take that bet?

Most people would. The potential upside ($50,000 gain) vastly exceeds the potential downside ($1,000 loss). Even accounting for the fact that losses feel worse than equivalent gains, this is a good deal.

## Finding the Threshold

These thought experiments force us toward a crucial question: *Where exactly is the threshold?* At what point does inequality become “too much”?

Here’s the key insight: **I don’t need to answer this question, and neither do you — at least not to agree on policy.**

Different people will have different thresholds based on their circumstances, values, and risk preferences. A young entrepreneur might accept more inequality in exchange for higher growth potential. A retiree on fixed income might prefer more security. Neither is wrong.

What matters for policy is recognizing that *whatever* threshold you prefer, you want to achieve it efficiently. And to do that, we need to understand the principle underlying these intuitions.

The principle people actually use, whether they realize it or not, is **decreasing marginal utility**.

Going from $20,000 to $40,000 is life-changing. You move from poverty to stability — from worrying about rent to having a cushion. Going from $100,000 to $120,000 is nice, but it’s not life-changing. You were already comfortable; now you’re slightly more comfortable.

A reasonable approximation is that utility scales with the *logarithm* of wealth:

> ***Utility ≈ log(wealth)***

This means doubling your wealth provides roughly the same utility boost regardless of your starting point. $10k→$20k feels about as good as $100k→$200k or $1M→$2M.

## Testing the Principle

Remember revealed preference from Chapter 2? Let’s apply it here.

Consider: would you rather have a guaranteed $1 million, or a 50% chance at $3 million (and 50% chance of nothing)?

Most people take the guaranteed million. The expected dollar value of the gamble is higher ($1.5M vs $1M), but the *expected utility* is lower. Using log utility:

- Guaranteed $1M: log($1,000,000) = 6.0
- 50% chance of $3M: 0.5 × log($3,000,000) + 0.5 × log($0) = 0.5 × 6.48 + 0.5 × (-∞) = very bad

Okay, that example breaks down at zero (log of zero is undefined). Let’s use a more realistic one:

Would you take a guaranteed $100,000, or a 50% chance at $300,000 vs. $20,000?

- Guaranteed $100k: log($100,000) = 5.0
- Gamble: 0.5 × log($300,000) + 0.5 × log($20,000) = 0.5 × 5.48 + 0.5 × 4.30 = 4.89

The guaranteed $100k has higher expected utility (5.0 > 4.89), so most people would take it — and they do.

This pattern holds across countless experiments in behavioral economics. People are “risk averse” in exactly the way that log utility predicts. Not because they’re irrational, but because they correctly perceive that a dollar means more to a poor person than to a rich one.

## The Preference Sovereignty Principle

Here’s the critical move: if people *prefer* outcome X to outcome Y — even when those outcomes are probabilistic gambles — then giving them X is, by definition, more ethical than giving them Y.

Call this the **preference sovereignty principle**: we cannot judge on people’s behalf what is ethical; only they can do that through their expressed preferences.

This isn’t some exotic philosophical claim. It’s almost tautological. If “good” means anything at all, it means “what people actually want.” An ethics that overrides what informed people would choose for themselves isn’t serving them — it’s dominating them.

The preference sovereignty principle means we don’t need to argue about abstract moral theories. We just need to ask: what do people actually prefer? And we can *measure* that — by watching what they choose.

## Chapter 4: From Individual Preferences to Social Policy

Now let’s apply this to society.

Imagine you’re about to be born into a society, but you don’t know which position you’ll occupy. You might be born rich or poor, talented or disabled, lucky or unlucky. Behind this “veil of ignorance,” which social arrangement would you choose?

This thought experiment, associated with philosopher John Rawls, clarifies our thinking about justice. When you don’t know where you’ll end up, pure self-interest leads you to care about everyone’s outcomes — because any of them could be yours.

## But We’re Not Behind the Veil

Here’s the catch: in real life, we’re *not* behind the veil of ignorance. You know roughly where you stand. A billionaire knows they’re a billionaire. A minimum-wage worker knows they’re a minimum-wage worker.

This means rational people will disagree about how much redistribution is optimal — and that’s fine. The billionaire rationally prefers less redistribution; the minimum-wage worker rationally prefers more. Both are acting in their self-interest, and democracy is the mechanism by which these competing interests get resolved.

**I’m not here to tell you what level of redistribution is correct.** That’s a political question, resolved through voting and negotiation among people with different interests.

What I *am* here to tell you is that whatever level gets chosen, there are efficient and inefficient ways to achieve it. And almost everyone is currently supporting inefficient ways.

## The Wrong Answer (About Method, Not Level)

Rawls argued that people behind the veil would choose to maximize the position of the worst-off person (“maximin”). Always protect the bottom, no matter the cost to everyone else.

But this is demonstrably false as a description of how people actually reason. Look at what people actually choose — their revealed preferences.

People don’t actually behave this way. They buy *some* insurance, but not against every possible risk. They accept jobs with variable compensation. They make investments that could lose money. Every day, people accept small chances of bad outcomes in exchange for better expected value.

If people really followed maximin, they would never drive cars, never eat food they didn’t grow themselves, never leave their homes. Maximin is empirically falsified by how humans actually choose.

## The Right Answer (About Method)

Economist John Harsanyi got it right: behind the veil, rational people would maximize *expected utility* — the probability-weighted average of outcomes, with each outcome’s value measured by utility (which decreases marginally with wealth).

This gives us a framework for *evaluating* social arrangements:

> ***Compare arrangements by the sum of log(wealth) across all members.***

This doesn’t tell you what the right arrangement *is* — that depends on tradeoffs between total wealth and distribution that different people will weigh differently. But it tells you how to *compare* arrangements consistently with how people actually value outcomes.

## A Concrete Example

Let’s see how this framework evaluates different arrangements. Consider Alice and Bob.

**Scenario A: Equal but poor**

- Alice: $5,000, Bob: $5,000
- Total utility: log($5,000) + log($5,000) ≈ 3.70 + 3.70 = **7.40**

**Scenario B: Unequal but richer**

- Alice: $19,000, Bob: $1,000
- Total utility: log($19,000) + log($1,000) ≈ 4.28 + 3.00 = **7.28**

Despite having *double* the total wealth ($20,000 vs $10,000), Scenario B produces *less* total utility because the distribution is so unequal. Bob’s poverty costs more utility than Alice’s wealth creates.

**Scenario C: Unequal and richer, but less extreme**

- Alice: $15,000, Bob: $5,000
- Total utility: log($15,000) + log($5,000) ≈ 4.18 + 3.70 = **7.88**

Now we beat both previous scenarios. More total wealth than A, better distribution than B.

This illustrates the core insight: **we can often have both more wealth and better distribution**. The apparent conflict between “growing the pie” and “sharing the pie” is often illusory — it depends on *how* we grow and share.

And that “how” is exactly what marketarianism is about.

## Chapter 5: The Core Insight — Separate the Questions

So we have a framework for evaluating outcomes. But here’s what matters for policy:

**The level of redistribution and the method of redistribution are separate choices.**

You can want high redistribution achieved efficiently, or high redistribution achieved inefficiently. You can want low redistribution achieved efficiently, or low redistribution achieved inefficiently.

Marketarianism says: whatever level you choose, choose efficient methods.

This sounds obvious, but almost no one does it. Progressives who want to help the poor support minimum wage laws (inefficient). Conservatives who want economic growth support tax loopholes (inefficient). Both sides have confused their goals with particular policy tools.

## The Problem with Inefficient Redistribution

If the government taxes income at 90%, people work less and invest less. The pie shrinks so much that even the people receiving transfers might end up worse off than under a lower-tax, less-equal system.

This is “deadweight loss” — economic value that simply evaporates because of distorted incentives.

But here’s the key: **deadweight loss varies enormously depending on what you tax.** Some taxes destroy a lot of value. Some destroy none. Some actually *create* value by correcting existing distortions.

This means there’s a free lunch available: achieve the *same* level of redistribution with *less* economic damage. Or equivalently: achieve *more* redistribution for the *same* economic cost.

Progressives should be salivating. They can have more of what they want. But instead, they defend inefficient tools because they’ve confused means with ends.

## The Key Insight: Not All Taxes Are Equal

The productivity-killing effects of taxation — what economists call “deadweight loss” — vary enormously depending on *what* you tax.

**Taxes with HIGH deadweight loss:**

- Income taxes (discourages work)
- Sales taxes (discourages consumption)
- Capital gains taxes (discourages investment)
- Corporate taxes (discourages business formation)

Tax these things, and you get less of them. That’s lost economic activity, lost wealth, lost utility.

**Taxes with ZERO deadweight loss:**

- Land value taxes

Wait, what? How can a tax have zero deadweight loss?

Because land isn’t produced by human effort. It just exists. If the government taxes the value of your land, you can’t respond by “producing less land” — you didn’t produce it in the first place.

The value of land comes almost entirely from location — from being near jobs, amenities, infrastructure, other people. When a new employer opens offices nearby and your land value doubles, you didn’t do anything. That’s pure windfall, what economists call “economic rent.”

Taxing windfalls doesn’t change anyone’s behavior because behavior didn’t create the windfall. Zero deadweight loss.

**Taxes with NEGATIVE deadweight loss:**

- Carbon taxes
- Pollution taxes
- Congestion taxes

These are even better — they don’t just avoid destroying wealth, they *create* wealth.

Here’s why: when you burn fossil fuels, you impose costs on others (climate change, air pollution, health problems) that you don’t pay for. This is a “negative externality.” Because you don’t pay the full cost, you consume more than you would if prices reflected true costs. This overconsumption is itself a form of deadweight loss.

A carbon tax makes you pay the true cost, eliminating the overconsumption. It has *negative* deadweight loss — it removes existing economic distortions.

## The Priority Order

This gives us a clear strategy for *any* level of taxation:

- **First**, tax negative externalities (carbon, pollution, congestion). This has negative deadweight loss — we actually grow the pie.
- **Second**, tax economic rents (land value). This has zero deadweight loss — we collect revenue without shrinking the pie.
- **Only then**, if more revenue is needed, tax productive activity. This has positive deadweight loss — necessary but costly.

**This priority order applies whether you want small government or big government.**

If you’re a libertarian who wants minimal taxation, you should *still* want what little tax exists to come from land value and externalities rather than income.

If you’re a socialist who wants massive redistribution, you should *especially* want efficient taxes — because you need to raise a lot of revenue, and inefficient taxes would devastate the economy.

The potential gains from getting this right are enormous. Economist Joseph Stiglitz and colleagues estimated that a revenue-neutral shift to land value taxation could increase U.S. GDP by 5–6% — permanently. That’s not an argument for more or less taxation; it’s an argument for *smarter* taxation.

## What To Do With The Revenue

Here’s where progressives and conservatives both go wrong — but in ways that hurt their own goals.

**The progressive mistake:** Complicated means-tested programs that phase out as income rises, creating implicit marginal tax rates that can exceed 100%.

Oregon’s Kids’ Credit, for example, provides $1,000 per child under six, phasing out from $25,000 to $30,000 income. For a single parent with three kids, this creates a 105% marginal tax rate in that range. They’re literally better off earning $25,000 than $30,500 — the extra work makes them poorer.

This is insane. Means-testing creates poverty traps. If you’re a progressive who wants to help the poor, you should *hate* means-testing. It undermines your own goals.

**The conservative mistake:** Opposing all redistribution, which is politically unsustainable. When economic shocks hit, people who lack safety nets don’t calmly accept market outcomes — they vote for politicians who promise to restrict markets through tariffs, immigration controls, and regulation.

The choice isn’t between free markets and redistribution. It’s between free markets *with* redistribution (sustainable) and restricted markets *without* redistribution (what happens when free-market-without-safety-net systems face crises).

If you’re a conservative who wants free markets, you should support *some* efficient redistribution mechanism, even if you want the level kept low. It’s insurance against political backlash.

**The right method (regardless of level):** Universal Basic Income — the same amount to everyone, with no phase-outs, no means-testing, no bureaucratic eligibility determinations.

A flat benefit combined with efficient taxes creates automatic progressivity:

- If you own less land than average, your UBI exceeds your land tax → net beneficiary
- If you own more land than average, your land tax exceeds your UBI → net contributor

The *level* of UBI is a political choice. But the *mechanism* of UBI is just obviously better than means-tested alternatives, regardless of level.

## Part II: The Complete Framework

## Chapter 6: Marketarianism Defined

We now have all the pieces.

**Marketarianism** is the principle that, regardless of how much redistribution you support, you should want markets to be as undistorted as possible, with redistribution handled through efficient taxes and transfers rather than market interventions.

**Market equitism** is what you get when someone who favors substantial redistribution applies marketarian principles. But a **market libertarian** who favors minimal redistribution should reach for the same policy *tools* — just calibrated to a lower level.

The framework has three components:

## Component 1: Efficient Revenue (LVT + Pigovian Taxes)

Tax the unimproved value of land. Tax negative externalities like carbon emissions. These revenue sources have zero or negative deadweight loss.

The *amount* you collect is a political choice. But whatever amount you need, collect it here first.

## Component 2: Efficient Distribution (UBI)

Distribute whatever level of benefits you choose as cash, equally to everyone. No means-testing, no phase-outs, no in-kind restrictions.

The *level* of UBI is a political choice. But whatever level you choose, this mechanism is better than alternatives.

## Component 3: Free Markets (Eliminate Distortionary Interventions)

Stop using market interventions to achieve what redistribution achieves more efficiently.

- Instead of minimum wage → let wages clear, supplement low incomes with UBI
- Instead of rent control → let rents clear, fund housing through UBI, reduce speculation through LVT
- Instead of tariffs → let trade flow, support displaced workers with UBI
- Instead of agricultural subsidies → let prices clear, farmers get UBI like everyone else

**This is where the political tribes need to update.**

Progressives: minimum wage, rent control, and tariffs are *your enemies*. They’re the expensive way to achieve your goals. They shrink the pie, they create perverse incentives, and they’re less effective at helping the poor than direct transfers. If you actually care about poor people, stop defending these policies out of tribal loyalty.

Conservatives: some level of redistribution is politically necessary. Stop fighting that battle and start fighting for *efficient* redistribution. A UBI funded by land value taxes should be far more acceptable to you than the current mess of welfare bureaucracy, market interventions, and corporate subsidies.

## Why Cash Beats In-Kind Benefits

A crucial insight: UBI (cash) is superior to in-kind benefits (food stamps, housing vouchers, public libraries).

Ask the recipients: would you rather have $100 in cash or $100 worth of food stamps?

Almost everyone prefers cash, because it lets them spend on whatever they need most. Food stamps restrict choices without offsetting benefit.

In-kind benefits are essentially taking cash out of people’s hands and spending it on what *you* think they should want. This paternalism creates deadweight loss — people end up with less utility than the same dollar value in cash would provide.

This applies regardless of redistribution level. If you’re giving someone $500/month, give them cash. If you’re giving them $2000/month, give them cash. The mechanism question is separate from the level question.

## Consumption vs. Investment: A Crucial Distinction

When progressives say they want to “tax billionaires,” they’re usually motivated by a sense that inequality is too extreme — that some people have too much while others have too little.

This intuition is basically right, but the framing is wrong. What matters for human welfare isn’t how much wealth someone *has* — it’s how much they *consume*.

Think about it: Jeff Bezos’s net worth is mostly Amazon stock. That stock represents productive assets — warehouses, servers, delivery networks — that produce goods and services for millions of people. If Bezos sold all his stock tomorrow to buy yachts and mansions, he’d be converting investment into consumption. The warehouses would still exist, but now he’d be using resources that could otherwise go to… anything else.

The relevant inequality isn’t between Bezos’s net worth and yours. It’s between Bezos’s *consumption* — the yachts, the houses, the private jets — and yours.

This might seem like a minor distinction, but it has profound policy implications:

**Wealth taxes are mostly silly.** A wealth tax on Bezos forces him to sell stock every year to pay the tax. This converts investment into government spending (which might be consumption or investment depending on how it’s spent). If Bezos was going to consume his wealth anyway, fine — you’ve just accelerated the timeline. But if he was going to leave it invested, you’ve forced a conversion from productive capital to whatever the government does with it, which may or may not be better.

**Consumption taxes make more sense.** If you want to reduce inequality of actual living standards — the thing that matters — tax consumption directly. Or, more simply: let people accumulate and invest freely, tax land values (which are largely about consumption of location), tax externalities, and redistribute with UBI. The rich will naturally consume more and thus contribute more, while their investments remain productive.

**Billionaires sitting on stock aren’t the problem.** The problem, if there is one, is billionaires *consuming* resources — buying up land, using carbon-intensive transport, consuming labor and materials that could go elsewhere. Those are the things to tax.

This reframe should actually satisfy both progressives and conservatives:

- Progressives get to tax high consumption (which is what they really care about, even if they don’t articulate it that way)
- Conservatives get to preserve the incentive to invest and create productive enterprises

## The Georgist Foundation

The ideas here aren’t new. The land value tax has a particularly long pedigree, associated with the 19th-century economist Henry George. His 1879 book *Progress and Poverty* asked a simple question: why does economic progress seem to coincide with persistent poverty?

His answer: land. As an economy grows, land values rise. This enriches landowners without any productive contribution on their part. Meanwhile, taxes on labor and capital discourage the very activities that create wealth. The solution was to shift taxation entirely onto land values — capturing the “unearned increment” while leaving productive activity untaxed.

George’s ideas were wildly popular in his time — he nearly won the New York City mayoral race in 1886, outpolling Theodore Roosevelt. His supporters ranged across the political spectrum because the logic was hard to argue with: land isn’t created by human effort, so taxing it doesn’t distort behavior, and the rental value of land is largely created by the community, so it should flow back to the community.

Economists across the spectrum have endorsed variations of this logic:

- **Adam Smith** (classical liberal) noted that ground rents are a particularly suitable source of taxation
- **Milton Friedman** (libertarian) called land value tax “the least bad tax”
- **Joseph Stiglitz** (progressive) showed how LVT could increase GDP by 5–6%

The convergence is remarkable: when you actually think through the economics, LVT is obviously better than income or sales taxes, regardless of your politics. The only people who lose are landowners who’ve been capturing unearned wealth — and that’s a constituency that crosses party lines.

Georgism eventually faded from public discussion, but its core insights are foundational to marketarianism. Land value tax is Component 1 of the framework for a reason: it’s the purest example of efficient revenue.

## Chapter 7: Addressing the Objections

## “Land taxes are unfair — I bought it with money I already paid taxes on”

You didn’t create the land. You didn’t create the location value — that came from the community around you (jobs, amenities, infrastructure). When a new transit line makes your land more valuable, should you capture that value created by public investment and community development?

Current land titles ultimately trace back to conquest, theft from indigenous peoples, or arbitrary government grant. The idea that current owners have absolute moral claim to all location value is a historical accident, not a moral necessity.

## “What about retired people on fixed incomes?”

Remember the distributional data: people below roughly the 70th percentile of land wealth would be net beneficiaries under revenue-neutral LVT + UBI. The stereotypical “asset-rich, cash-poor” retiree on valuable land is, in most cases, actually quite wealthy — they just have illiquid wealth.

If they truly can’t afford the tax, they have an asset they can sell, borrow against, or downsize from. And we could grandfather existing owners if the transition seems too disruptive.

## “Property values are unpredictable — you can’t control them”

This is already true under existing property taxes. And volatility isn’t a market failure — it’s information. If land values rise because demand exceeds supply in your area, higher land taxes encourage some owners to sell or develop more intensively, which addresses the underlying shortage.

If you want someone else to bear the risk, that’s what insurance is for.

## “Won’t people just spend UBI on drugs and gambling?”

The empirical evidence from cash transfer programs worldwide consistently shows recipients spend money on exactly what you’d hope: food, housing, education, healthcare, small business investments. The “welfare queen” stereotype is largely myth.

And even if some people make choices you’d consider unwise — so what? The preference sovereignty principle says we don’t get to override informed preferences just because we think we know better. That’s paternalism, and it leads nowhere good.

## “Why give money to rich people who don’t need it?”

Because means-testing creates worse problems than it solves. Every dollar spent determining who “deserves” help is a dollar not going to help. Every phase-out creates work disincentives. Every eligibility boundary creates cliffs and traps.

Rich people will pay back their UBI many times over through land taxes and (if we still need income taxes) income taxes. The system is progressive *in net*, which is what matters.

## Chapter 8: The Political Economy Problem

Even if marketarianism is correct, democracies don’t reliably produce optimal policy.

Voters are rationally ignorant. The chance that your individual vote changes anything is essentially zero, so why spend hours researching policies? This creates systematic bias toward policies with visible, concentrated benefits and invisible, diffuse costs.

Tariffs protect visible workers (steelworkers) while imposing invisible costs on everyone (higher prices). Politicians support tariffs.

Efficient policies often have the opposite profile: diffuse benefits (everyone gains from a better economy) and concentrated costs (landowners pay LVT). This makes them politically toxic even when they’re socially optimal — regardless of whether you’re left or right.

## The Multi-Axis Political Model

The traditional left-right spectrum confuses things. Consider instead two axes:

**Horizontal:** Anti-market ← → Pro-market **Vertical:** Anti-transfer ← → Pro-transfer

This gives four quadrants:

**Top-Left: Socialist / “Pro-Government”** Anti-market, pro-transfer. Heavy regulation AND redistribution.

**Top-Right: Neoliberal / “Free Market Welfare State”** Pro-market, pro-transfer. Free markets AND redistribution.

**Bottom-Left: Reactionary Populism** Anti-market, anti-transfer. Restricts markets (tariffs, immigration controls) but opposes redistribution to “undeserving” groups.

**Bottom-Right: Libertarian / “Anti-Government”** Pro-market, anti-transfer. Free markets but minimal redistribution.

## The Marketarian Insight

Notice that the top-right and bottom-right quadrants *agree on the horizontal axis* — both want free, undistorted markets. They disagree on the vertical axis — how much redistribution.

Marketarianism says: **the horizontal axis is the technical question; the vertical axis is the political question.**

Everyone should agree on the horizontal axis. Free markets are more efficient than distorted markets. This is not a values question; it’s an empirical question with a clear answer.

The vertical axis — how much redistribution — is where legitimate political disagreement lives. Different people with different interests will reasonably disagree.

But here’s the tragedy: **both the top-left and bottom-left quadrants are filled with people who’ve confused the two axes.**

Socialists use market distortions (minimum wage, rent control) to achieve redistribution, when direct transfers would achieve more redistribution with less economic damage. They’re undermining their own goals.

Reactionary populists oppose redistribution but support market distortions (tariffs, immigration restrictions). They’re getting the worst of both worlds — less economic efficiency AND less help for struggling workers.

## The Instability Trap

America has historically occupied the bottom-right-ish area: relatively free markets, limited safety nets. But this position is unstable.

When economic shocks hit — trade competition, technological displacement, recessions — people without safety nets don’t accept their fate quietly. They vote for market restrictions: tariffs, immigration limits, industrial policy.

The research is stark. Regions most exposed to Chinese import competition after 2001 saw increased political polarization, more nativist candidates winning office, and stronger support for populist presidential candidates. The safety net failed them, so they voted to restrict the market instead.

The bottom-right drifts to the bottom-left without meaning to. Free markets without safety nets are politically unsustainable.

Countries with robust safety nets — Scandinavia, notably — also have the freest markets. This isn’t coincidence. Safety nets make market dynamism politically sustainable.

This is an argument for *some* level of redistribution being necessary for stable free markets — not an argument for any particular level. Conservatives can accept this and still argue for lower redistribution than progressives want.

## Breaking the Tribal Lock

Both American political tribes are trapped in suboptimal positions.

**Progressives** want to help the poor but often reach for market restrictions (minimum wage, rent control, tariffs) instead of direct redistribution. These are the expensive ways to achieve equity — they shrink the pie. Progressives should be market equitists: high redistribution through efficient mechanisms.

**Conservatives** want economic growth but oppose redistribution, making their preferred policies politically unsustainable. When shocks hit, they lose elections to populists who promise market restrictions. Conservatives should accept efficient redistribution (UBI) as the price of maintaining free markets, while arguing for a modest level.

Marketarianism offers a framework where both sides can get more of what they actually want:

- Progressives get more effective redistribution (same dollars, better outcomes)
- Conservatives get freer markets (no minimum wage, rent control, tariffs)

The disagreement narrows to: how big should the UBI be? That’s a legitimate political question. But it’s a much more productive debate than the current mess, where both sides are shooting themselves in the foot with inefficient policies.

## Chapter 9: Better Democratic Mechanisms

Even understanding marketarianism, how do we get democracies to implement it?

One approach is simply better education and advocacy — hence this document. But there may be deeper fixes to democratic mechanisms themselves.

## The Jury Model

Consider **election by jury**: randomly selected citizens deliberate and make electoral decisions on behalf of the population.

Two things matter for good decisions:

- **Alignment:** Decision-makers’ interests match the population’s interests
- **Competence:** Decision-makers have good information and reasoning ability

Random selection optimizes for alignment. A randomly selected jury is statistically representative of the population — it has no systematic interest divergence. Unlike elected representatives who can be captured by special interests, randomly selected citizens are just… citizens.

Deliberation optimizes for competence. Give jurors protected time, comprehensive information from multiple perspectives, expert testimony, and structured discussion. Research consistently shows ordinary citizens, given these resources, develop sophisticated understanding of complex issues.

This isn’t utopia. Athens used sortition for most government positions during its golden age. Venice used a hybrid system for 500 years. Ireland used citizens’ assemblies to break deadlocks on abortion and marriage equality. Georgia currently uses grand juries to appoint ethics boards and election boards.

The deeper point: marketarianism may require better selection mechanisms for decision-makers. The current system is too easily captured by concentrated interests who benefit from inefficient policies.

## Chapter 10: Historical Perspective — How We Got Here

We’ve now laid out the full marketarian framework in dry, technical terms. But this isn’t new. For over a century, various intellectual movements have grappled with the efficiency vs. equity puzzle, with varying degrees of coherence.

## The Georgist Tradition

The closest precursor to marketarianism is **Georgism**, named after the 19th-century economist Henry George. His 1879 book *Progress and Poverty* asked a simple question: why does economic progress seem to coincide with persistent poverty?

His answer: land. As an economy grows, land values rise. This enriches landowners without any productive contribution on their part. Meanwhile, taxes on labor and capital discourage the very activities that create wealth. The solution was to shift taxation entirely onto land values — capturing the “unearned increment” while leaving productive activity untaxed.

George’s ideas were wildly popular in his time — he nearly won the New York City mayoral race in 1886, outpolling Theodore Roosevelt. His supporters ranged across the political spectrum because the logic was hard to argue with: land isn’t created by human effort, so taxing it doesn’t distort behavior, and the rental value of land is largely created by the community, so it should flow back to the community.

Economists across the spectrum have endorsed variations of this logic:

- **Adam Smith** (classical liberal) noted that ground rents are a particularly suitable source of taxation
- **Milton Friedman** (libertarian) called land value tax “the least bad tax”
- **Joseph Stiglitz** (progressive) showed how LVT could increase GDP by 5–6%

The convergence is remarkable. When you actually think through the economics, LVT is obviously better than income or sales taxes, regardless of your politics.

## Original Neoliberalism

In the 1930s, a group of intellectuals saw that classical laissez-faire liberalism was failing. The Great Depression had discredited the idea that markets, left entirely alone, would produce good outcomes. But they also saw that socialism and fascism were monstrous alternatives. They wanted a “third way”: markets as the primary engine of prosperity, but with a strong role for government in setting rules, correcting market failures, and ensuring broad-based prosperity.

They called this project **neoliberalism**. The German economist Alexander Rüstow, one of its founders, proposed ideas that sound remarkably modern: wage subsidies instead of minimum wages (same goal, less distortion), ending corporate welfare and regulatory capture, strong competition policy. The Walter Lippmann Colloquium of 1938 brought together thinkers who agreed on the need to reform liberalism — to preserve markets while making them work for everyone.

Then the project got hijacked.

Over the following decades, the movement’s center of gravity shifted. The “social market” wing lost influence to the laissez-faire wing. By the time “neoliberalism” re-emerged in the 1980s under Reagan and Thatcher, it had become something very different: a project of deregulation and tax cuts without the corresponding commitment to strong safety nets and correcting market failures. The original vision — free markets *plus* social insurance, efficient redistribution, Pigovian taxes on externalities — got abandoned.

Today, “neoliberal” is mostly a slur, thrown at anyone vaguely centrist by critics who have no idea about the term’s origins. But the original insight remains valid: you can have free markets AND a strong safety net. You can have economic efficiency AND equity. These aren’t enemies — they’re complements, when implemented correctly.

Some have called this vision the **free market welfare state**. It’s the recognition that free markets are the best engine for creating wealth, but that markets alone won’t distribute that wealth in ways that maintain social stability or satisfy most people’s preferences. You need both: the market to grow the pie, and transfers to share it more broadly.

## Marxism and Its Blind Spots

Marx correctly identified real problems: workers could be exploited, capital could concentrate, and market economies could produce enormous inequality. His diagnosis had elements of truth.

But his prescription was catastrophically wrong. By abolishing markets and private property, Marxist regimes destroyed the very mechanism — prices — that allows efficient allocation of resources. The result was economic stagnation, massive deadweight loss, and often political tyranny as states centralized power to enforce economic planning.

The Marxist error was conflating *distribution* with *mechanism*. You can achieve egalitarian outcomes without abolishing markets. In fact, you achieve them more effectively by preserving markets (efficiency) while redistributing the gains (equity). Marxism destroyed the pie in an attempt to share it.

## Conservative Skepticism: Sowell and Public Choice

Meanwhile, conservative economists developed important critiques of government intervention. Thomas Sowell, among others, emphasized that market failures are often rarer than progressives assume, and even when real, government solutions frequently fail worse than the problems they’re meant to solve.

This is a serious objection. Regulatory capture is real. Politicians are corruptible. Bureaucracies develop their own interests. The cure can be worse than the disease.

But notice what this objection is *not* saying. Sowell isn’t claiming that monopolies don’t exist, or that externalities are fake, or that markets always produce optimal outcomes. He’s making a *practical* claim: that government interventions often fail, so we should be skeptical about proposed fixes.

A marketarian can fully accept this skepticism while maintaining that:

- Some market failures are real and severe enough to warrant correction
- Some corrections are simple enough to implement without being captured (a carbon tax is harder to corrupt than complex regulations)
- Doing redistribution efficiently (LVT + UBI) is better than doing it inefficiently (rent control + minimum wage), even if you’re skeptical about government in general

The Sowell objection is an argument for *simple, hard-to-corrupt* policies — which is exactly what marketarianism recommends.

## The Nordic Success Story

The Nordic countries — Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Finland — offer the closest real-world approximation of marketarian principles.

This surprises people because the Nordics are associated with “big government.” But look closer:

- **Highly efficient markets**: The Nordics consistently rank among the world’s freest economies. They have minimal business regulation, strong property rights, free trade, and flexible labor markets. They largely avoided the distortionary interventions (rent control, high minimum wages, heavy industry regulation) that characterize less successful welfare states.
- **Efficient taxation**: Nordic tax systems rely heavily on consumption taxes (VAT) and broad-based income taxes with relatively few deductions, rather than the narrow, loophole-ridden systems common elsewhere. Some have experimented with land value taxation.
- **Efficient redistribution**: Benefits tend to be universal rather than means-tested, reducing administrative costs and avoiding poverty traps.

The result: high economic output AND low inequality AND high life satisfaction. Not because they somehow escaped economic tradeoffs, but because they implemented redistribution efficiently enough that it didn’t destroy the productive economy.

The Nordics aren’t perfect marketarians — they have distortions too. But they’re closer than most, and their success demonstrates that the framework works.

## Thevenin-Norton Precision

Here’s what the marketarian framework gives us that these historical movements lacked: **radical precision**.

In electrical engineering, there’s a concept called Thevenin-Norton equivalence: any complex circuit can be reduced to a simple equivalent that behaves identically from the outside. You can analyze enormously complex systems using simple, exact transformations.

Marketarianism offers something similar for economic policy. Instead of vague debates about “more government” vs. “less government,” we can decompose any policy into:

- Its effect on efficiency (deadweight loss)
- Its effect on distribution (who gains, who loses)

These are mathematically separable. A policy’s distributional effects can be exactly offset by transfers, leaving only its efficiency effects. This means we can evaluate policies on their efficiency merits alone, knowing that any distributional concerns can be addressed separately.

This precision cuts through decades of confused debate. Minimum wage vs. direct transfers isn’t a values question — it’s an efficiency question with a measurable answer. Rent control vs. LVT+UBI isn’t left vs. right — it’s inefficient vs. efficient.

The historical movements got pieces of this. Georgists understood efficient taxation. Original neoliberals understood the need for both markets and redistribution. Conservatives understood government failure. Nordics stumbled onto workable implementations. But none had the complete, precise framework that separates efficiency from equity with mathematical clarity.

That’s what marketarianism offers: not a new ideology, but a cleaner way of thinking about economics that reveals the hidden agreement underneath the endless war.

## Part III: The Vision

## Chapter 11: What Victory Looks Like

Imagine marketarianism fully implemented:

**The tax system** is radically simple. Most revenue comes from land value tax and carbon taxes — neither of which distorts economic decisions. Whatever remains needed comes from a flat income tax with no deductions or exemptions.

**The benefit system** is even simpler. Every adult gets a monthly UBI payment, the same amount regardless of anything. No caseworkers, no eligibility determinations, no phase-outs. Just money.

**Markets are free.** No minimum wage (replaced by UBI). No rent control (LVT handles speculation). No tariffs (displaced workers have UBI). No agricultural subsidies. Businesses can hire, fire, restructure, automate — knowing displaced workers have a floor. Workers can take risks, start businesses, change careers — knowing they won’t starve.

**The environment is protected** through pricing, not command-and-control regulation. Want to emit carbon? Pay the social cost. This creates automatic incentives for green innovation without bureaucrats picking winners.

**Poverty effectively ends.** Not because everyone is equal, but because the floor is high enough. Some people are still much richer than others — that’s fine, even desirable as incentive. But no one lacks basic necessities.

## The Numbers

Total U.S. land value: roughly $23–25 trillion. At 5% tax rate (below full rental value): ~$1.2 trillion annually.

Carbon tax at $50/ton: ~$250 billion annually (declining as emissions fall — which is the point).

Total from efficient taxes: ~$1.4–1.5 trillion. That’s roughly $4,000 per adult per year, or $330/month, with *zero* deadweight loss.

Scale up LVT toward full rental value, add other Pigovian taxes, eliminate inefficient tax expenditures: potentially $10,000–15,000 per adult annually.

Is that utopia? No. But it’s a solid floor, achieved without sacrificing economic growth. And it’s politically sustainable because everyone benefits from the efficient economy it enables.

## Chapter 12: Getting There

## For Progressives

You want to help the poor. Great. But *how* matters.

Stop defending market restrictions as ways to help workers. Minimum wage, rent control, tariffs, occupational licensing — these are the expensive path to equity. They shrink the pie.

Advocate for cash instead. UBI is the progressive policy par excellence: largest transfer to the poorest, no strings attached, no poverty traps.

And advocate for efficient funding. LVT is the *most* progressive tax possible — land ownership is even more concentrated than income. Taxing land and distributing proceeds as UBI would be the largest redistribution in American history.

## For Conservatives

You want economic growth and individual liberty. Great.

Recognize that opposing all redistribution is politically unsustainable. When shocks hit, people without safety nets vote for market restrictions. The choice isn’t free markets vs. redistribution — it’s free markets with redistribution vs. restricted markets without.

UBI actually embodies your values better than the current welfare state. It’s simple (no bureaucracy), treats everyone equally (no picking winners), doesn’t create dependency (no phase-outs), and respects individual choice (cash, not mandates).

Milton Friedman advocated something very similar. Henry George was embraced by free-market economists. This isn’t leftism in disguise.

## For Everyone

The deepest insight is that left vs. right is a trap. Both sides fight over dividing a fixed pie when they should be working together to grow the pie while using efficient mechanisms to share it.

**Marketarianism is not a position on how much to share. It’s a position on how to share efficiently.**

The disagreement should narrow to: how big should the UBI be? How high should the LVT rate be? Those are legitimate political questions where different interests will clash. But everyone should agree on the *mechanisms* — efficient taxes, universal transfers, free markets.

The real enemies aren’t on the other side of the redistribution debate. They’re the rent-seekers who benefit from inefficient policies and exist on both left and right: landowners who oppose LVT, polluters who oppose carbon taxes, bureaucracies that would shrink under UBI, protected industries that benefit from tariffs.

These groups have captured both parties. They are the enemy. Not each other.

## Digging Deeper: Where Do These Preferences Come From?

*This section explores the evolutionary and philosophical foundations of the framework above. It’s not necessary for understanding or applying marketarianism, but it may satisfy readers curious about deeper justifications.*

## The Evolutionary Basis

When we see a bird tending to its egg at great personal cost, or humans instinctively rushing to help someone in medical distress, we’re witnessing biological phenomena that we can understand the same way we understand why moths fly toward light.

Four billion years ago, molecular replicators emerged with two properties: they could copy themselves, and those copies could mutate. Everything else — from finch beaks to moral intuitions — flows from this.

Genes that make more copies of themselves become more prevalent. That’s not a theory; it’s a tautology. And since our brains are built by genes, our preferences — including moral intuitions — are downstream of selection pressure.

## Why We’re Risk-Averse

Our ancestors faced survival challenges where resources had sharply diminishing returns. The difference between zero food and some food was life or death. The difference between adequate food and abundant food was merely comfort.

Genes that motivated risk-averse behavior around survival resources made more copies of themselves than genes that motivated risk-seeking behavior. We inherited those genes, and with them, the intuition that a dollar means more when you’re poor.

Log utility isn’t an arbitrary assumption we impose on ethics. It’s a description of preferences shaped by millions of years of selection in environments where diminishing marginal returns were a matter of life and death.

## Two Types of Altruism

The apparent altruism we observe in nature has two sources:

**Kin altruism:** When a bird protects its egg, genes are protecting probable copies of themselves in that egg. California ground squirrels give alarm calls even at personal risk because nearby squirrels likely carry the same genes. If the warning saves two close relatives, the “call” genes come out ahead even if the caller is eaten.

**Reciprocal altruism:** In ancestral environments of small groups with repeated interactions, helping others often meant helping yourself, because they would help you back. Our brains evolved emotions like gratitude, guilt, and moral outrage to facilitate this cooperation.

Recent work shows human moral cognition is well-described by Nash bargaining — maximizing the product of both parties’ gains. Evolution built us to intuitively calculate others’ benefits because people who signal cooperation get selected as partners.

## Ethics as Subjective Preference

This framework reveals that ethics isn’t about discovering eternal truths. When someone says “you shouldn’t kill people,” they’re not making a metaphysical claim about the universe. They’re expressing a preference: “I would prefer a world where people don’t kill each other.”

The is-ought problem — David Hume’s observation that you can’t derive “ought” from “is” — dissolves once we see that “ought” just means “what people prefer.” There’s no mysterious gap between facts and values; values are facts about preferences.

This doesn’t make ethics arbitrary or nihilistic. Preferences are real, they can be measured, and we can build social arrangements that satisfy them better or worse. That’s what marketarianism does: it takes the preferences people actually have (as revealed by their choices) and derives the social arrangements that best satisfy those preferences in expectation.

## The Veil of Ignorance Revisited

The thought experiment of choosing social arrangements without knowing your position isn’t just a philosophical trick. It captures something real about ethics: the recognition that “I” could have been anyone.

When you don’t know which person you’ll be, self-interest and collective interest align. The arrangements that maximize your expected utility are the arrangements that maximize expected utility across everyone. Selfish preference under identity uncertainty just *is* utilitarianism.

This is why the preference sovereignty principle works: we’re not imposing an external standard of ethics, we’re deriving ethics from what people themselves would choose if they had to bet on outcomes without knowing their position.

The whole edifice of ethics is genetic self-interest filtered through the complexity of social environments where cooperation often beats defection. Once you see this, traditional philosophical debates resolve into straightforward questions about what people actually prefer and how to satisfy those preferences efficiently.

## Conclusion: The Simple Truth

**Marketarianism** is not a position on how much redistribution there should be. It’s a position on *how* to achieve whatever level gets chosen through democratic politics.

The core claims:

- **The method question is separate from the level question.** You can favor high redistribution or low redistribution. Either way, you should want efficient methods.
- **Some methods are objectively more efficient than others.** Land value taxes beat income taxes. Pigovian taxes beat regulations. UBI beats means-tested welfare. These aren’t value judgments; they’re empirical facts about deadweight loss.
- **Almost everyone currently supports inefficient methods.** Progressives defend minimum wage, rent control, and tariffs. Conservatives defend tax loopholes and oppose all redistribution (inviting backlash that leads to market restrictions). Both are undermining their own goals.
- **Switching to efficient methods would benefit almost everyone.** The pie would be bigger. Whatever share you currently get, you’d get more of it in absolute terms. The only losers are rent-seekers: landowners capturing unearned location value, polluters externalizing costs onto others.

The efficient tools are simple: **Land value taxes. Pigovian taxes. Universal basic income.**

The *level* of each is a political choice. The *use* of each should be common ground.

*A note on where I stand: I do have views on redistribution levels — I lean toward more rather than less. But I’ve tried to separate that from the core argument here. If you’re a libertarian who wants minimal government, marketarianism still applies to you. The small amount of taxation you accept should come from land value and externalities. The small amount of benefits you accept should be distributed as cash. You’ll still disagree with progressives about levels, but you should agree on mechanisms.*

*The real enemies aren’t people who want different redistribution levels than you. They’re the rent-seekers who benefit from inefficient policies — landowners who oppose LVT, polluters who oppose carbon taxes, bureaucracies that would shrink under UBI, protected industries that benefit from tariffs. These groups have captured both parties, convincing each that inefficiency serves their interests.*

*It does not.*

# you know what georgists never talk about? the deadweight loss they create.

clay shentrup9 min read·Jan 28, 2026

--

*or: how i learned to stop worrying and love vickrey auctions*

you’ve probably heard the georgist pitch: “tax land, not buildings! land supply is perfectly inelastic, so land value tax creates zero deadweight loss!”

this is true. for land. but here’s what nobody mentions: the way you *capture* that land value can create massive deadweight loss on improvements. and traditional georgist taxation does exactly that — it creates *more* deadweight loss than regular property ownership.

i’m going to show you three different mechanisms for land value taxation, moving from maximum deadweight loss to zero. they all use the same elegant auction structure (vickrey auctions, which are beautiful and we’ll get into that). they only differ in how they split the payment between government and property owner.

but first, let’s talk about what deadweight loss actually means.

## deadweight loss: the value that just… disappears

deadweight loss is economic value that gets destroyed in a transaction. here’s a simple example:

you build a $500k house. a developer wants your land to build offices — they value the land at $2m but your house at $0 (teardown). under any reasonable market system, you’re going to lose that $500k house value because the buyer doesn’t want it.

that $500k is deadweight loss. the house still exists, it’s still valuable to *someone*, but it gets destroyed in this transaction. that loss creates an incentive problem: if there’s a decent chance of teardown, why build the nice house at all?

here’s the key insight: **this happens under regular property ownership too**. fee simple has deadweight loss! when you improve your property, you only recoup that value if the buyer also values your improvements. if they don’t, you lose it.

so the question isn’t “does l.v.t. create deadweight loss?” it’s “does l.v.t. create *more or less* deadweight loss than regular ownership?”

and here’s the uncomfortable truth georgists don’t talk about: traditional l.v.t. creates *more*.

## why traditional georgist taxation makes things worse

let’s use a concrete example. three bidders:

- **alice:** values land at $1.0m, house at $1.3m → total $2.3m (highest)
- **bob:** values land at $1.5m, house at $0.7m → total $2.2m
- **charlie:** values land at $0.9m, house at $1.0m → total $1.9m

alice should win (highest total value). under *any* efficient auction, she’ll pay the second-highest bid ($2.2m). so far so good — that’s standard auction theory going back to vickrey in 1961.

but how do we split that $2.2m between government (land tax) and incumbent (compensation for improvements)?

**pure georgist approach:** government gets the *market* land value — the second-highest land bid. that’s $1.5m (bob’s bid). incumbent gets the remainder: $0.7m.

**the problem:** alice values the house at $1.3m. incumbent receives $0.7m. **deadweight loss: $0.6m.**

under regular fee simple ownership, when alice pays $2.2m for the property, she’s mentally accounting that as “$1.0m for land (my valuation) + $1.2m for house.” the incumbent is receiving what the *winner* values things at, not what the *market* values them at.

using market land value for the split means incumbent gets less than winner values improvements at. that’s pure deadweight loss.

## enter vickrey auctions

before we get to the solutions, let’s talk about vickrey auctions because they’re genuinely beautiful and they’re the foundation for all three mechanisms.

a vickrey auction is simple: highest bidder wins, but pays the *second-highest* bid.

why is this brilliant? because it makes truthful bidding a dominant strategy. here’s why:

**if you understate your bid:** you might lose when you should have won. you valued it at $2.3m but bid $2.1m, someone else bid $2.2m, you lose. you just missed out on $0.1m of surplus ($2.3m value — $2.2m cost).

**if you overstate your bid:** doesn’t help! you still pay second-price. bid $3m instead of $2.3m? congrats, you still pay $2.2m. zero benefit.

**therefore:** just bid your true value. it’s optimal.

this is called “incentive compatibility” — the mechanism makes honesty the best strategy. no game theory phd required, just simple self-interest.

now here’s where it gets cool: we’re going to use vickrey logic for *two different auctions simultaneously* — one for the total property, and one for bare land.

## mechanism 1: pure georgist (maximum deadweight loss)

**payment split:**

- government: $1.5m (second-highest land bid)
- incumbent: $0.7m (remainder)

this is “pure” georgism — capture the true market land value. but as we showed, deadweight loss is $0.6m because alice values house at $1.3m but incumbent only gets $0.7m.

why would anyone choose this? ideological commitment to capturing “true” market land value. but you’re paying for it in efficiency.

## mechanism 2: partial fix (some deadweight loss)

**payment split:**

- government: $1.0m (alice’s stated land value)
- incumbent: $1.2m (remainder)

better! now incumbent gets $1.2m instead of $0.7m. but alice values house at $1.3m, so there’s still $0.1m deadweight loss.

where does this $0.1m loss come from? vickrey second-price discount. alice values total at $2.3m but pays $2.2m — she gets $0.1m surplus. in this split formula, that entire discount comes from the house component, so incumbent loses it.

## mechanism 3: fee simple equivalent (zero deadweight loss)

**payment split:**

- incumbent: $1.3m (alice’s stated house value)
- government: $0.9m (remainder)

perfect! incumbent receives exactly what alice values the house at. zero deadweight loss.

this is economically identical to fee simple ownership. when you sell under fee simple, buyer pays you an amount and mentally accounts it as “land + house.” here, we’re making that accounting explicit: incumbent receives what winner values improvements at.

**but wait — doesn’t government get less?** yes! $0.9m instead of $1.0m or $1.5m. that’s the tradeoff: zero deadweight loss means government captures less of the winner’s surplus. but government still captures all *land appreciation* (more on this below).

## the crucial enforcement mechanism: m.a.d.

here’s the question you should be asking: “why would alice state her land and house values honestly? she pays $2.2m either way!”

great question. this is where the mechanism gets elegant.

**if incumbent refuses alice’s offer:**

- improvements get destroyed
- bare land goes to highest *land* bidder (bob with $1.5m)
- bob pays second-highest land price ($1.0m — alice’s bid)

this creates **mutually assured destruction** (m.a.d.):

- winner loses: gets nothing instead of property worth $2.3m
- incumbent loses: gets $0 instead of $1.3m

so both parties prefer acceptance. but here’s the key: this *also* creates incentive for alice to bid her land value honestly.

**why alice should bid land honestly:**

suppose alice doesn’t win the total property — say bob had bid $2.4m total instead. bob makes an offer to incumbent. if incumbent refuses, we go to the bare land auction.

alice bid land at $1.0m. bob wins bare land at… alice’s bid! second-price vickrey.

**if alice understated** (claimed $0.5m): bob wins bare land at $0.5m. alice helped bob pay less and gets nothing herself.

**if alice overstated** (claimed $2.0m): alice might win bare land herself, paying second-price ($1.5m from bob). but alice only values bare land at $1.0m! she’d be paying $1.5m for land worth $1.0m to her. loss of $0.5m.

**therefore:** state true land value. standard vickrey logic applies to the bare land auction.

this is why component bidding matters! without the bare land auction fallback, alice would have no reason to accurately split her bid. the enforcement mechanism creates honest revelation of *both* total value *and* components.

## land appreciation: government captures it all

here’s the magic: under all three mechanisms, government captures land appreciation.

suppose a subway gets built nearby. land values increase $1m across the board:

- alice: land $2.0m, house $1.3m → total $3.3m
- bob: land $2.5m, house $0.7m → total $3.2m

under mechanism 3:

- incumbent: $1.3m (house value unchanged)
- government: $1.9m (was $0.9m, up $1.0m!)

government captures the full $1m land appreciation. incumbent’s house payment is unchanged because house value didn’t change.

this is the whole point of land value taxation: land appreciation comes from community investment (subway, schools, economic development), not owner investment. government should capture it. under fee simple, owner captures it — that’s the inefficiency we’re fixing.

## implementation: continuous bidding

in practice, this would work like:

- **continuous bidding rounds** (monthly or quarterly)
- you submit: monthly land rent + house value
- system converts rent to present value using 30-year treasury rate
- if you’re outbid, you get 30 days notice
- new winner negotiates with you (or you negotiate with them)
- if you refuse, improvements destroyed, bare land auctioned

the 30-year treasury rate is the only “imposed” parameter — everything else discovered through competitive bidding. and treasury rate is market-determined, not bureaucrat-chosen, so it’s just a technical conversion factor.

## why this matters: no assessors needed

the traditional l.v.t. problem: how do bureaucrats figure out what land is worth separate from buildings?

they can’t! it’s expensive, imprecise, politically fraught. that’s why l.v.t. has never been implemented at scale despite 145 years of advocacy since henry george.

this mechanism solves it: **the market tells you**. competitive bidding reveals:

- true land values (through bare land auction)
- true improvement values (through component bidding)
- who should own what (through total value bidding)

no assessors. no appeals. no political fights over valuations. just pure market discovery through auction mechanisms we’ve understood since vickrey’s 1961 paper.

## the uncomfortable truth

here’s what i want georgists to grapple with: **even perfect georgist taxation creates deadweight loss**.

the claim is always “land supply is inelastic, therefore zero deadweight loss.” true for land! but when you capture *market* land value (mechanism 1), you’re necessarily creating deadweight loss on improvements because incumbent receives less than winner values improvements at.

this isn’t a failure of markets or auctions. it’s inherent to the georgist goal of capturing market land assessment rather than winner’s personal valuation.

mechanism 3 solves this by switching goals: instead of “capture market land value,” we aim for “replicate fee simple efficiency while capturing land appreciation.”

government gets less of winner’s surplus in any given transaction. but government still captures all land appreciation (which is the big prize), and we eliminate deadweight loss on improvements (which preserves investment incentives).

## revenue potential

quick back-of-envelope: gaffney (2009) estimates u.s. land value at $23 trillion. at 5% annual return, that’s $1.15 trillion per year in land rent.

that’s 6% of g.d.p. you could:

- fund substantial u.b.i.
- replace distortionary taxes on labor and capital
- invest in infrastructure and public goods

even mechanism 3 (giving owners most favorable terms) captures this through land appreciation over time. every time property changes hands, government gets the residual after compensating improvements.

## comparison to harberger taxation

some of you might know about harberger taxation (self-assessed property values, forced sale at stated price). how’s this different?

**harberger:** you declare total property value, pay percentage tax (say 7%), anyone can force-purchase at your declared price.

**our mechanism:** bid components separately, pay second-price, no bureaucrat-chosen tax rate.

key differences:

- **no imposed tax rate.** harberger requires bureaucrat to choose that 7%. we use only market-determined values plus treasury rate for technical conversion.
- **separates land from improvements.** harberger taxes total property value — that includes improvements! our mechanisms explicitly separate them.
- **second-price vs first-price.** we use vickrey (pay second-highest), harberger uses first-price (pay your own stated value). second-price makes truthful bidding simpler and more obviously optimal.

both achieve the same underlying goal (capture land value), but our mechanism is cleaner and more explicitly separates land from improvements.

## choosing between mechanisms

**mechanism 1:** choose if you’re committed to “pure” georgist theory and accept maximum deadweight loss as the cost of capturing true market land value.

**mechanism 2:** middle ground. less deadweight loss than mechanism 1, captures more government revenue than mechanism 3.

**mechanism 3:** optimal for economic efficiency. zero deadweight loss, perfect fee simple replication, captures all land appreciation. this is what i recommend.

the progression from 1→2→3 shows how to move from pure georgist theory (with its unacknowledged problems) to fee simple equivalence (with zero deadweight loss) while maintaining the key benefit: public capture of land appreciation.

## the broader point

this isn’t just about land taxation. it’s about mechanism design.

we took a 145-year-old problem (how to assess land separately from improvements) and solved it through competitive bidding. we took an unacknowledged issue (georgist l.v.t. creates deadweight loss) and fixed it by switching from market assessment to winner assessment.

the tools are vickrey auctions (1961), m.a.d. dynamics (cold war era), and present value calculations (econ 101). nothing exotic. just careful thinking about incentives and efficiency.

and we ended up with something better than where we started: fee simple efficiency + public land value capture. best of both worlds.

**tl;dr:** traditional georgist l.v.t. creates more deadweight loss than regular property ownership (georgists never talk about this). we present three auction mechanisms with decreasing deadweight loss. mechanism 3 achieves zero deadweight loss (perfect fee simple replication) while capturing all land appreciation for public revenue. the key is using vickrey auctions for both total property and bare land, with bare land fallback creating honest component bidding. no bureaucratic assessment needed — market discovers all values.

# wealth and welfare

clay shentrup4 min read·Apr 1, 2020

--

the more wealth we have, the more we can improve human welfare. this is central to conservative adages like, “a rising tide lifts all boats”.

but the more wealth a given *individual* has, the less benefit they get from each additional unit of wealth. we say that wealth has [decreasing *marginal utility*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_utility#Law_of_Diminishing_marginal_utility). a reasonable approximation is that `utility = log₂(wealth)`, so a person gets about the same utility improvement from doubling their wealth no matter how much wealth they have.

this decreasing marginal utility means that we can not only improve welfare by *increasing* wealth, but also by *redistributing* wealth. that is, we can make people better off by *growing* the pie, but also by *sharing* the pie more equally. for instance, imagine a two-person micro-society comprised of alice and bob. if bob and alice both have 5000$, that’s a utility of 24.58. but suppose bob has 1000$ and alice has 19,000$. that’s a utility of 24.18 — a *lower* net utility despite having *double* the available wealth.

this is the intuition behind the progressive adage “tax the rich”. if transferring wealth from high to low increases total utility, then taxing those with the most and redistributing downward seems like an obvious welfare improvement. but this framing obscures a critical question: does the mechanism of taxation itself destroy wealth in the process? if taxing productive activity shrinks the pie while redistributing it, the net welfare effect is ambiguous. the goal should be redistribution instruments that minimize that destruction — which is where the choice of *what* to tax becomes everything.

## conflicting goals

but confiscating wealth decreases the incentive to create wealth in the first place. the lost productivity caused by taxing productive behavior is a form of what economists call [deadweight loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deadweight_loss). the legendary venture capitalist paul graham even [penned one of his classic essays](http://www.paulgraham.com/ineq.html) on the idea that societies which generate a lot of wealth must *by definition* have a certain degree of inequality.

and so maximizing human welfare requires a delicate balance between incentivizing wealth creation (which entails, among other things, keeping taxes reasonably *low*), and redistributing wealth from richer to poorer — which entails making taxes fairly high. we want to maximize something like the product of “amount of pie” and “equal sharing of pie” — in spite of the fact that these two goals seem to be in conflict. so how do we do that?

## efficient taxes

the trick is to have as much of our tax base as possible come from taxes which don’t decrease productivity. there are two categories that achieve this.

## rents aka windfalls

sometimes people grow richer through pure luck rather than as the result of their productive work. for instance, suppose a large employer opens a new office near your plot of land, sending the rental value of that land through the roof. unlike a real estate project, which requires planning and hard work, this “windfall” comes through pure dumb luck.

the upshot of this is that if the government taxes away that windfall, it *by definition* won’t decrease your productive output. it *cannot* affect your incentives, because you didn’t do anything to produce that value. in this case, the appropriation of those earnings is called a [*land value tax*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_value_tax). we ideally want to tax away 100% of the *unimproved* value of land, to make as much of our tax base as possible free from the destructive effects of deadweight loss.

## negative externalities

an arguably even better target for taxation is *negative externalities*. an example would be a tax on carbon dioxide emissions. the problem of such emissions are that they create a cost that’s paid by society (in the form of drought, fires, higher grocery prices, etc.) rather than by the individual emitting the carbon dioxide. because the individual emitting the carbon doesn’t pay the cost, it feels to them as if they’re being *paid* to burn more fossil fuels — it literally has the exact same effect as a subsidy. and subsidies lead to overconsumption, which creates another form of deadweight loss.

for example, you might consume 10 dollars worth of gasoline, even though you think it’s only worth 6 dollars, because the lack of a carbon tax makes it cost only 6 dollars. you’re effectively paying 10 dollars for something worth 6 dollars, because the government is picking up the 4 dollar remaining tab. this overconsumption is tantamount to *shrinking* the pie.

the solution is to “internalize” the negative externality by means of what’s known as a *pigovian tax*. in this case, we want to charge a carbon tax on emissions of carbon dioxide, and tax other pollutants such as methane as well. this is even better than “rent” taxes like land value tax, because not only does it not *add* deadweight loss, it actually *removes* deadweight loss. in other words, it has negative deadweight loss.

## taxing productivity

only once we’ve exhausted all possible windfall taxes and pigovian taxes do we want to tax “generated” wealth that arises from productive activity. in this way, we keep taxes on productive activity to a bare minimum.

further, we want these “productivity taxes” to be [*flat* not progressive](https://www.ubicenter.org/us-flat-tax), due to the simplicity and other advantages. ideally, the rate is 100% of the externalized cost or rental value, in the case of pigovian taxes or land value taxes respectively.

a better way to make the tax system progressive overall is to distribute a flat negative tax amount, a.k.a. a “universal income”, rather than have a differential tax rate. a constant universal subsidy combined with a flat marginal tax rate creates a net-progressive tax regime.

# why kaldor-hicks efficiency is useless

clay shentrup3 min read·May 12, 2026

--

## why kaldor-hicks efficiency is useless

kaldor-hicks efficiency is the most widely used criterion in cost-benefit analysis, law & economics, and applied welfare economics. it says a policy is efficient if the winners could *hypothetically* compensate the losers and still come out ahead. the compensation doesn’t have to actually happen — it just has to be theoretically possible.

this criterion is incoherent. here’s why.

## three fruits, two people

alice has an apple and an orange. bob has a banana. their preferences:

- alice: banana > apple > orange
- bob: orange > apple > banana

**direction 1:** transfer the apple from alice to bob. bob could hypothetically compensate alice by giving her his banana. bob ends up with the apple, which he prefers to the banana he gave up. alice ends up with the orange and banana, having traded an apple for a banana she values more. both better off. kaldor-hicks efficient. ✓

**direction 2:** give the apple from bob back to alice. alice could hypothetically compensate bob by giving him the orange. bob ends up with the orange, which he prefers to the apple he lost. alice ends up with the apple, which she prefers to the orange she gave up. both better off. kaldor-hicks efficient. ✓

the same transfer passes the kaldor-hicks test going *both ways*. the criterion literally cannot tell you which direction to go. this is known formally as the scitovsky paradox (tibor scitovsky, 1941), and it has never been resolved.

## why this matters

kaldor-hicks is the foundation of cost-benefit analysis in government policy, the “wealth maximization” approach that swept through american law schools, and much of applied welfare economics. it is used constantly to justify real policy decisions affecting real people.

and it’s circular.

the reason is simple. kaldor-hicks asks whether *hypothetical* compensation is possible — not whether it actually happens. but whether compensation is possible depends on the specific preferences and endowments at each allocation, which change with the allocation itself. so the test gives different answers depending on which state you start from, and both answers can be “efficient.”

## what to use instead

samuelson (1950) proposed a stricter criterion that avoids the paradox entirely: instead of asking whether winners could compensate losers at one specific allocation, ask whether the policy change expands the entire **utility possibilities frontier** — the set of all achievable utility distributions — beyond the original one. if the new frontier lies entirely outside the old one, then *some* redistribution exists that makes everyone better off, and the reversal can’t also pass the test. no circularity.

most real policies don’t pass the samuelson criterion strictly, because they expand the frontier in some dimensions and contract it in others. that’s an honest result. it means the policy creates winners and losers and you can’t pretend otherwise. kaldor-hicks “solved” this by just ignoring it — declaring the policy efficient anyway because the winners *could* compensate the losers, even when they never do.

the practical upshot: use **pareto efficiency** as the standard and **actual compensation** as the mechanism. if a policy change creates net gains, use those gains to actually compensate the losers through cash transfers (UBI) funded by efficient taxes (land value tax, pigouvian taxes) that create zero or negative deadweight loss. don’t just hypothesize the compensation and call it science.

the entire kaldor-hicks apparatus exists because economists in 1939 decided that actual compensation was “too impractical”. but we have the tools now. taxes and transfers are not impractical. they’re just less convenient for economists who want to recommend policies without worrying about who gets hurt.

as upton sinclair put it: “it is difficult to get a man to understand something, when his salary depends on his not understanding it.”

# taxing rent fixes nothing you actually care about a reply to “rent: a new framework”

clay shentrup8 min read·May 30, 2026

--

there is one tax that economists across the spectrum accept as efficient. milton friedman called the land value tax the least bad tax. plenty of economists to his left reach for it too. the reason is specific, and [“rent: a new framework”](https://www.liberalcurrents.com/rent-a-new-framework/) borrows the prestige of that one tax and stretches it over a dozen things that share none of its properties. once you see why the land tax works, most of the essay’s prescriptions point the wrong way.

start with what the essay gets right, because it gets a lot right. every state banning direct auto sales is a cartel statute with no safety rationale. licensing a hair braider as a cosmetologist protects incumbents and nobody else. residency-slot caps manufacture a doctor shortage. pharma evergreening milks expired innovation. exclusionary zoning is artificial scarcity stacked on top of natural scarcity. the diagnosis of each is right and the proposed repeals are right. the problem is the theory tying them together, and what that theory implies about the cure.

the essay treats all rent as one disease with one cure: recover it for the public. for land, that works. for almost everything else on the list, recovering the rent does nothing about the harm the essay says it cares about.

**why the land tax is special**

land supply is fixed. a tax on the rental value of land cannot change how much land exists, so it cannot change anyone’s behavior at the margin. there is no quantity to shrink in response to the tax. the charge is capitalized into a lower purchase price the day it is announced, so it cannot be dodged by selling. the harm of untaxed land rent is about who pockets socially created value, not about anything being misallocated. the land market already allocates land roughly efficiently under private ownership. the tax changes the distribution, not the allocation. that is the whole basis of the efficiency claim, and it depends entirely on supply being fixed.

**monopoly is the opposite case**

a monopoly makes its money by producing less than the competitive quantity and charging more. the damage to society is the trades that never happen. the buyers priced out, who would have paid a competitive price and now go without. the monopolist’s profit is a transfer from the buyers who remain. the deadweight loss is the vanished trades, and it is a separate quantity from the profit.

now tax the profit. a tax on the monopolist’s profit does not move its price or the quantity it sells, because it does not touch marginal revenue or marginal cost. the firm restricts output to the identical point it did before. the treasury is richer and the foregone trades are still foregone. to repair a monopoly you have to get more produced: break the firm up, strip the entry barrier, push price toward cost. each of those enlarges output. recovering the rent leaves output exactly where the monopolist set it. the essay proposes the output-enlarging remedies in its policy section, which is the signal that its own cure has quietly walked away from its own framing.

**legal rent makes the contradiction sharp**

the dealer laws, the residency caps, the braiding license. in every case the rent exists because the state blocks entry. remove the block and the rent evaporates and consumers capture the surplus directly, with no detour through the treasury. tax the rent instead and you turn the government into a financial partner in the barrier, with a standing fiscal interest in keeping the dealership cartel alive. recover-the-rent and repeal-the-barrier are not two settings on the same dial. they pull against each other, and the essay never notices it is recommending both.

**the land-banking mistake**

the policy section proposes a second, higher rate on “unimproved and speculatively held” land, with “escalating carrying costs until development becomes the rational choice.” this is the negative-deadweight-loss claim, and the example it rests on does not support it. picture a downtown lot that earns a modest return as parking but would be worth far more as a building site. the story says a land tax shrinks the holder’s return until selling beats holding, so the lot finally gets developed and society banks a productivity gain. the flaw is in the setup. if the lot really is worth more developed than parked, the owner already has every reason to develop it, or to sell to someone who will, tax or no tax. that incentive was there before the assessor showed up. the tax does not summon the move to higher use. it only changes who collects the rent along the way. the supposed efficiency gain is one incentive counted twice, which is why the tax is neutral, with zero deadweight loss and not negative. (i walk through this example, and the one narrow case where a genuine loss does survive, here.) a penalty rate aimed at idle land is chasing a problem the ordinary profit motive already handles, and it pays for the gesture by reintroducing the use-dependent distortion a land tax is prized for not having, plus the assessment fight over whether a parking lot, a working farm, or a generous backyard counts as idle.

**the harder-work fallacy**

jeremiah johnson’s version of the negative-deadweight-loss claim runs the other way: taxing the owner of vacant land makes him put it to more productive use. the tax makes him poorer, a poorer man values each remaining dollar more, so he develops the land or sells to someone who will. the trade happens, so the result must be better.

but consider what a trade can actually prove. the only allocation we can *know* is inefficient is one that would not survive a voluntary trade. a swap both parties accept freely is a pareto improvement: each comes out ahead by their own lights, so we can be sure welfare rose. we cannot read minds or measure utilities, so a pareto improvement is the only hard evidence of a gain we ever get. other reallocations might raise utility too, but we have no way to know it, and that is the whole problem.

the income effect does not clear that bar. it forces a trade by changing the endowment, not by surfacing a better allocation. take away your apples until your marginal utility for apples drops far enough that you will swap an orange for an apple. you make the swap. it is a pareto improvement over the fruit bowl i left you with, not over the one you started with. i manufactured it by making you poorer.

that is jeremiah’s argument exactly. some allocation y1 may be a pareto improvement over the starting point x1. some y2 may be a pareto improvement over the post-tax point x2. but forcing x1 down to x2, so the trade from x2 to y2 can happen, says nothing about whether y2 beats x1. we have no evidence the developed land is more productive than the land left alone. we know only that the owner traded up from a spot we pushed him into, and trading up from a loss does not undo the loss. (i take the same claim apart with a worked utility example here.)

**the definition swallows the reward for risk**

rent is defined as returns from position rather than competitive value creation. that definition cannot separate a monopoly rent from a quasi-rent: the temporary above-normal return that pays an investor back for sinking money into something risky and new. that return is the thing that got the risky thing built. tax it away after it appears and the next one does not get built. the essay concedes that building a payment network is real value creation, then proposes capping the return, with no account of how large a return was needed beforehand to call the network into being. a theory of rent that inspects returns only after they show up cannot tell a toll from a payback, and that distinction is the entire policy question.

**“still profitable after the cap” is not a measurement**

the essay treats the eu and australian interchange caps as proof that regulators measured the rent: the processors stayed profitable, so the difference must have been extraction. survival under a price ceiling shows nothing of the kind. a cap can claw back real rent while also killing efficient activity and shifting costs onto cardholders through thinner rewards, which is the documented european outcome. a firm can clear a cap that also destroys genuine value. profitability afterward tells you the firm survived. it does not tell you the capped amount was pure rent.

**temu cuts the other way**

the essay offers temu as a natural experiment in how much rent middlemen extract. but retail and grocery are among the most competitive, thinnest-margin businesses in the economy, with discounters entering constantly. by the essay’s own test, where rent is the return competition fails to erode, those supply-chain margins are mostly not rent. temu’s lower prices come from a different product mix, from arbitraging the duty-free treatment of small direct shipments that has since been curtailed, and from moving friction onto the buyer in wait times and returns. if the intermediary margins really were rent, the essay’s own test says they would already be gone.

**marx is set dressing**

the piece bills itself as a marxist essay. the analytical work is entirely marginalist: supply elasticity, output held below the competitive level, price above marginal cost, returns competed down over time. surplus value and the labor theory of value add nothing those tools do not already supply, and they hand a hostile reader a free target. cut the classical scaffolding and the argument gets sturdier and harder to wave away.

**what’s actually left**

strip out the unifying theory and a sound program survives, but it is not one program. for land and spectrum, where supply is fixed, recover the rent. the tax is efficient, the gain is fairness, and the bonus is the chance to retire taxes that do cause distortion. for monopoly, licensing, patents, and chokepoints, supply is not fixed and the harm is suppressed output, so the cure is to enlarge output by tearing down the barrier, and recovering the rent is beside the point at best and counterproductive at worst. “set the market free” is the right banner for the second group and the wrong one for the first, because the land market is already allocating land fine. taxing land rent changes who holds the money, not how the land gets used.

the essay’s instinct, tax the rent, is right for exactly one of its four categories: the one where supply is fixed. for the other three, the thing you actually care about is the output that never gets produced, and you cannot tax that back into existence. you have to let it be made.

# understanding social utility through harsanyi’s argument

## introduction

clay shentrup2 min read·Apr 16, 2023

--

people often debate whether it's possible to sum individual utilities to create a social utility. there are many traditional rebuttals to skepticism of interpersonal utility comparisons, usually materialist in nature, claiming that utility should ultimately be quantifiable — like the number of dopamine molecules in one's brain. however, for practical purposes, we can avoid these esoteric arguments and look at the issue from the perspective of a randomly selected member of society. this person would prefer the state of the world that maximizes net utility, as it is the state most likely to maximize their own expected utility.

### the analogy of currency bags

to illustrate this point, let's consider an analogy. imagine you have two bags containing notes of various currencies, such as yen, euros, or dollars. you are allowed to pick a random bill from one of the two bags, knowing only their average bill size. naturally, you would want to choose the bag with the highest average, even if it might actually be worse for you because it contains a lower-value currency. from an expectation standpoint, it's still the better option. it *does not matter* that the currencies are of differing denominations and thus “cannot be summed” to produce a specific comparable value. it works *in expectation*.

### random noise

another way to think of it is, imagine you write down your utilities for several different outcomes, and we pick two random probability distributions (“lotteries”) of those outcomes. you will want us to use the lottery that gives you the highest sum. but now say we add random numbers to the different outcomes. you will still want us to pick the outcome with the highest expected score, because it’s still the highest expected utility for you *in your own utility scale*. in a group of people, all the other members’ utilities act like random noise. you still just want whatever outcome maximizes the sum of utilities because it maximizes your *own* expected utility, given all the other utilities act like random noise from your perspective.

### harsanyi's argument and the concept of social welfare

this is similar to the argument proposed by j.c. harsanyi in a 1953 article. he suggested that "optimizing social welfare" means "picking the state of the world all individuals would prefer if they were in a state of uncertainty about their identity." in other words, if you are equally likely to be anyone, your expected utility would be the summed utility in the world divided by the number of people - that is, the average utility. this idea supports the notion that social utility is, in fact, averaging.

### conclusion

by examining harsanyi's argument, we can gain a better understanding of social utility from a practical perspective. while there are certainly more complex philosophical debates surrounding this concept, harsanyi's approach allows us to appreciate the value of maximizing social welfare in a way that is accessible and relevant to everyday life. his insights highlight the importance of considering the collective well-being of society, rather than focusing solely on individual utilities.

# the schöntrup index: a more nuanced measure of economic inequality

clay shentrup5 min read·Jan 13, 2025

--

*pronunciation: “shurn-troop” — the ‘ö’ sounds like the ‘u’ in “burn”, and the ‘up’ sounds like “oop” in “troop”*

## historical context

the schöntrup index is distinctive among economic metrics in that it directly incorporates the principle of diminishing marginal utility through its logarithmic transformation of incomes. while g.d.p. per capita simply averages incomes linearly (treating a dollar the same whether it goes to a rich or poor person), the schöntrup index acknowledges that an additional dollar means more to someone with less money. this makes it uniquely suited for welfare comparisons across societies with different income distributions.

## the mathematical foundation

the schöntrup index is calculated by taking the arithmetic mean of the base-2 logarithm of all post-tax and post-transfer individual incomes in a society, normalized to 2017 u.s. dollars using purchasing power parity. this approach has several key advantages over the gini coefficient:

- the logarithmic scale naturally captures the diminishing marginal utility of income, reflecting how an additional dollar means more to someone earning $10,000 than to someone earning $100,000.
- using base-2 logarithms makes the results more intuitively interpretable — each one-point increase in the index represents a doubling of income.
- the p.p.p. adjustment ensures meaningful international comparisons by accounting for differences in local purchasing power.

## empirical advantages

while the gini coefficient effectively captures relative inequality, it fails to account for absolute living standards. a society where everyone earns $2 per day would have a perfect gini score of 0, despite endemic poverty. the schöntrup index, by contrast, would immediately flag this as problematic through a very low score.

consider two example countries, each with a simple two-tier income distribution (in 2017 u.s. dollars p.p.p.):

country a:

- bottom 50%: $20,000
- top 50%: $80,000 this yields a schöntrup index of 15.3 and a gini coefficient of 0.30.

country b:

- bottom 50%: $5,000
- top 50%: $20,000 this yields a schöntrup index of 13.3 and a gini coefficient of 0.30.

this comparison perfectly illustrates the key difference between these measures. both countries have identical gini coefficients (0.30) because they have the same relative inequality (the top group earns 4 times more than the bottom group). however, country a’s higher absolute income levels result in a schöntrup index that’s 2 points higher. this demonstrates how the schöntrup index captures both the distribution and absolute level of prosperity in a society, while the gini coefficient only measures relative inequality.

## policy implications

the schöntrup index encourages policymakers to focus on both growth and distribution simultaneously. unlike the gini coefficient, which might incentivize policies that reduce inequality at the expense of overall economic development, the schöntrup index rewards balanced approaches that lift the entire income distribution.

this has profound implications for development economics. a rising schöntrup index indicates genuine progress in living standards, while a stagnant or declining index — even with stable or improving gini coefficients — suggests the need for policy intervention.

## real-world applications

recent applications of the schöntrup index have revealed interesting patterns in economic development:

- rapidly developing economies often show faster growth in their schöntrup indices than their g.d.p. per capita would suggest, indicating broad-based economic gains.
- advanced economies with strong social safety nets tend to maintain stable schöntrup indices even during economic downturns, suggesting effective redistribution mechanisms.
- countries with resource-dependent economies frequently show volatile schöntrup indices, highlighting the importance of economic diversification.

# Abolishing Public Parking: The Economic Case

clay shentrup2 min read·Nov 15, 2024

--

Inarimae, Japan pop 2100

The economic inefficiencies of in-kind government benefits are well-documented. One of the most egregious examples is public parking. By providing free or subsidized parking spots, governments are conferring an in-kind benefit that is often worth far less to citizens than the actual market value of that land.

The “gift card fallacy” illustrates this dynamic. Imagine an employer offering a $100/month parking spot. If an employee would only be willing to pay $70 for that spot, there is $30 in “dead weight loss” — the benefit is worth $70 to the employee, not the full $100 that the employer is spending.

In this case, a more efficient solution would be for the employer to simply give the employee $90 in cash. The employee receives $90 in value instead of just $70, while the employer spends $90 instead of $100 — a clear win-win.

This same principle applies to public parking. Citizens may only value many public parking spots at a fraction of the land’s true market price. By selling or renting out those spaces and providing citizens the cash equivalent, governments could unlock significantly more economic value.

The transition could happen gradually. Governments could start by instituting a land value tax on public parking, using the revenue to fund a universal basic income (UBI) or broad-based tax cuts. This would allow citizens to decide how to best utilize the value, rather than being locked into an in-kind parking benefit.

Importantly, this approach is more equitable than the status quo. Public parking subsidies disproportionately benefit wealthier car owners, while lower-income individuals without vehicles receive no such benefit. Converting to a cash-based system would be effectively progressive.

Furthermore, eliminating public parking opens the door to numerous secondary benefits. It would improve pedestrian safety and urban livability, as car owners pay the full cost of car ownership rather than having it subsidized. It would also free up valuable land for more productive uses.

Many countries, like Japan, have already largely abolished public parking, recognizing the inherent inefficiencies. As governments seek to maximize the value of public resources, eliminating public parking in favor of a cash-based system is an idea worth serious consideration.

# Opportunity Needs an Economic Constitution

clay shentrup10 min read·Sep 22, 2026

--

The thing that first struck me about Opportunity was that its policies do not look like a random collection of centrist compromises. They look like the downstream consequences of a fairly specific branch of economics.

Once I learned the party’s history, that made perfect sense. [Gareth Morgan was a trained economist](https://www.nzherald.co.nz/nz/gareth-morgans-the-opportunities-party-officially-registered/FGNCFUHEFGJF6PD54W53HDIZAA/), and [Geoff Simmons, another economist who had worked for the Morgan Foundation, was deeply involved in developing the party’s early policies](https://www.nzherald.co.nz/nz/the-opportunities-party-to-contest-2020-election-after-all/KOFOPS7MX2WCZLP3P32WFPZXSQ/). A lot of what looks unusual about Opportunity makes much more sense once you realize that economists helped build the thing from the ground up.

Land value taxation. Universal cash. Pigouvian pricing. Housing liberalization. Markets where markets work; intervention where they do not. These are not random “centrist” positions. They are highly consistent with a particular way of thinking about welfare, incentives, market failure, and efficient redistribution.

That coherence is valuable. My concern is that a party can inherit good policies without preserving the intellectual machinery that produced them. Eventually somebody asks: why not a wealth tax? Why not subsidize this industry? Why not means-test this benefit? Why not make this service free? Without a common framework for answering those questions, a coherent system gradually becomes a collection of intuitions.

There is a fairly simple framework underneath a lot of this.

## Start with welfare

In Wealth and Welfare, I start with a useful approximation from welfare economics: suppose utility is roughly logarithmic in consumption. Then social welfare looks something like:

**W = sum log(c_i)**

where c_i is each person’s consumption.

You do not need to believe logarithmic utility is literally exact. The important implication is that both the size and distribution of the pie matter. More consumption generally raises welfare, but because of diminishing marginal utility, another $1,000 matters much more to someone consuming $30,000 a year than to someone consuming $3 million.

So if you can transfer resources from richer people to poorer people without shrinking the pie, there is a strong welfare argument for doing it. The interesting problem is that redistribution often does shrink the pie because taxes and subsidies change people’s marginal choices.

That is where optimal tax theory begins.

Suppose producing something costs $10 and somebody values it at $11. There is a mutually beneficial trade available. Now impose a $2 tax wedge. The trade disappears. The government does not even collect the tax because the transaction never happens. Society has simply lost $1 of surplus.

That is deadweight loss.

Taxes can change whether people work another hour, make another investment, construct another building, or complete another trade. So the question is not merely how much redistribution we want. It is how to achieve that distribution while destroying as little value as possible.

The Second Welfare Theorem gives us the idealized version: redistribute endowments, then let competitive markets handle allocation. Real governments cannot implement perfect individualized lump-sum transfers, so optimal tax theory is largely about getting as close to that ideal as practical.

Once you look through that lens, a lot of Opportunity’s policies start to fall into place. And it is striking how often economists following the same welfare-and-incentives logic arrive at something similar. Consider, for example, the Niskanen Center’s classic essay, [“The Free-Market Welfare State”](https://www.niskanencenter.org/the-free-market-welfare-state-preserving-dynamism-in-a-volatile-world/).

## The Gift Card Fallacy

The same logic applies to the spending side.

Suppose government can give somebody $110 in cash. Instead it spends $110 providing something that person values at only $100. Ten dollars of welfare has disappeared.

I call this The Gift Card Fallacy.

An in-kind benefit is basically a gift card. Food assistance is money that can only be spent on food. A housing benefit is money that can only be spent on housing. Free transit is money that can only be spent on transit. Free parking is a subsidy that can only be consumed by storing a car on valuable land.

People often compare these benefits with receiving nothing. Of course the benefit looks attractive under that comparison. But the economically relevant question is: compared with what?

Compare $100 of subsidized housing with $100 cash. Compare $100 of free transit with $100 cash. Compare a subsidized parking space with the cash value of the resource being given away. If somebody would rather have the cash, the restriction destroyed value.

Unless there is some separate reason to restrict choice — an externality, information problem, paternalistic concern, administrative issue, or other market failure — cash weakly dominates. The recipient can always spend the cash on exactly the same thing if that is really what they value most.

That is one road to UBI.

There is another: means testing itself creates marginal tax rates. If somebody loses 50 cents of benefits for each additional dollar earned, that is economically a 50% implicit marginal tax rate. Calling it “benefit withdrawal” rather than “tax” does not change the incentive.

This is why the distinction between marginal and effective tax rates matters so much. A universal payment combined with a flat marginal income tax can still be [highly progressive](https://www.ubicenter.org/us-flat-tax) in effective terms. If net tax is tY — B, then the effective tax rate is t — B/Y. At low incomes it can be strongly negative; as income rises it approaches t.

Max Ghenis, founder of the UBI Center, has spent years making essentially this argument. In [this talk](https://youtu.be/E69uu1vU2C4?is=GQTl7vml24kOwKtg), he discusses the distortions created by means testing and the distinction between marginal and effective tax rates. (I [interviewed Max about UBI and tax-benefit design](https://youtu.be/XWZA_mzxDXw?is=vPVtnP5xOYiouArn).)

The principle is simple: if you want redistribution, redistribute. Do not unnecessarily distort every marginal decision along the way.

## Excludable goods and the free-transit problem

This framework also helps distinguish things markets are naturally good at allocating from things they are not.

An **excludable** good is one where you can prevent somebody from consuming it unless they pay. Groceries are excludable. Housing is excludable. Parking is excludable. Transit is excludable.

National defense is the classic contrast. It is difficult to defend my house from invasion while deliberately allowing the invader to destroy my neighbor’s house because he failed to pay his defense subscription. The benefits spill over to non-payers, creating the familiar public-good problem.

That distinction matters because Opportunity currently advocates [permanent universal fare-free public transport](https://www.opportunity.org.nz/free_public_transport). There may be good second-best arguments for subsidizing transit: driving produces congestion, pollution and other externalities, and political constraints may prevent those harms from being priced correctly. Fare collection also has administrative costs, and the marginal cost of another passenger can sometimes be low.

But “transport is important” is not by itself an argument for making an excludable service free. If the objective is redistribution, cash preserves more choice. If the objective is reducing driving externalities, the first-best policy is to price those externalities directly through congestion, road and pollution pricing, then let people decide whether to drive, take transit, cycle, walk, or do something else.

Parking makes the same point even more starkly. Giving somebody free parking is effectively giving them money that can only be spent on parking. Imagine instead allowing people to surrender their claim on subsidized parking in exchange for cash. Many would take the cash. That tells us the restricted benefit is not worth its cost to them.

This is exactly the kind of issue that worries me. A party can retain the vocabulary of economic sophistication while gradually becoming detached from the principles that originally made its policies coherent.

## Why LVT fits — and why the mechanism matters

Land value taxation fits the framework beautifully, but only if we are clear about why.

Opportunity’s current [Tax Reset](https://www.opportunity.org.nz/tax-reset) sometimes explains LVT as discouraging land banking, encouraging denser development, and redirecting investment from property into productive business. I think that confuses the central economic mechanism.

The great virtue of a pure LVT is not that it makes landowners poorer until they behave more productively. It is that the tax base is largely outside the owner’s productive control.

Unimproved land value comes overwhelmingly from location and external conditions: nearby jobs, infrastructure, schools, restaurants, parks, natural amenities, agglomeration, scarcity, zoning, and everything else happening around the parcel. The owner cannot substantially reduce the tax by working less, hiring fewer people, investing less, or constructing fewer improvements.

That means there is essentially no [substitution effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitution_effect) against productive activity.

If the tax makes an incumbent landowner poorer and that person responds by working more, selling, subdividing, or developing, that is an [income effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_choice#Income_effect). Those are distinct concepts. Making somebody poorer can certainly change their behavior, but “make people poorer so they work harder” is not an efficiency argument.

Capitalization makes the point even clearer. Once an LVT is anticipated, the net present value of the future tax stream gets capitalized into the land price. The incumbent owner takes the capital loss. A subsequent buyer pays less for the parcel precisely because the buyer will assume the future tax burden.

Selling does not let the incumbent escape the loss, and the new owner’s productivity has nothing to do with how wealthy the seller was before the transaction. Nor does the buyer face some newly created productivity imperative: the future tax stream was already reflected in the lower acquisition price.

I explore that specific confusion in More Buildings Is Not More Productive.

The actual virtue of LVT is already remarkable: you can tax economic rent while creating very little deadweight loss. If the revenue replaces taxes on labor, capital, buildings or transactions, the overall reform can increase efficiency because you are removing distortions elsewhere.

Zero deadweight loss is already the superpower. We do not need to invent another one.

This is another place where “compared with what?” matters. People hear “new land tax” and compare it with no tax. But if LVT finances a universal payment or replaces part of an income tax, the relevant comparison is one complete tax-and-transfer system against another.

## Pigouvian taxes complete the picture

Sometimes the market is already distorted before government acts.

Suppose an activity creates $100 of private benefit but imposes $150 of uncompensated harm on other people. The activity happens because the decision-maker sees the private benefit without facing the full social cost.

The market price is missing information.

A Pigouvian tax puts that missing marginal cost into the price. This is one of the unusual cases where a tax can have negative deadweight loss: rather than creating a distortion, it removes an existing one.

And notice how market-oriented the solution is. Government does not have to decide whether the emitter should reduce output, innovate, substitute technologies, or simply emit and pay. Estimate the marginal external damage, put it into the price, and let decentralized actors decide what to do.

Price the externality. Let the market discover the quantity.

Now we have something more interesting than “centrism.” We have a decision rule: care about total welfare and its distribution; redistribute where the welfare gain exceeds the efficiency cost; minimize unnecessary substitution effects; prefer taxes on rents and externalities to taxes on productive margins; prefer cash to restricted benefits unless some identifiable market failure justifies the restriction; use markets for excludable goods where prices can do the allocation; and use collective provision where non-excludability or other market failures prevent markets from working.

Opportunity was founded by economists, so it is not surprising that many of its original policies independently converged on something close to this framework. The question is whether the framework survives after the people who originally understood it are gone.

## Institutions forget

Here is a simple test. Ask people inside the party to explain tax incidence; the difference between marginal and effective tax rates; the difference between an income effect and a substitution effect; why deadweight loss arises; when an in-kind benefit should beat cash; or why a Pigouvian tax can improve rather than reduce efficiency.

These are not obscure PhD-level questions. They are the machinery underneath policies Opportunity already advocates.

If that machinery disappears, drift becomes almost inevitable. A subsidy sounds compassionate. A means test sounds targeted. A wealth tax sounds fair. Free transit sounds generous. LVT gets defended as a tool for making landowners more productive. Each proposal can sound plausible in isolation while the architecture underneath the party gradually dissolves.

That is why I think there is value in explicitly stating the economic framework at the party’s constitutional or charter level. Not because particular policies should be frozen forever — exactly the opposite. The point is to preserve a method for deciding when policies should change.

There is also a second institutional problem: even if the party understands these ideas internally, mass politics is a terrible environment for transmitting them. I have spent days debating Opportunity policies on social media and repeatedly encountered people attacking UBI without understanding marginal versus effective rates, assuming landlords can mechanically pass LVT through to tenants without understanding incidence, or discussing pollution pricing without understanding externalities.

This is not because ordinary voters are stupid. They are busy. You cannot teach welfare economics to somebody scrolling LinkedIn for 45 seconds between meetings.

Opportunity already recognizes the underlying problem in its [Citizens’ Voice](https://www.opportunity.org.nz/citizens-voice) proposal: take a representative sample of ordinary people and give them time, evidence, experts, competing arguments, questioning, and deliberation.

My more esoteric interest is applying the same basic mechanism one level upstream, to choosing candidates. That is the idea behind [Election by Jury](https://www.electionbyjury.org/manifesto).

New Zealand’s national electoral system is not something a single party can unilaterally redesign, but a party has much more control over its own candidate-selection and list processes. A representative jury of members could spend hours or days hearing candidates explain their reasoning, watching them challenge each other, examining evidence, questioning experts, and then privately scoring them.

This need not replace ordinary membership democracy wholesale. It could be advisory. It could determine part of a party list. It could be combined with the existing process. Every other list position could come from a deliberative jury. There are many ways to experiment with the mechanism without immediately handing the entire institution over to it.

For a party whose comparative advantage is relatively technical policy, the attraction is straightforward: instead of asking which candidate can compress a complicated idea into a slogan, ask whose reasoning survives sustained scrutiny once ordinary people have actually been given the time to understand it.

## Principles, not coordinates

Paul Graham makes a useful distinction in [The Two Kinds of Moderate](https://www.paulgraham.com/mod.html). One kind of moderate deliberately chooses positions near the middle. The other independently reasons through questions and merely happens to land somewhere near the middle in aggregate.

The distinction matters here. “We are centrists” is not much of an intellectual defense against drift. The center itself moves. A policy framework grounded in welfare, incentives, market failures and efficient redistribution is much harder to dilute because it gives you an independent test for new ideas.

Entropy comes for every institution. The important thing to preserve is not merely a list of policies inherited from the economists who built the party.

It is the machinery that generated them.

# more buildings is not more productive

## what a land value tax actually does, and the one thing about welfare we can ever know for sure

clay shentrup12 min read·May 31, 2026

--

on may 7, 2023, jeremiah johnson posted the tweet that started this: a satellite shot of an australian property whose owner had turned down a $50 million offer, captioned [“land value tax would solve this.”](https://x.com/JeremiahDJohns/status/1655370306666545159)

stop on that number. someone offered this person fifty million dollars and they said no, more than once. so whatever he gets from that land, the dirt, the quiet, a view, the plain refusal to be moved, he values it above fifty million dollars. that is the loudest statement of preference you will ever see, and the last thing it resembles is a man confused about his own options. jeremiah looked at the most deliberate, highest-stakes choice a person can make and called it a problem for the tax code to fix.

that image is the whole essay, because it forces the question nobody pauses on: what does “more productive” even mean?

the next day we went a few rounds. i made the dull point that land already ends up with whoever values it most, so “put it to more productive use” is empty. he tagged that “econ-brain” and answered with anecdotes: owners [“would be paying crushing lvt that they likely couldn’t afford,”](https://x.com/JeremiahDJohns/status/1655378358148952064) so the tax [“would absolutely increase allocative efficiency,”](https://x.com/JeremiahDJohns/status/1655383139349417985) a full lvt would make a lot [“worth negative for an inefficient usage,”](https://x.com/JeremiahDJohns/status/1655383558624509954) and the real ones out there are [“holding land for no real reason”](https://x.com/JeremiahDJohns/status/1655384281844154371) because [“people are not rational automatons.”](https://x.com/JeremiahDJohns/status/1655384768085733377)

underneath all of it is a single error, and it has a name every economist learns in the first month: he has confused an income effect with a substitution effect, the two most routinely conflated forces in the subject. i’ll give his case full strength first and then run the confusion in slow motion. at full strength it’s clean: owners sit on land below its best use, the tax pushes them to build or sell, more gets built, that’s an efficiency gain. and the appeal is real. you see an empty lot in a dense city and a building feels like progress while the dirt feels like sloth.

but finding where it breaks means getting clear on the word jeremiah leans on without inspecting it. “productive” has exactly one coherent meaning, and it is not “covered in buildings.”

visual demonstration: the fact that 2b > 2a does not mean 2b > 1a

## what “productive” is allowed to mean

and that one coherent meaning is his own. jeremiah said “allocative efficiency,” which is precisely the welfare concept, resources flowing to their highest-valued use, so holding him to what people actually want only takes his word at face value. on that meaning, productivity is about value, and value lives in people, not in concrete. a use is productive to the precise degree that it serves what people actually want, and there is no second yardstick. so the real question is never “are there more buildings here,” it’s “are people better off,” and those two questions can point in opposite directions.

run it to the edge. suppose a community is happiest on open, undeveloped land, no towers, no density, just dirt. then the dirt is the productive use, by definition, and putting up buildings would be the waste. that lands as strange only because an aesthetic has been smuggled in, an unspoken premise that structures are value and emptiness is laziness. drop the premise and it comes clear: a building is only ever a bet about value, and the bet pays off only when people prefer it to whatever they gave up to get it. the structure is never self-justifying. the wanting is what justifies it.

now the part i think is the most underrated idea in all of economics. we cannot measure how much anyone wants anything. there is no utility meter. and even if there were, we could not add your reading to mine, because there is no shared unit between two people’s wants. so for nearly every change in the world, the kind that helps some and hurts others, there is simply no fact about whether “total value” rose. anyone who says otherwise has quietly decided whose wants count for how much and dressed the decision up as arithmetic.

against all that fog there is one miraculous exception. if a change makes at least one person better off and leaves nobody worse off, then every coherent way of weighing welfare agrees the world improved. you need no meter and no shared unit. it is the single case in the entire subject where we can flatly know that things got better. that is a pareto improvement, and its importance is almost impossible to oversell: it is the only bridge anyone has across the chasm of unmeasurable, incomparable wants. everything we are entitled to call “more productive” has to walk across that bridge.

a voluntary trade walks across it. both sides expect to gain or they walk away, so the trade lifts at least one person and sinks none. which is exactly why a refusal carries information. when the australian owner turns down $50 million, he is telling you, in the one language economics reads without a translator, that no buyer has put a deal in front of him that beats keeping the land. the reallocation jeremiah wants is not a pareto improvement. the man priced it and declined.

so “developing the lot is more productive” turns out to be the claim that the developed world is a pareto improvement over the held one. it is false, and the owner proved it false with the most expensive “no” most of us will ever witness. what jeremiah is doing, underneath, is counting the building he can see and ignoring everything the owner would have to surrender to get it, which he cannot see. the foregone value is no less real for being invisible from the sidewalk.

## but doesn’t the tax fix that?

there is a more sophisticated version, and it earns the formal treatment, even though the machinery runs over most readers’ heads. the thought: maybe the owner holds inefficiently, and the tax, by raising his costs, prods him into developing, so the tax brings about the efficient outcome. this is where the training is supposed to catch you, and where it didn’t.

a land value tax is a lump-sum charge on inelastic land. you owe it whether you raise a tower or grow weeds, so it leaves the price of every option exactly where it was, which is why it carries zero deadweight loss. the one thing it changes is the owner’s wealth. it makes him poorer.

fix his preferences. let w be his wealth, v_H(w) his utility from holding, and v_D(w) his utility from the best alternative, developing or selling, both rising with w.

- he held, so at his actual wealth, v_H(w) ≥ v_D(w).
- the tax cuts w to w′ < w, and because it’s a charge on the land itself rather than on what he does with it, it leaves the price of his options untouched. only wealth moves.
- now he develops or sells, so v_D(w′) ≥ v_H(w′).
- utility rises with wealth, so v_D(w′) ≤ v_D(w).
- set 4 against 1: v_D(w′) ≤ v_D(w) ≤ v_H(w).

he ends up worse off than he began. he built or sold, exactly as predicted, and his welfare fell. in the discipline’s own terms: with prices fixed and only wealth moving, the entire behavior change is, by the slutsky decomposition, a pure income effect, with no substitution effect at all. only a substitution effect can carry efficiency information, because only it reflects a changed tradeoff between the options. jeremiah watched the choice flip and read it as a better use revealing itself. it was a man getting poorer. (the same point dispatches the “worth negative for an inefficient usage” line. if the lot were truly worth more developed, the owner already bore that cost as foregone rent, and turning it into an explicit tax bill changes the build-versus-hold tradeoff not at all. what the bill can do is make him poorer, and if that pushes him to build or sell, the push is the income effect again, not a better use coming to light. what gets built then tells you his bank balance shrank. it does not tell you developing was the right call all along.)

without the symbols: the same move can look like a pareto improvement from the poorer, post-tax position, while from where the owner actually stood it is no improvement at all, since the proof leaves him below where he began. the tax is what moved him from the one position to the other, and quoting the verdict at the poorer position as though it described the original is the whole mistake. where the revenue then goes is a separate and real question about distribution. it is not the efficiency claim.

## why this isn’t a pigouvian tax

efficiency arguments do have a legitimate home, and naming it shows why this one isn’t living there.

a pigouvian tax earns its keep by closing a real externality, and the mechanism is contingency. you pay it only if you pollute, so it prices an action you take. that contingency is the whole point: because the bill rises and falls with what you do at the margin, it bends what you do, which is a substitution effect, and by erasing a pre-existing wedge between private and social cost it can even run negative deadweight loss.

a land value tax is contingent on nothing the owner does. it’s a charge on the land itself, owed the same whether he builds a tower or lets it sit, and he can’t sell his way out of it either, because it’s already baked into the lower price a buyer will pay. since no choice of his moves the bill, it changes no price he weighs at the margin, so it exerts none of the corrective pull a pigouvian tax does. it can still move him by making him poorer, but that’s the income effect once more, which corrects no externality and surfaces no better use. what the assessment measures runs the other way entirely: the value his location soaks up from everyone else, the worth that the surrounding community, the infrastructure, the neighbors deposit into that one spot. the pigouvian tax claws back harm you emit and bends behavior with a substitution effect, because it’s a price on your action; the land tax claws back value you absorb and creates no substitution effect, because it’s a charge on the world’s activity around you rather than yours. both recapture an externality. only one prices the taxed party’s behavior, and that contingency is the whole source of the substitution effect, the one kind of steering that carries efficiency information.

so the lvt closes no wedge in the owner’s own decision. he already eats the full opportunity cost of underusing the lot, the rent he forgoes, so his private cost already equals the social cost of his choice. there is nothing there to correct. absent an actual market failure, the land was already allocated efficiently, which is just the first welfare theorem talking, and “i can name a guy who seems dumb” is not a market failure.

## the theorem he’s actually arguing with

there’s a clean way to see the entire mistake, and it carries a name he’ll respect. the second welfare theorem says that any efficient allocation you might want, on whatever distributional grounds, can be reached by a lump-sum redistribution of endowments followed by ordinary competitive trade. its engine is the exact distinction this essay keeps pressing. a lump-sum transfer carries only an income effect and never a substitution effect. it can and does change what gets built and who ends up holding it, since moving wealth moves demand. but that change can never tell you the result is more productive: a transfer has winners and losers, so it is never a pareto improvement, and a pareto improvement is the only thing that can rank one arrangement above another. it introduces no inefficiency either, since it drives no wedge between cost and value. it simply resettles the economy somewhere else on the efficient frontier, with no verdict attached that the new spot beats the old. that is what lets the theorem split the labor. the transfer decides which efficient allocation you reach; the market keeps the allocation efficient; neither does the other’s job.

the land value tax is the textbook lump-sum instrument, which is why optimal-tax theory loves it and why it has no deadweight loss. now look at what the theorem assigns it. the tax does the distribution. the market does the allocation. and to be fair to jeremiah, real development can follow the tax, the owner building or selling to someone who will. the theorem just tells you what that development is. it’s the market reallocating at the new, poorer endowment the tax created, which is the income effect from earlier seen from above. that gives you a different efficient point under a different distribution, not a climb from a worse allocation to a better one, because there was never a worse one to climb from. jeremiah credits the distributive instrument with the market’s allocative work, and merging those two jobs is the error, stated now at the level of a result he won’t overturn in a quote-tweet.

so the honest case for the tax was never that it relocates land to better uses. absent a market failure the land was already as well placed as it could be, and any move the tax triggers is the market answering a new distribution, not repairing a misallocation. the real case is plainer and stronger: recapture the rent a location absorbs from everyone around it, and hand the proceeds to whoever we decide should have them. that case was his to make the moment he stopped insisting the tax makes anything “more productive.”

## the quiet presumption in the word “irrational”

which leaves the move jeremiah keeps reaching for: the owners are irrational, they hold for no reason, people aren’t rational automatons. it changes nothing, and being precise about why is the cleanest way to end.

start with what the argument never needed. nowhere above did the owner have to be rational. the income-effect point is a fact about the tax, not about him: it moves his wealth and leaves the price of building-versus-holding untouched, so it corrects no price and reveals no better use, whether he responds to being poorer shrewdly or foolishly. irrationality is orthogonal to all of it. grant him every bias in the catalogue and the tax still corrects nothing, because it changes no price he faces and tells him nothing he didn’t know. it only makes him poorer.

worse for the argument, the word doesn’t even do what it needs to. it needs “irrational” to mean the owner is holding when he ought to let go, so that prying him loose is a fix. but irrationality has no direction. to call his behavior irrational is to say it doesn’t track his real preferences, and the instant you say that you’ve discarded your only reading of which way those preferences run. for all anyone knows it runs the other way, and the price he’d rationally refuse is higher than the one he’s refusing now. you cannot get “he should develop” out of “he’s confused,” because confusion points nowhere in particular. to assume it points at the policy is to assume the very thing the argument set out to prove.

and this is the worst case in which to try it. “irrational” means a broken link between what you want and what you do, and the charge can be fair when a payoff is buried or technical, when someone truly can’t trace how an action serves their goals. skipping the matched 401(k), say. this is the reverse. the payoff is one gigantic, perfectly legible number, fifty million dollars, set before a man who looked straight at it and said no. there is no hidden link he failed to follow. he knows exactly what he is forgoing and forgoes it anyway, which is what wanting something looks like when the price is spelled out in full.

so “irrational” here renames a preference you don’t share as a defect, presumes you read a stranger’s values better than he does on the question where he’s been clearest in the world, and then presumes the defect bends conveniently toward the policy. “people aren’t rational automatons” only makes the presumption sound gentle. deciding you know what people should want, and which way they’re wrong about it, has a name, and it isn’t rigor.

which is the irony worth ending on. econ-brain is supposed to name the failure mode of trusting a model past the point where it tracks anything real. counting buildings as output, and reading a man’s values off a satellite photo, is that failure exactly. it just wasn’t mine.

# does a land value tax have zero or negative deadweight loss?

clay shentrup4 min read·Jun 26, 2022

--

i was recently reading the wikipedia article on [optimal taxation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_tax), when i came across an assertion that [land value taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_tax#Land_value_taxation) (LVT) have not *zero* deadweight loss, but actually *negative* deadweight loss.

> Economic theory suggests that a pure land value tax which succeeds in avoiding taxation of improvements could actually have a negative [deadweight loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deadweight_loss) ([positive externality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_externality)), due to productivity gains arising from efficient land use.

i’ve explained in more depth why this argument fails, and this image summarizes it.

[one of the citations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_tax#cite_note-31) was to the economist nicolaus tideman, so i reached out to him. he proposed the following example.

> *You own a downtown lot that is used for parking. Your income is $250,000 per year, and there is no land tax, so at a discount factor of 10%, your lot is worth $2.5 million for parking. But someone would give you $10 million as a building site. That is the market value of the land. You expect that price to rise by 10% per year, so you understand your full income to be $1.25 million. [that’s the 250k$ you make from one more year of parking revenues, plus the 1m$ appreciation from 10m$ to 11m$.] If you sell, your income from the sale price will be $1 million per year. Best to hold.*
> 
> 
> 
> *Now implement a land tax at a rate of 10% of the value of the land. The value of the land falls to $5 million, with taxes of $500,000 per year. Your 10% price gain, in this example, just offsets your taxes, so your net profit (parking plus price gain minus taxes) is $250,000 per year. But if you sell the land for $5 million, then, at the assumed discount rate of 10%, your income is $500,000. Now, because of the land tax removing half the income from speculating, it is best to sell.*

but the problem with this example is that in order for the lot to be worth 10m$ to the prospective buyer, it has to be develop-able into an asset that generates a 10% return. but if it could do that, then you as the current owner would *already *have an incentive to develop it, rather than making merely a meager 2.5% return as parking.

### winner’s curse

another relevant concept is called the [winner’s curse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winner%27s_curse). this just means the winner tends to be someone overly optimistic. but whether the winner’s curse applies to land value taxes centers on the notion of what the winner’s curse actually means. in my understanding, there are two essential factors that determine variability in bids for an asset:

- different levels of ability for the bidder to productively utilize the asset. (i.e. renoit can afford to pay more for scarce oil paint than i can, because he can turn it into a painting worth millions of dollars, whereas i have zero artistic ability.)
- different levels of *optimism* about the inherent market value of the asset.

because the first factor essentially cancels out in the market, this leaves the second factor as the dominant determinant of bid variability, meaning the winner will be “cursed” with lower returns, by definition. so the winner’s curse doesn’t inherently hold any great implications for land value taxes.

that said, here’s my own re-framing of this scenario, which i believe offers a more interesting “true conundrum”.

> *You own a parcel worth 2.5M to you, but worth 10M to someone else. so it would seem to make sense to sell for profit. *but* you expect a future positive externality receipt (e.g. a gold meteor lands on your property, you strike oil, Google builds new offices nearby, etc.) with an NPV of 1M (an NPV analogous to the more complicated expectation of 10% growth in your example). So really, that other party should pay you 11M (the 10M they think it’s worth to develop, plus the price of that meteor that’s going to land on your property). But they don’t know/believe that 1M, so they’re only willing to pay you 10M right now. So you’re just going to wait until the meteor lands next year, and then sell it to them for the full 11M. But there’s ****economic loss**** here because the property went undeveloped for that full year.*

now, in principle, *you* could just develop the property right now, and also pocket the externality. but maybe you don’t have access to the capital and/or skills to do so. in which case, this seems like a legitimate case of lost productive capacity. but, as i continue, there’s still a simple way out of this conundrum.

> *So then the question is, who’s right? If the prospective buyer is really confident they’re better at predicting the future than you, then they can just **“short” the property**. I.e. you sell the land for a future payment of 2f — e, where f is the actual future value and e is your expected future value (add in standard 0.3% to 3% shorting fees of course).*

tideman agrees with this in theory, but suggests that such an esoteric financial instrument might not exist in practice. i would tend to agree with that. and, ironically, this may be the kind of arena in which blockchain-based “smart contracts” could actually be useful, because they could facilitate such esoteric financial instruments.
